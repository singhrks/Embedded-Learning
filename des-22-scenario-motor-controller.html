Scenario: a safety-critical motor controller

The final scenario, and the one that draws on the whole track: hard deadlines measured in microseconds, a hazard that can injure someone, and a design where most of the safety argument is not in software at all.

The prompt

"Design the controller for a powered industrial door — a roller shutter on a loading bay. It must not close on a person. Take me through the design."

1 · Clarify

  WHAT IS THE HAZARD, EXACTLY? -> a person or vehicle
        crushed by a closing door. Also fingers in the
        mechanism, and the door falling if a spring or
        cable fails.
  WHAT IS THE SAFE STATE? -> THINK BEFORE ANSWERING.
        Stopping is safe if the door is not already on
        someone. If it IS, stopping still traps them, so
        the correct response to an obstruction while
        closing is to STOP AND REVERSE. And on power loss
        the door must not fall - which is mechanical
        (a brake, a counterbalance), not software.
  SPEED AND MASS? -> a heavy door moving at, say,
        0.2 m/s. Stopping distance determines how quickly
        detection must act.
  DETECTION MEANS? -> typically a light curtain or safety
        photocells across the opening, plus motor current
        or torque sensing as a secondary.
  DUTY CYCLE? -> hundreds of cycles per day, for years.
  ENVIRONMENT? -> industrial: dust, temperature range,
        electrical noise from nearby machinery, possibly
        vehicles impacting it.
  STANDARDS? -> powered doors are regulated. I would want
        the applicable standard and an assessor rather
        than guessing at clause detail; what I can design
        to is the shape - risk assessment, a rated safety
        function, and evidence.
  VOLUME? -> thousands. Certification cost is significant
        and amortised over a modest volume.

RESTATE:
  "The hazard is crushing during closing. The safe
  response is stop-and-reverse, not just stop, and on
  power loss the door must be held mechanically. I expect
  the safety function to be largely hardware with software
  supervision, so I'll design that first and the motion
  control second."

2 · The safety function — mostly not software

   THE CHAIN, and the deliberate design decision is how
   little of it depends on firmware being correct.

   LIGHT CURTAIN  ---+
   (dual channel,    |
    self-testing)    |
                     v
                +---------------+       +------------------+
                | SAFETY RELAY  |------>| MOTOR CONTACTORS |
                | or safety     |       | (two, in series, |
                | controller    |       |  monitored)      |
                +---------------+       +------------------+
                     ^                          ^
                     |                          |
   E-STOP -----------+                          |
                     |                          |
   +----------------------------+               |
   |  MAIN MCU                  |---------------+
   |  - motion profile          |   enable (must be
   |  - current control         |    actively driven)
   |  - obstruction reversal    |
   |  - diagnostics, comms      |
   +----------------------------+

   WHY THIS SHAPE:

   THE LIGHT CURTAIN IS A RATED SAFETY DEVICE. It is dual-
   channel and self-testing - it periodically verifies its
   own outputs can switch, so a welded or failed channel is
   detected rather than silently defeating protection. You
   buy this rather than building it, because building a
   certified sensing device is a project of its own.

   TWO CONTACTORS IN SERIES, MONITORED. A single contactor
   can weld shut - a common failure - and then software
   cannot stop the motor at all. Two in series means one
   welded contact does not defeat the stop, and monitoring
   their auxiliary contacts detects the weld so the machine
   refuses to start again. This is a standard pattern worth
   knowing by name.

   THE MCU'S ENABLE IS AN ACTIVE SIGNAL. A hung MCU stops
   driving it and the drive is removed. Safety does not
   depend on the MCU doing something correct; it depends on
   it CONTINUING TO DO something, which is a much weaker
   and therefore much safer requirement.

   POWER LOSS: the door must not fall. That is a brake that
   is engaged when de-energised, or a counterbalance - a
   mechanical property, and I would confirm it with the
   mechanical designer rather than assume it.

  COMFORTABLE MARGIN, which matters because a safety
  system must tolerate worst-case paths and because
  certification will ask you to justify the timing rather
  than observe it.

  STOPPING DISTANCE IS THE REQUIREMENT THAT SIZES
  EVERYTHING. If the door moves at 0.2 m/s and the total
  response - detection, decision, contactor drop-out,
  mechanical braking - is 100 ms, the door travels 20 mm
  after the trigger plus the braking distance. THAT number
  determines where the light curtain must be positioned
  and whether the response budget is acceptable. Deriving
  it, rather than assuming a deadline, is the strong
  answer here.

Follow-up questions

Why is "stop" the wrong safe state here?Senior

Because the hazard is crushing, and stopping leaves the person crushed. A heavy door that halts while bearing on someone has removed the additional force but not the existing one, and the person cannot free themselves. The correct response to an obstruction while closing is to stop and reverse to fully open, which removes the load.

This is why "what is the safe state?" is a domain question rather than a technical default — the reflexive answer of de-energising everything would be actively harmful here.

Two refinements worth adding. On power loss the answer is different again: the door must not fall, so a de-energised brake or counterbalance holds it — and there is no power to reverse with, which is precisely why that property must be mechanical. And after a reversal, the door should require a deliberate command to close again rather than retrying automatically, because an automatic retry on a person is the same hazard repeated.

Listening forReversal rather than stopping, the different answer for power loss, and no automatic retry.
Why two contactors in series?Senior

Because a contactor can weld shut, and it is a well-known failure mode rather than a remote possibility — switching an inductive motor load causes arcing, and eventually a contact fuses. With a single contactor, that failure means software cannot remove power from the motor at all, and every software-based protection becomes irrelevant.

Two in series means one welded contact does not defeat the stop: the other still opens the circuit. And the auxiliary contacts are monitored, so the weld is detected — the controller sees that a contactor did not open when commanded and refuses to start the next cycle. That turns a dangerous latent fault into a machine that stops working and asks for service, which is exactly the behaviour you want.

The general principle it illustrates: redundancy is only useful with detection. Two contactors without monitoring would silently degrade to one after the first weld, and you would be running unprotected without knowing. The monitoring is what makes the redundancy real.

Listening forDetection of the welded contact, not just the redundancy.
Why buy a light curtain rather than building the detection?Mid

Because it is a rated safety device, and building an equivalent means taking on its certification, its self-test design and its failure analysis — a project comparable in size to the rest of the controller.

What a certified curtain provides that a pair of photocells does not: dual-channel outputs so a single failure does not defeat it; periodic self-testing that verifies its own output switching, so a stuck output is detected rather than silently blinding the protection; a specified response time you can use in your stopping distance calculation; a defined resolution so you know what object size it reliably detects; and immunity specifications for the industrial environment.

The design work that remains yours: positioning it so the stopping distance is covered, wiring it to the safety relay correctly, monitoring its diagnostic output, and deciding the behaviour when it reports a fault — which must be "refuse to close under power", because a blinded sensor reporting no obstruction must never be interpreted as all clear.

The general lesson: for rated safety components, buy. Differentiation is elsewhere.

Listening forSelf-testing and the specified response time feeding the stopping distance calculation.
How do you calculate where the light curtain must be positioned?Senior

From the total response time and the door's speed, working backwards from the requirement that the door must stop — or reverse — before it can injure someone.

The response chain: the curtain's own detection and output time (specified, typically single-digit milliseconds); the safety relay's response; the contactors' drop-out time (tens of milliseconds, and mechanical); plus the mechanical braking and the door's remaining travel from its momentum. Sum those, multiply by the closing speed, and that is the distance the door travels after the beam is broken.

So if the total is 100 ms at 0.2 m/s, the door moves 20 mm plus braking distance after detection — and the curtain must be positioned so that a person detected at that point is not reached. The standards for these machines specify how to compute the required separation, including allowances for approach speed and for reaching through, and that calculation is one I would want to do against the actual standard rather than from memory.

The design consequences: reducing closing speed reduces the required separation, which may be the cheapest way to meet it; and faster contactors or an electrically-released brake reduce the response time directly.

Listening forSumming the full response chain including mechanical drop-out, and deferring to the standard for the separation formula.
What role does motor current sensing play if there is already a light curtain?Mid

It catches what the curtain cannot see. A light curtain protects a plane across the opening; it does not detect an object already inside that plane, something on the floor below the lowest beam, a person leaning in from the side, or the door fouling on its own mechanism.

Current sensing detects the mechanical consequence: an obstruction raises the torque required, so the motor current rises above the profile expected for that position and speed. Comparing against an expected profile — rather than a fixed threshold — is much more sensitive, because the normal current varies with position, direction and temperature.

Its limitations, which must be stated: it is slower than the curtain (you need several samples to distinguish an obstruction from noise), it is less sensitive for a soft obstruction, and it is not a rated safety function — so it supplements the curtain rather than replacing it.

A useful refinement: learn the normal current profile during commissioning and re-learn it slowly over time, so mechanical wear does not cause false trips — while bounding how much drift is accepted, because a progressive rise is itself a maintenance signal.

Listening forProfile comparison rather than a fixed threshold, and honesty about it being secondary.
How does the certification requirement change your software architecture?Senior

It pushes hard toward partitioning, because the verification burden scales with the size and complexity of whatever is in scope.

So the safety-related function — monitoring the enable, checking contactor feedback, supervising the obstruction response — is kept small, simple and separate, ideally on its own microcontroller or at minimum in a clearly bounded module with no dynamic allocation, no recursion, bounded loops and full structural coverage. Everything else — the motion profile, diagnostics, comms, the user interface — sits outside that boundary and is developed to normal standards.

The constraints inside the boundary typically include a coding standard such as MISRA, static analysis, a qualified toolchain, requirements traceable from the hazard analysis down to individual tests, and documented evidence for all of it.

The consequence for the design round: I would not put the safety logic inside the same binary as a communications stack, because that would drag the stack into scope. And I would say that this must be planned from the start — the evidence is generated by the process, so retrofitting it onto finished code is close to impossible.

I would also be explicit that I would want the actual standard and a competent assessor rather than working from recollection of clause detail.

Listening forPartitioning to shrink scope, and appropriate humility about clause-level requirements.
The door is used 500 times a day for ten years. What does that imply?Senior

About 1.8 million cycles, which makes wear the dominant design consideration for several components.

Contactors have a rated electrical life often in the hundreds of thousands of operations, so they will need replacement — which means they must be serviceable, their cycle count should be recorded, and the controller should report when they approach their rating rather than waiting for a weld. Using solid state switching for the routine starts and stops while reserving the contactors for the safety function extends their life substantially, and is a design decision worth raising.

Mechanical: cables, springs, rollers and the brake all wear, and a progressive change in the current profile or in travel time is an early indicator — which is a good reason to trend those values rather than only alarming on thresholds.

Flash: if the controller logs each cycle, that is 1.8 million writes; naive logging would exhaust the endurance, so the storage design needs wear levelling or a medium suited to frequent writes.

The safety function itself needs periodic proof testing — a safety mechanism nobody verifies may have failed silently, and the self-testing light curtain plus contactor monitoring gives you much of that automatically, which is part of why they are specified.

Listening forContactor wear with cycle counting, and the flash endurance implication of per-cycle logging.
Summarise what makes this design safe, in three sentences.Senior

The primary protection is a rated, self-testing light curtain wired through a safety relay to two monitored contactors in series, so no single failure — including a welded contact or a hung processor — can prevent the drive being removed.

The MCU's contribution is an active enable signal and a stop-and-reverse response, so safety depends on the software continuing to do something rather than on it doing something correct, and the reversal removes the crushing load rather than merely halting it.

Mechanical properties carry what electronics cannot: a de-energised brake holds the door on power loss, and the whole arrangement is periodically self-tested and monitored so that latent failures surface as a machine that refuses to run rather than as protection that has silently stopped working.

The thread running through all three is that the safety argument depends as little as possible on firmware being correct — which is the general principle worth taking away from this scenario.

Information theory: entropy and cross-entropy

How to measure surprise, and how to measure the gap between two distributions. Cross-entropy is the loss function used to train essentially every model in this track, and it deserves to be understood rather than imported.

Information as surprise

Entropy is far more intuitive when read as average surprise than as a formula involving logarithms, and that reading carries all the way to why cross-entropy is the right training loss.

   THE SELF-INFORMATION of an outcome:

        I(x) = -log P(x)

   WHY THAT FORM? Because it is the only one satisfying
   three requirements anyone would want:

     · a CERTAIN event carries no information:
           P = 1 -> I = 0
     · a RARER event carries more:
           P smaller -> I larger
     · INDEPENDENT events ADD:
           I(x, y) = I(x) + I(y)
       and log is the function that turns the product
       P(x)P(y) into a sum.

   UNITS DEPEND ON THE LOG BASE:
        log2  -> BITS       (the natural unit for storage)
        ln    -> NATS       (the natural unit for maths and
                             for every ML framework)

   EXAMPLES:
        a fair coin        -log2(0.5)     = 1 bit
        a fair die         -log2(1/6)     = 2.58 bits
        one of 50,000
          tokens, uniform  -log2(1/50000) = 15.6 bits
        "the" after
          "once upon"      -log2(0.0001)  = 13.3 bits
        "time" after
          "once upon a"    -log2(0.95)    = 0.07 bits

   THAT LAST PAIR IS THE WHOLE IDEA OF A LANGUAGE MODEL: a
   good model assigns high probability to what actually
   comes next, so real text carries LOW surprise under it.
   Training minimises average surprise. Nothing more
   mysterious than that.

   ENTROPY is the AVERAGE surprise of a distribution:

        H(P) = -sum_x P(x) log P(x)  =  E[-log P(x)]

     fair coin           H = 1 bit          (maximum for 2
                                             outcomes)
     biased coin p=0.9   H = 0.469 bits
     certain outcome     H = 0 bits         (no surprise
                                             ever)
     uniform over n      H = log2(n)        (MAXIMUM)

   AND THE INTERPRETATION THAT MAKES IT CONCRETE: H(P) IS
   THE MINIMUM AVERAGE NUMBER OF BITS NEEDED TO ENCODE
   SAMPLES FROM P. That is Shannon's source coding theorem,
   and it is why this subject and data compression are the
   same subject.

   WHICH LEADS DIRECTLY TO A CLAIM WORTH SITTING WITH:
   A LANGUAGE MODEL IS A COMPRESSOR. A model that predicts
   the next token well can encode text in few bits. The
   better the prediction, the better the compression - and
   the training objective is literally "minimise the
   compressed size of the training data".

Cross-entropy and KL divergence

These two quantities are the loss function of every language model ever trained, so it is worth knowing exactly what each measures and how they differ.

/* --- CROSS-ENTROPY: encoding P's data with Q's code --- */

        H(P, Q) = -sum_x P(x) log Q(x)

    P is the TRUE distribution, Q is the MODEL's.

    If Q = P, cross-entropy equals entropy - you cannot do
    better. Any mismatch costs extra bits.

/* --- KL DIVERGENCE: exactly that extra cost --- */

        KL(P || Q) = H(P, Q) - H(P)
                   = sum_x P(x) log( P(x) / Q(x) )

    PROPERTIES:
      KL >= 0, always              (Gibbs' inequality)
      KL = 0 if and only if P = Q
      NOT SYMMETRIC: KL(P||Q) != KL(Q||P)
      NOT a distance - it violates the triangle inequality

    THE ASYMMETRY IS MEANINGFUL AND WORTH KNOWING:

      KL(P || Q)  "FORWARD", MODE-COVERING.
                  Heavily penalises Q(x) being near zero
                  where P(x) is not. Q is forced to spread
                  out and cover everything P does.
                  THIS IS WHAT MAXIMUM LIKELIHOOD MINIMISES
                  (chapter 09) - which is why generative
                  models trained this way produce some
                  low-quality samples: they would rather
                  cover everything than be sharp.

      KL(Q || P)  "REVERSE", MODE-SEEKING.
                  Q can safely ignore parts of P; it just
                  must not put mass where P has none.
                  Produces sharp but narrow results.
                  Used in variational inference and in the
                  RLHF objective (chapter 24).

/* --- WHY CROSS-ENTROPY IS THE LOSS FUNCTION --- */

    For a classification problem the true distribution is
    ONE-HOT: P(correct class) = 1, everything else 0.

    So the sum collapses to a single term:

        H(P, Q) = -log Q(correct class)

    AND THAT IS THE NEGATIVE LOG LIKELIHOOD from chapter 09.
    Cross-entropy loss, negative log likelihood, and
    minimising KL divergence from the data distribution are
    THE SAME OBJECTIVE seen from three angles.

    Also note: since H(P) is fixed by the data,
        minimise cross-entropy  ==  minimise KL(P||Q).
    The entropy of the data is a constant floor you cannot
    get below, which is why a well-trained model's loss
    plateaus at a non-zero value - that floor is the
    irreducible ambiguity of language.                    */

Implementing it

The formulas as written are numerically unusable, and the standard implementation looks different for reasons worth understanding rather than copying.

import numpy as np

def cross_entropy(logits, target_idx):
    """Numerically stable, from raw logits."""
    logits = logits - np.max(logits)          # chapter 06
    log_probs = logits - np.log(np.sum(np.exp(logits)))
    return -log_probs[target_idx]

# EQUIVALENTLY, and this is what frameworks do:
#     loss = logsumexp(logits) - logits[target]
# ONE SUBTRACTION AND ONE STABLE REDUCTION. No softmax, no
# log of a probability, no chance of log(0).

# --- THE GRADIENT, and why it is beautiful ---
#
#   dL/dlogits = softmax(logits) - one_hot(target)
#
# The messy softmax Jacobian (chapter 06) cancels against
# the log in cross-entropy, leaving a SUBTRACTION.
#
#   Interpretation: the gradient is the PREDICTION ERROR.
#   Push down the probability of everything you predicted,
#   push up the probability of what was correct, in
#   proportion to how wrong you were.

def cross_entropy_backward(probs, target_idx):
    grad = probs.copy()
    grad[target_idx] -= 1.0
    return grad

# --- BATCHED, in PyTorch ---
import torch
import torch.nn.functional as F

logits  = torch.randn(32, 50000)     # batch, vocab
targets = torch.randint(0, 50000, (32,))

loss = F.cross_entropy(logits, targets)     # RAW LOGITS

# NEVER:
#   probs = F.softmax(logits, -1)
#   loss = F.nll_loss(torch.log(probs), targets)
# It works, and it is less accurate and slower, and it
# throws away the cancellation above.

# --- LABEL SMOOTHING ---
loss = F.cross_entropy(logits, targets, label_smoothing=0.1)
# Instead of a one-hot target, use 0.9 for the correct class
# and 0.1/(V-1) spread over the rest.
#
# WHY: a one-hot target asks the model to output probability
# exactly 1, which requires an INFINITE logit. The model
# pushes logits ever larger, becomes overconfident
# (chapter 09) and generalises worse. Smoothing gives it a
# finite target to reach. Standard in vision and machine
# translation; used more selectively for LLM pretraining.

# --- PERPLEXITY ---
perplexity = torch.exp(loss)
# The exponential of the cross-entropy in NATS.
#
# THE INTERPRETATION: "the model is as uncertain as if it
# were choosing uniformly among this many options."
#   perplexity 1      perfect prediction
#   perplexity 20     a good modern LLM on general text
#   perplexity 50000  no better than uniform over the
#                     vocabulary
#
# AND THE CAVEAT THAT INVALIDATES MOST COMPARISONS:
# PERPLEXITY DEPENDS ON THE TOKENISER. A model with a larger
# vocabulary spreads the same text over fewer tokens, so its
# per-token perplexity is not comparable to a model with a
# different vocabulary. Compare BITS PER BYTE instead
# (chapter 16).
The one identity to remember 
minimise cross-entropy
  == maximise likelihood        (chapter 09)
  == minimise KL(data || model)
  == minimise the compressed size of the data
  == minimise the model's average surprise

Five descriptions of the same optimisation. Which one you use depends on which makes the current problem obvious.

The other quantities worth knowing

A few further quantities appear often enough in papers to be worth recognising, even if you never compute one yourself.

/* --- MUTUAL INFORMATION --- */
        I(X; Y) = H(X) - H(X|Y)
                = KL( P(X,Y) || P(X)P(Y) )

    "How many bits does knowing Y save you about X?"
    Zero exactly when X and Y are independent. SYMMETRIC,
    unlike KL.

    WHERE IT APPEARS: feature selection, InfoNCE and
    contrastive learning objectives, analysing what a
    network layer retains about its input, and the
    information-bottleneck view of representation learning.

/* --- CONDITIONAL ENTROPY --- */
        H(X|Y) = H(X, Y) - H(Y)
    "Remaining uncertainty about X once Y is known."
    A LANGUAGE MODEL ESTIMATES exactly
        H(next token | context)
    and reducing it is the whole training objective.

/* --- THE ENTROPY OF ENGLISH --- */
    Shannon estimated ~1.0-1.3 bits per character for
    English text, using human prediction experiments.

    Modern large models achieve roughly 0.6-0.8 BITS PER
    BYTE on general text - BELOW Shannon's human estimate.
    That is a genuinely meaningful benchmark, because it is
    tokeniser-independent and directly comparable across
    models and across decades.

/* --- CHANNEL CAPACITY, for completeness --- */
    Shannon's other great result: a noisy channel has a
    maximum reliable rate, and codes exist that approach it.
    Not directly used in this track, but it is the same
    mathematics, and it is why error-correcting codes and
    machine learning keep borrowing each other's tools.

/* --- ENTROPY AS A DIAGNOSTIC, which is genuinely
       useful --- */

    def output_entropy(probs):
        return -np.sum(probs * np.log(probs + 1e-12))

    · HIGH entropy in the output distribution -> the model is
      uncertain about this token. Often a good place to
      branch, to ask for confirmation, or to spend more
      compute.
    · ENTROPY COLLAPSING TOWARD ZERO during training ->
      overconfidence, or a degenerate solution.
    · ENTROPY OF THE ATTENTION WEIGHTS -> whether a head is
      focused on one position or spread across many
      (chapter 19). A useful interpretability probe.
    · AND FOR DATA: the entropy of your label distribution
      tells you the accuracy a trivial "always predict the
      majority class" model would get. Compare against that,
      not against zero.                                    */

How it shows up

SymptomCause
nan losslog(0) — softmax applied before cross-entropy, or no epsilon
Loss plateaus at a non-zero valueExpected — that floor is the data's own entropy
Loss of exactly log(vocab_size) at step 0Correct — a uniform distribution. A useful sanity check
Loss much higher than log(V) at step 0Initialisation is too large, or labels are misaligned
Model becomes overconfident and generalises worseOne-hot targets push logits to infinity — try label smoothing
Perplexity comparisons disagree with qualityDifferent tokenisers — use bits per byte
Generative samples are blurry or genericForward KL is mode-covering
A model collapses to one output modeReverse KL is mode-seeking
Accuracy beats a baseline by littleCompare against the majority-class entropy, not zero

Power, energy and thermal limits

Power is the constraint that shapes every modern design decision — why clocks stopped rising, why phones have big and LITTLE cores, why your laptop is fast for ten seconds. For battery-powered embedded work it is often the primary specification, not an afterthought.

Power versus energy

Two different quantities, two different goals

Power (watts) is the instantaneous rate. It determines heat, and therefore cooling, package and thermal throttling.

Energy (joules = watts × seconds) is the total consumed. It determines battery life.

They pull in different directions. Running twice as fast at three times the power saves energy if the device can then sleep — the "race to idle" strategy. Deciding which you are optimising is the first question in any power discussion, and getting it wrong wastes months.

Where the power goes

Before you can reduce power consumption you need to know where it actually goes, and in modern chips the answer has shifted substantially from what it was two decades ago. Both halves of the equation below matter, and they respond to completely different remedies.

   P_total  =  P_dynamic  +  P_static

   P_dynamic = α × C × V² × f        switching: charging and
                                      discharging gate capacitance
        α  activity factor — fraction of gates toggling per cycle
        C  capacitance — grows with transistor count and wire length
        V  supply voltage
        f  clock frequency

   P_static  = I_leak × V            leakage: current through
                                      transistors that are "off"

Two facts follow, and they explain most of modern computing:

  1. V is SQUARED. Halving the voltage quarters dynamic power.
     But lower voltage means slower switching, so f must drop too.
     Reducing both gives roughly CUBIC savings — this is the entire
     basis of dynamic voltage and frequency scaling.

  2. Leakage grows as transistors shrink and as temperature rises,
     and it is paid whenever the circuit is powered at all — even
     idle. At modern nodes it can be 30-40% of total power. Hence
     POWER GATING: physically disconnect unused blocks, do not
     merely stop clocking them.

Why performance costs so much more than it returns

/* Approximate, but the shape is right and it is the shape that matters. */

  frequency   voltage   dynamic power   relative performance
  ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
   1.0 GHz     0.7 V        1.0 x              1.0 x
   1.5 GHz     0.8 V        1.96 x             1.5 x
   2.0 GHz     0.9 V        3.31 x             2.0 x
   2.5 GHz     1.05 V       5.63 x             2.5 x
   3.0 GHz     1.2 V        8.82 x             3.0 x

   3x the performance costs ~9x the power.
   Or, read the other way: HALF the frequency at reduced voltage
   costs about ONE EIGHTH the power.

This is why:
  · a LITTLE core at 1 GHz does background work at a tiny fraction
    of the energy a big core would spend
  · two cores at 1.5 GHz beat one at 3 GHz on both power AND
    throughput — when the work parallelises
  · "turbo" is a short-duration exception, not a sustainable state

The three walls

WallWhat happenedConsequence
Power wall (~2005)Air cooling caps a chip at roughly 100–150 W; frequency scaling hit that ceilingClocks flattened at 3–4 GHz. Performance now comes from parallelism (ch. 19).
End of Dennard scaling (~2006)Historically, shrinking transistors let voltage drop proportionally, keeping power per area constant. Below ~1 V, threshold voltage and leakage stopped that.More transistors no longer come with free power savings.
Dark siliconYou can fit more transistors on a die than you can afford to switch simultaneouslyChips are full of specialised blocks — video codecs, NPUs, crypto engines — used one at a time. Specialisation became the way to spend transistors.
Why accelerators exist

A fixed-function block doing one job — H.265 decode, AES, convolution — can be 10 to 1000× more energy efficient than a general-purpose core doing the same work, because it eliminates instruction fetch, decode, register file traffic and speculation. Dark silicon makes that trade obviously worthwhile: the block costs area, which is cheap, and saves energy, which is not. This is why a modern SoC is mostly not CPU, and why NPUs appeared in phones.

Idle states are a hierarchy, and deeper is not always better

ACPI C-states / ARM idle states — deeper saves more, wakes slower

  C0 / running     executing instructions
  C1 / WFI         clock gated, caches live         wake: ~ns
  C2               deeper clock gating              wake: ~µs
  C3               L1/L2 flushed, caches off        wake: ~10s of µs
  C6+ / power off  core powered down, state saved   wake: ~100s of µs
                   to always-on memory

  ARM's WFI (wait for interrupt) and WFE (wait for event) are the
  instructions that request this; PSCI CPU_SUSPEND selects the depth.

THE BREAK-EVEN CALCULATION that governs every idle decision:

  entering a state costs E_transition and T_wake.
  It is only worth entering if the expected idle time exceeds

        T_breakeven = E_transition / (P_shallow - P_deep)

  An OS governor predicts the next wake (from timers and past
  behaviour) and picks the deepest state whose break-even it clears.
  Predict wrong and you spend MORE energy than you saved.
Why a periodic timer is expensive out of proportion to its work

A task that wakes every 1 ms to do nothing prevents the system from ever reaching a deep idle state. The work is negligible; the lost sleep is not. This is why Linux has a tickless kernel (NO_HZ), why timer coalescing and deferrable timers exist, and why a single badly written polling loop can halve a device's battery life. When profiling power, count wakeups, not CPU percentage — powertop exists precisely to show you this.

Thermal behaviour

Power that goes in comes out as heat, and heat is what ultimately limits sustained performance. This is why a benchmark result can depend on how long the benchmark has been running.

   T_junction = T_ambient + P × R_thermal

     R_thermal = thermal resistance, °C per watt, from junction to air.
     Determined by package, heatsink, airflow, and PCB copper.

  A phone SoC might have R_thermal ≈ 15 °C/W with no heatsink.
     5 W sustained  ->  75 °C above ambient  ->  throttling.

  Which is why:

    · sustained performance is often HALF of burst performance
    · a benchmark's first 10 seconds do not predict the next 10 minutes
    · thermal throttling is a PERFORMANCE bug you cannot fix in software
    · leakage rises with temperature, and leakage generates heat —
      a positive feedback loop that must be actively broken by
      throttling, or the part destroys itself

TDPThermal Design Power — what the cooling must remove. Not maximum power, and not average power. A design target, frequently misread as a spec.

What software controls

Much of a system's power consumption is decided by software rather than hardware. These are the levers you actually control, and the ones that make the largest difference in practice.

Ranked by leverage. The top of this list dwarfs the bottom.

  1. DO LESS WORK.
     A better algorithm saves energy in every term at once. No power
     technique competes with not executing the instructions.

  2. MOVE LESS DATA.
     Energy per operation, roughly, at a modern node:
        32-bit integer add          ~0.1 pJ
        32-bit float multiply       ~1 pJ
        32-bit SRAM (cache) read    ~5 pJ
        32-bit DRAM read           ~200 pJ   <- 2000x the add
        wireless transmit, per bit  ~1000 pJ

     A DRAM access costs more energy than a thousand arithmetic
     operations. Cache optimisation (chapters 13-14) is therefore
     ALSO the most effective power optimisation available to you.

  3. SLEEP MORE, AND DEEPER.
     Batch work. Coalesce timers. Use interrupts, never polling.
     Minimise wakeups, not just CPU time.

  4. USE THE RIGHT UNIT.
     Offload to an accelerator, or to a LITTLE core.

  5. LET THE GOVERNOR DO ITS JOB.
     Do not pin frequency to maximum "for consistency" outside
     of a measurement session.
/* polling versus interrupts, in energy terms */

/* BAD: the core never sleeps, the whole SoC stays in a shallow state */
while (!(readl(base + STATUS) & READY))
        ;

/* BETTER: sleep until the hardware says something happened */
wait_event_interruptible(dev->wq, dev->ready);

/* In bare metal, WFI is the whole idea: */
    while (!ready) {
        __asm__ volatile("wfi");   /* clock-gate until an interrupt */
    }

/* Batching beats spreading: 100 wakeups doing 1 unit of work each
   costs far more than 1 wakeup doing 100 units, because each wakeup
   pays the transition energy and blocks deep idle.               */

Measuring power

Power is measurable at several levels, from whole-system to per-component, and the right instrument depends on the question. Guessing at power behaviour is even less reliable than guessing at performance.

# Linux: where are the wakeups coming from?
sudo powertop                    # per-process wakeups/second and estimated power
sudo powertop --auto-tune        # applies known-good power settings

# what state is the CPU actually in?
cpupower monitor                 # residency in each C-state and P-state
cat /sys/devices/system/cpu/cpu0/cpufreq/scaling_cur_freq
cat /sys/devices/system/cpu/cpu0/cpufreq/scaling_governor

# thermal zones and trip points
cat /sys/class/thermal/thermal_zone*/type
cat /sys/class/thermal/thermal_zone*/temp     # millidegrees C

# on-die energy counters, where available
perf stat -e power/energy-pkg/ ./program

# battery-powered embedded work: an external measurement is
# ground truth. A current-sense resistor plus a scope, or a
# purpose-built power analyser, shows the wakeup pattern directly
# in a way no software counter can.

Power in embedded design

In a battery-powered device, power is not an optimisation — it is the primary design constraint, and it changes decisions that would be obvious on a machine with a mains supply.

   determines whether a product ships.                            */

   sleep current      2 µA
   active current     8 mA
   active time        20 ms per reading
   reading interval   10 s

   I_avg = 218 µA  ->  43 days.

   Same code, same features, 12x shorter life. Sleep current is
   almost always the number that decides the product.            */

How to practise, and the pattern method

Interview problems look infinite. They are not — they are perhaps thirty recurring patterns in different costumes. This track teaches the patterns, and gives you a judge in the page so you write and run code rather than reading about it.

Why patterns, not problems

It is worth being blunt about the approach most people take, because it is the one that feels like progress and produces the least of it. The comparison below is the argument for everything that follows.

   THE FAILING STRATEGY: grind 500 problems, one at a time,
   memorising solutions.

     · you meet problem 501 in the interview and it is not
       one you memorised
     · you cannot explain WHY the solution works, and the
       follow-up question exposes that immediately
     · retention is poor, because nothing connects

   THE WORKING STRATEGY: learn the PATTERN, then solve
   several problems that wear it.

     "Find a pair summing to a target in a SORTED array"
     "Find the container holding the most water"
     "Remove duplicates in place"
     "Sort an array of 0s, 1s and 2s"
     "Trap rainwater"

   FIVE DIFFERENT PROBLEMS. ONE PATTERN: two pointers moving
   toward each other, or in the same direction, exploiting
   an ordering invariant.

   Once you SEE the pattern, problem 501 is recognisable
   even though you have never met it, because you are not
   matching the problem - you are matching its SHAPE.

   THE RECOGNITION SIGNALS ARE LEARNABLE, and each chapter
   in this track states them explicitly:

     "sorted array" + "pair/triplet"      -> two pointers
     "contiguous subarray" + "max/min"    -> sliding window
     "kth largest"                        -> heap
     "all permutations/combinations"      -> backtracking
     "count the ways" / "min cost"        -> DP
     "shortest path, unweighted"          -> BFS
     "cycle in a linked list"             -> fast/slow
     "next greater element"               -> monotonic stack
     "prefix/word lookup"                 -> trie
     "connected components"               -> union-find or DFS

   THAT TABLE IS MOST OF THE INTERVIEW. The rest is
   implementing it without bugs, which is what the runnable
   problems here are for.

The method for a single problem

The same six steps, every problem, until they happen without thinking. They are not study advice — they are what the interviewer is watching you do, which is why practising them in silence defeats the point.

/* Follow this EVERY TIME, including in the interview. It is
   also exactly what the interviewer is scoring you on. */

  1. RESTATE THE PROBLEM IN YOUR OWN WORDS.
     "So I'm given a sorted array and a target, and I need
      the indices of two numbers that add to it. Is there
      always exactly one answer?"
     -> catches misunderstanding before you write anything,
        and it is the single highest-scoring 30 seconds of
        the whole interview.

  2. ASK ABOUT THE CONSTRAINTS AND THE EDGE CASES.
     · How large is n? (this DETERMINES the target
       complexity - chapter 02)
     · Sorted? Duplicates? Negative numbers? Empty input?
     · Can I modify the input?
     · What should I return if there is no answer?

  3. WORK A SMALL EXAMPLE BY HAND.
     On paper, with 4-5 elements. THIS IS WHERE THE PATTERN
     REVEALS ITSELF. Skipping this step is the most common
     cause of a wrong approach confidently pursued.

  4. STATE THE BRUTE FORCE, WITH ITS COMPLEXITY.
     "I could check every pair - O(n^2). Let me improve
      that."
     Never stay silent while thinking. And never refuse to
     state a brute force because it is "too obvious" - it
     establishes a baseline and buys you thinking time.

  5. FIND THE INSIGHT THAT REMOVES WORK.
     Usually one of: the input is SORTED (binary search or
     two pointers), I am RECOMPUTING something (memoise or
     prefix sum), I only need the TOP FEW (heap), or there
     is REDUNDANT STATE I can drop.

  6. STATE THE APPROACH AND ITS COMPLEXITY BEFORE CODING.
     "Two pointers from both ends, O(n) time, O(1) space.
      Shall I code that?"
     -> gives the interviewer a chance to redirect you
        BEFORE you spend ten minutes on the wrong thing.

  7. CODE IT. Talk while you type. Use real names.

  8. TRACE IT ON THE EXAMPLE, out loud, line by line.
     You will find your own bug about a third of the time,
     and finding your own bug scores far better than the
     interviewer finding it.

  9. STATE THE EDGE CASES and check them: empty, one
     element, all equal, negatives, overflow, the target
     absent.

 10. STATE THE FINAL COMPLEXITY, time AND space.

/* AND THE THING PEOPLE GET WRONG: THE INTERVIEW IS NOT A
   TEST OF WHETHER YOU KNOW THE ANSWER. It is a simulation
   of working with you. A candidate who reasons clearly to
   a good-but-not-optimal solution usually outscores one
   who silently types a memorised optimal answer.        */

How this track is built

Every chapter is built to the same shape, so that after two or three of them you always know where to look for the thing you want.

The judge in the page

Every problem has a real editor. Write a solution, press Run tests (or Ctrl/⌘+Enter), and it executes against the test cases and shows you which ones fail, with the input, what was expected, and what you actually returned. It runs in your browser — no server, no account, no network. Your code is saved in local storage per problem, so you can close the tab and come back.

The runner executes JavaScript, because that is what a browser can run with zero dependencies. Reference solutions are given in C++, Python and Java as well, since those are what you will actually write in an interview. If your target language is C++, read the C++ solution and type the JavaScript — the algorithm is what you are practising, and the syntax difference for these problems is small.

A realistic schedule

How long this takes depends entirely on how much time you actually have, and the honest answer is that a compressed plan means covering less rather than covering the same material faster. Pick the row that matches your situation.

/* --- IF YOU HAVE 8+ WEEKS --- */
    week 1     complexity, arrays (02-08)
    week 2     searching, sorting, greedy (09-13)
    week 3     hashing, strings, bits, maths (14-19)
    week 4     lists, stacks, queues, heaps, design (20-26)
    week 5     trees and tries (27-30)
    week 6     graphs (31-34)
    week 7     recursion, backtracking (35-36)
    week 8     dynamic programming (37-41)
    then       range queries (42), then mixed practice
               under time pressure (43)

    3-5 problems a day, done PROPERLY, beats 15 skimmed.

/* --- IF YOU HAVE 2 WEEKS --- */
    Do the EASY and MEDIUM problems only, in this order:
      arrays/hashing (04,05,06,14) -> binary search (09,10)
      -> greedy (13) -> linked lists (20,21) ->
      trees (27,28) -> graphs (31,32) -> DP intro (37)
    Then chapter 43.
    THESE SIX AREAS COVER THE LARGE MAJORITY OF WHAT IS
    ACTUALLY ASKED.

/* --- IF YOU HAVE 2 DAYS --- */
    Read chapter 44, then chapter 43, then do the EASY
    problems in 04, 05, 09, 14, 20, 27, 31. Accept that you
    are optimising for "not freezing" rather than for
    solving a hard problem.

/* --- THE RULES THAT MAKE PRACTICE WORK --- */

  · ATTEMPT BEFORE READING. Give every problem an honest
    20-25 minutes before opening the hints. The struggle IS
    the learning; reading a solution you did not struggle
    with teaches almost nothing.

  · WHEN YOU DO LOOK, CLOSE IT AND RETYPE FROM MEMORY.
    Understanding a solution and being able to produce one
    are different skills, and only the second is tested.

  · REDO PROBLEMS. Anything you needed help with, redo in
    three days, then in a week. Spaced repetition works
    here exactly as it does for vocabulary.

  · SAY IT OUT LOUD. Practise narrating. Interviews are
    verbal, and the first time you try to think out loud
    should not be in the interview.

  · TIME YOURSELF eventually. 20 minutes easy, 30-35
    medium. But only after the pattern is solid - timing
    yourself while still learning just creates anxiety.

  · KEEP A LOG of every problem you got wrong and WHY:
    "off-by-one in the binary search bound", "forgot the
    empty-input case", "did not see it was a graph". After
    thirty entries the log tells you exactly what to drill,
    and it is almost never what you assumed.            */

Check yourself

  • Explain why pattern-based practice beats problem-count practice.
  • Recite at least six recognition signals and the pattern each implies.
  • List the ten steps of the per-problem method in order.
  • Say which step buys you the most credit for the least time, and why.
  • Explain why you state the approach and complexity before coding.
  • Say what the interview is actually measuring.
  • Give the four practice rules: attempt first, retype from memory, redo, narrate.
  • Describe what belongs in an error log and what it is for.

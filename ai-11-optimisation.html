Gradient descent and its variants

The loop that actually trains everything. Ten lines of arithmetic, a handful of hyperparameters, and more practical folklore than any other part of the subject.

The algorithm

Gradient descent is three lines and the entire engine of modern machine learning. Everything later in the chapter is an attempt to make these three lines behave better.

   REPEAT:
      1. compute the loss on some data
      2. compute the gradient of the loss w.r.t. every
         parameter        (chapter 07)
      3. step every parameter AGAINST its gradient

        theta  <-  theta - lr * grad

   THAT IS ALL. Everything below is about doing step 3
   better.

   THE THREE FLAVOURS, by how much data step 1 uses:

     BATCH (full)      the whole dataset per step.
                       Accurate gradient, impossibly slow,
                       and it gets stuck in whatever basin it
                       starts in.
     STOCHASTIC (SGD)  one sample per step.
                       Noisy, fast per step, and the noise
                       helps escape bad regions.
     MINI-BATCH        32-2048 samples. WHAT EVERYONE
                       ACTUALLY USES: a good gradient
                       estimate, and it saturates the
                       hardware's parallelism.

   THE LEARNING RATE IS THE MOST IMPORTANT HYPERPARAMETER
   IN THE FIELD:

     TOO SMALL     training crawls; may never arrive
     TOO LARGE     the loss oscillates, or diverges to nan
     JUST RIGHT    steady decrease

     loss                    loss                 loss
      |\                      |  /\  /\            |\
      | \____                 | /  \/  \           | \
      |      \____            |/         \         |  \___
      +----------- good       +----------- too     +------
                                            large     too small
                                            (bouncing) (crawling)

   AND THE UNCOMFORTABLE TRUTH: THERE IS NO WAY TO KNOW THE
   RIGHT VALUE WITHOUT TRYING. The standard method is an
   LR RANGE TEST - increase the learning rate exponentially
   over a few hundred steps, plot the loss, and pick roughly
   an order of magnitude below where it starts rising.

Momentum and adaptivity

Plain gradient descent is rarely used, and the reasons are concrete: specific failure shapes in the loss surface that specific modifications fix.

/* --- PLAIN SGD --- */
    theta -= lr * g

    Problems: it oscillates across narrow valleys, crawls
    along flat directions, and treats every parameter
    identically regardless of how large its gradients
    usually are.

/* --- MOMENTUM --- */
    v = beta * v + g              /* beta typically 0.9 */
    theta -= lr * v

    An exponentially weighted average of past gradients.
    THE PHYSICAL PICTURE: a ball rolling downhill has
    inertia. It builds speed in consistent directions and
    averages away oscillation across the valley.

    beta = 0.9 means the effective average covers roughly the
    last 1/(1-beta) = 10 gradients.

/* --- NESTEROV MOMENTUM --- */
    Evaluate the gradient at where momentum is ABOUT to take
    you, rather than where you are. A "look-ahead" correction
    that slightly improves convergence.

/* --- ADAGRAD: per-parameter learning rates --- */
    s += g^2                       /* elementwise */
    theta -= lr * g / (sqrt(s) + eps)

    Parameters with historically large gradients get smaller
    steps. Good for sparse features.
    FATAL FLAW: s only grows, so the effective learning rate
    decays monotonically to zero and training stops.

/* --- RMSPROP: fix the decay --- */
    s = beta2 * s + (1 - beta2) * g^2
    theta -= lr * g / (sqrt(s) + eps)

    An exponential average instead of a running sum, so it
    adapts to the RECENT gradient scale and never dies.

/* --- ADAM: momentum + RMSProp. THE DEFAULT. --- */
    m = beta1 * m + (1 - beta1) * g          /* 1st moment */
    v = beta2 * v + (1 - beta2) * g**2       /* 2nd moment */

    m_hat = m / (1 - beta1**t)               /* BIAS
                                                CORRECTION */
    v_hat = v / (1 - beta2**t)

    theta -= lr * m_hat / (sqrt(v_hat) + eps)

    DEFAULTS: beta1 = 0.9, beta2 = 0.999, eps = 1e-8.
    For transformers, beta2 = 0.95 is common and more
    stable.

    WHY THE BIAS CORRECTION: m and v start at zero, so early
    estimates are biased toward zero. Dividing by
    (1 - beta^t) - which is small early and approaches 1 -
    removes exactly that bias. Without it the first hundred
    steps take almost no movement.

    THE MEMORY COST: TWO EXTRA FULL-SIZE STATE TENSORS.
    Training a 7-billion-parameter model in fp32 needs
        28 GB weights + 28 GB gradients + 56 GB Adam state
        = 112 GB
    before activations. THIS IS WHY OPTIMISER STATE
    SHARDING (ZeRO, FSDP) exists, and why 8-bit optimisers
    are used.

/* --- ADAMW: use this one --- */
    theta -= lr * (m_hat / (sqrt(v_hat) + eps)
                   + weight_decay * theta)
                   ^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^
                   DECOUPLED from the adaptive scaling

    In plain Adam, L2 regularisation added to the gradient
    gets divided by sqrt(v) like everything else - so
    parameters with large gradients receive LESS
    regularisation, which is backwards. AdamW applies decay
    directly to the weights.

    ADAMW IS THE STANDARD FOR TRANSFORMERS. If you are
    choosing an optimiser and have no specific reason
    otherwise, use AdamW with lr around 3e-4 for a small
    model and weight_decay = 0.1.

/* --- THE NEWER ONES, briefly --- */
    LION       sign-based updates, less memory (one state
               tensor), competitive results
    SHAMPOO / SOAP   approximate second-order preconditioning
    ADAFACTOR  factorises the second-moment matrix to save
               memory; used for very large models
    MUON       orthogonalised momentum, strong recent results
               on transformer training

   None of these has displaced AdamW as the safe default.  */

Learning rate schedules

The learning rate is the hyperparameter that matters most, and holding it constant is almost never right. How it changes over training is a design decision in its own right.

/* A CONSTANT LEARNING RATE IS ALMOST NEVER OPTIMAL. Large
   early to make progress, small later to settle.        */

import numpy as np

# --- WARMUP: essential for transformers ---
def warmup_cosine(step, warmup=2000, total=100000,
                  peak=3e-4, final=3e-5):
    if step < warmup:
        return peak * step / warmup          # LINEAR RAMP
    p = (step - warmup) / (total - warmup)
    return final + 0.5 * (peak - final) * (1 +
                                           np.cos(np.pi * p))

/* WHY WARMUP IS NOT OPTIONAL FOR ADAM + TRANSFORMERS:
   at step 1, Adam's second-moment estimate is based on a
   single gradient and is unreliable, so the adaptive
   denominator is meaningless and the first few steps can be
   enormous. That destabilises LayerNorm statistics and
   attention patterns before they have formed. Ramping from
   zero over 1-2k steps avoids it. Skipping warmup is one of
   the most common causes of "my transformer will not train".

/* THE SCHEDULES YOU WILL MEET:
     COSINE          smooth decay to near zero. THE DEFAULT
                     for LLM pretraining.
     LINEAR DECAY    simple, works well for fine-tuning
     STEP            drop by 10x at fixed milestones.
                     Classic vision training.
     ONE-CYCLE       up then down, with high peak LR.
                     Fast convergence for smaller models.
     CONSTANT + COOLDOWN   hold, then decay at the end.
                     Convenient when the total number of
                     steps is not known in advance, which is
                     increasingly common.

/* --- GRADIENT CLIPPING: not optional either --- */
    torch.nn.utils.clip_grad_norm_(model.parameters(), 1.0)

    If the total gradient norm exceeds the threshold, scale
    the WHOLE gradient down to that norm - preserving its
    direction. A single bad batch can otherwise produce a
    gradient large enough to destroy the model in one step,
    and recovery may take thousands of steps or never
    happen.

/* --- THE COMPLETE TRAINING LOOP --- */
import torch

model = MyModel()
opt = torch.optim.AdamW(model.parameters(), lr=3e-4,
                        weight_decay=0.1,
                        betas=(0.9, 0.95))

for step, (x, y) in enumerate(dataloader):
    lr = warmup_cosine(step)
    for g in opt.param_groups:
        g['lr'] = lr

    logits = model(x)
    loss = F.cross_entropy(logits.view(-1, V), y.view(-1))

    opt.zero_grad(set_to_none=True)   # <<< chapter 07
    loss.backward()
    torch.nn.utils.clip_grad_norm_(model.parameters(), 1.0)
    opt.step()

    if step % 100 == 0:
        print(f"{step} loss {loss.item():.4f} lr {lr:.2e}")

/* THAT LOOP TRAINS ANY MODEL IN THIS TRACK, INCLUDING THE
   TRANSFORMER IN CHAPTER 28. The architecture changes; this
   does not.

/* --- GRADIENT ACCUMULATION, when the batch will not fit --- */
    for i, (x, y) in enumerate(dataloader):
        loss = compute_loss(x, y) / accum_steps
        loss.backward()                    # ACCUMULATES
        if (i + 1) % accum_steps == 0:
            clip_grad_norm_(...)
            opt.step()
            opt.zero_grad(set_to_none=True)

    Effective batch size = batch_size * accum_steps.
    NOTE THE DIVISION BY accum_steps - without it the
    effective learning rate is multiplied by accum_steps.  */
Debugging a training run, in order 
1. OVERFIT ONE BATCH. Take 8 samples, turn off
   regularisation, and train until the loss is ~0. If it
   cannot, the bug is in the model or the data
   pipeline, not the optimiser. Do this FIRST, always.
2. Check the step-0 loss against the theoretical value
   (log V for a V-way classifier — chapter 10).
3. Print gradient norms per layer (chapter 07).
4. Run an LR range test.
5. Only then tune anything else.

What the loss landscape is actually like

The picture most people carry — a bumpy surface full of local minima to get trapped in — turns out to be misleading for networks of realistic size, and the reality is more interesting.

/* THE PICTURE IN TEXTBOOKS - a smooth bowl with one minimum
   and some traps - is misleading for high-dimensional
   networks.

/* --- LOCAL MINIMA ARE NOT THE PROBLEM --- */
    In n dimensions, a local minimum requires ALL n
    eigenvalues of the Hessian to be positive. For large n
    that is astronomically unlikely at a random critical
    point. SADDLE POINTS - some directions up, some down -
    dominate instead.

    And saddles are escapable: gradient descent with any
    noise eventually finds a descending direction. This is
    part of why SGD's noise is a feature, and why the
    "getting stuck in a local minimum" worry from classical
    optimisation largely does not apply here.

/* --- MOST MINIMA ARE EQUIVALENT --- */
    Empirically, the many minima that gradient descent finds
    in a large network have similar loss. You are not
    searching for THE optimum; you are finding one of
    enormously many adequate ones.

/* --- FLAT MINIMA GENERALISE BETTER --- */
    A wide, flat basin means small parameter perturbations
    barely change the loss - so the solution is robust, and
    it tends to transfer to unseen data. Sharp minima do not.

    THIS EXPLAINS SEVERAL PRACTICES:
      · SMALL BATCHES generalise better than very large ones:
        their gradient noise cannot settle into a narrow
        basin
      · SAM (sharpness-aware minimisation) explicitly seeks
        flat regions
      · weight averaging (SWA, EMA of weights) lands in the
        middle of a basin rather than on its wall, and is
        nearly free

/* --- THE PRACTICAL CONSEQUENCES --- */
    · noise HELPS. Do not try to eliminate it.
    · the batch size and learning rate are COUPLED. The
      common heuristic: scale lr with the square root of the
      batch size for Adam, linearly for SGD.
    · a "bad run" is often just an unlucky initialisation or
      data order. Try a different seed before redesigning.
    · AND WATCH FOR LOSS SPIKES in large-model training.
      They are common, usually recoverable, and the standard
      response is to roll back to a checkpoint and skip the
      offending data batch - which is a real operational
      procedure, not a joke.                              */

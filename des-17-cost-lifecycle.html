Cost, BOM and product lifecycle

Cost is a requirement, not an afterthought, and treating it as one is a reliable senior signal. So is understanding that the price of a part is a small fraction of what it actually costs to put in a product for ten years.

What a design actually costs

   FOUR CATEGORIES, and candidates usually see only the
   first.

   1. BOM - the parts, per unit
        processor, memory, radio, sensors, passives, PCB,
        connectors, enclosure, battery, antenna.
        Scales with volume; negotiable at scale.

   2. MANUFACTURING - per unit
        assembly, test cycle time, provisioning time,
        yield loss, packaging, logistics.
        A 30-second test at a million units is 8300 hours
        of line time.

   3. NON-RECURRING ENGINEERING - once
        design, layout, tooling for the enclosure,
        certification (EMC, radio, safety - each a real
        sum and a real schedule), firmware development.
        Amortised across the volume: at 200 units, NRE
        dominates completely; at 2 million it disappears.

   4. LIFECYCLE - for years
        firmware maintenance and security updates, cloud
        service operation, field support and returns,
        spares and repair, obsolescence management.
        The category nobody budgets and everybody pays.

   THE VOLUME QUESTION DECIDES WHICH ONE MATTERS:

     200 units      NRE dominates. Use a dev-kit-derived
                    design, an off-the-shelf module, and
                    do not optimise the BOM at all -
                    engineering time costs more than
                    parts.
     20,000         balanced. Custom PCB pays for itself;
                    BOM optimisation starts to matter.
     2,000,000      BOM is everything. Ten cents is
                    $200,000. Now it is worth weeks of
                    engineering to remove a part.

   ASKING "WHAT IS THE VOLUME?" EARLY is one of the
   highest-value clarifying questions available, because
   the correct engineering approach inverts across that
   range.

Where the money is, and the levers

TYPICAL BOM SHAPE for a connected sensor product:

    processor / module      25-40%
    radio (if separate)     10-20%
    enclosure + mechanical  15-25%
    PCB + assembly          10-15%
    battery                  5-15%
    sensors                  5-15%
    passives + connectors     5-10%

  START FROM THE ACTUAL BREAKDOWN, not intuition. The
  answer to "cut $2" is almost never in the passives.

THE LEVERS, roughly by yield:

  INTEGRATE     a part with the radio, MCU and memory in
                one module removes several parts and a
                certification burden. A pre-certified radio
                module costs more per unit than a bare
                chip and saves tens of thousands in
                certification - which inverts at high
                volume.
  DOWNSIZE      a smaller MCU, if the memory budget allows
                (which is why the budget matters)
  ELIMINATE     the crystal (use the internal oscillator -
                accepting the accuracy trade), the external
                flash (if OTA can be single-bank), a
                regulator (if one rail can serve)
  SIMPLIFY      fewer PCB layers, larger component
                packages (cheaper assembly), fewer unique
                part numbers
  RELAX         temperature grade, tolerance, component
                lifetime - where the environment genuinely
                permits
  NEGOTIATE     at volume, price is a conversation

WHAT NOT TO CUT, and say this explicitly in a design
round:
  - anything supporting safety
  - test points and debug interfaces (chapter 15)
  - the ability to update firmware
  - decoupling capacitors
  Saving twenty cents by deleting a debug header costs
  far more the first time a unit comes back.
Availability is a design input, not a purchasing problem

A perfect part with a 52-week lead time is not a part you can ship, and recent supply disruption ended more projects than any technical factor. The design responses: prefer families with pin-compatible members so a shortage becomes a firmware change rather than a respin; do not design to the exact limit of a part's memory or peripheral count, so substitution stays possible; abstract the vendor HAL behind your own interface; and check for a published longevity commitment on anything going into a ten-year product. Raising this unprompted marks you as someone who has shipped.

The obligations you take on

A DEVICE WITH A TEN-YEAR FIELD LIFE COMMITS YOU TO:

  SECURITY MAINTENANCE
    If it runs Linux, someone tracks kernel and library
    CVEs for a decade and ships fixes. That requires the
    update mechanism to still work, the signing keys to
    still exist and be uncontaminated, and the build to be
    REPRODUCIBLE years later - pinned toolchains, archived
    artefacts, archived symbols for decoding crash logs.

  CERTIFICATE EXPIRY
    Device certificates and the CAs they trust have
    lifetimes. A device with a hard-coded trust store that
    expires becomes permanently unable to connect. This
    has happened to real products. The trust store must
    be updatable.

  SERVICE EVOLUTION
    The cloud service will be rewritten. The device must
    tolerate that, which argues for a versioned, stable
    interface and a device that degrades gracefully when
    the far end changes.

  OBSOLESCENCE
    Parts go end-of-life. You need last-time-buy
    forecasting, or a qualified alternative, or a planned
    respin.

  CRYPTOGRAPHIC AGILITY
    Algorithms fine today may not be in ten years. The
    design should allow changing them.

  AND AN END-OF-SUPPORT POLICY, stated to customers, so
  the obligation has a defined end rather than being
  open-ended.

THE DESIGN CONSEQUENCE: several of these are impossible
to retrofit. An updatable trust store, a reproducible
build and archived symbols cost almost nothing at design
time and cannot be added to a fleet already deployed.

Questions

How does the production volume change your engineering approach?Mid

It inverts it. At 200 units, non-recurring engineering dominates: design time, certification and tooling are spread over almost nothing, so the right approach is an off-the-shelf module, a dev-kit-derived design and no BOM optimisation at all — engineering hours cost far more than parts. A pre-certified radio module that costs $8 instead of a $3 chip is obviously correct because it saves tens of thousands in certification.

At 2 million units, the arithmetic reverses completely: ten cents is $200,000, so weeks of engineering to remove a part or drop a PCB layer pays for itself many times over, and taking on the certification yourself to use the bare chip becomes the right answer.

At 20,000 it is balanced — a custom PCB pays for itself, some BOM work is justified, and modules are still often right.

Which is why "what is the volume?" is one of the highest-value clarifying questions in a design round: the correct answer to almost every subsequent question depends on it.

Listening forThe inversion, with concrete numbers rather than a general statement that volume matters.
You must remove $2 from a $15 BOM. Where do you start?Senior

From the actual cost breakdown, biggest items first — typically the processor or module, the radio, the enclosure, the PCB and assembly, and the battery. Intuition is unreliable here; the answer is almost never in the passives.

Then ask of each large item: what requirement forces this, and is that requirement real? The levers by yield: drop to a smaller MCU if the memory budget allows; remove external flash if the update scheme can be single-bank plus recovery; use the internal oscillator instead of a crystal, accepting the accuracy trade and checking it against the UART and radio requirements; reduce PCB layers; relax temperature grade where the environment permits; consolidate rails.

What I would protect and say so: anything supporting safety, test points and debug interfaces, the ability to update firmware, and decoupling. Saving twenty cents by deleting a debug header costs more the first time a unit is returned.

And the non-obvious point: at volume, a part's cost includes qualification, second-sourcing and ten-year availability. A cheaper part with a single supplier can be more expensive overall.

Listening forStarting from data, naming what must not be cut, and total cost of ownership rather than unit price.
Module or bare chip for the radio?Mid

Volume and certification decide it. A pre-certified module costs several dollars more per unit and comes with the radio certification already done — which for a bare chip means an RF layout you must get right, an antenna you must design and tune, and a certification campaign costing tens of thousands and weeks of schedule, repeated for each region you sell into.

So at low and medium volume the module is almost always correct: it converts a large fixed cost and a large technical risk into a modest per-unit cost. At high volume the fixed cost amortises away and the per-unit saving dominates, so the bare chip wins — provided you have RF layout competence in-house, which is a genuine skill and not one every team has.

Other factors worth raising: modules simplify supply (one part, one qualification) and often have better long-term availability commitments; bare chips give you more control over antenna placement and board size, which matters in a constrained enclosure. And a module still requires you to follow its integration guide — ground plane, keep-out, antenna clearance — or its certification does not transfer.

Listening forCertification cost as the deciding factor, and the caveat that a module's certification is conditional on correct integration.
What does a ten-year field life commit you to?Senior

Security maintenance for the whole period, which means tracking CVEs in everything you shipped — kernel, libraries, protocol stacks — and being able to ship fixes ten years later. That in turn requires a build that is reproducible a decade on (pinned toolchains, archived artefacts) and signing keys that still exist and have not been compromised.

Certificate expiry, which has broken real products: device certificates and the CAs they trust have lifetimes, and a hard-coded trust store that expires makes the device permanently unable to connect. The trust store must be updatable.

Service evolution — the cloud service will be rewritten, so the device needs a versioned, stable interface and graceful degradation. Component obsolescence, needing last-time-buy forecasting or a qualified alternative. Cryptographic agility, since algorithms acceptable today may not be. And archived symbols per release, or a crash log from a field unit cannot be decoded.

The design point: several of these cost almost nothing at design time and cannot be retrofitted to a deployed fleet. And there should be a stated end-of-support date, so the obligation is bounded.

Listening forCertificate expiry and build reproducibility — concrete, and both bite real products.
How do you decide between building and buying a subsystem?Senior

By asking whether it is differentiating. If a subsystem is what makes the product worth buying, build it. If it is table stakes — a BLE stack, a TCP/IP stack, a filesystem, a bootloader, an OTA mechanism — buy or adopt an existing one, because you will not out-engineer a mature implementation and the maintenance is permanent.

The costs of buying that people underestimate: licence terms (particularly for anything shipping in firmware), the dependency on a vendor's roadmap and support quality, integration effort that is rarely as small as advertised, and the fact that you inherit its vulnerabilities in code running with full privilege.

The costs of building: not the initial development, which is usually estimated adequately, but the ten-year maintenance, the edge cases you discover in the field, and the fact that the person who wrote it will leave.

The middle path worth mentioning: adopt an open-source implementation, which gives you the code and the ability to fix it without a vendor dependency, at the cost of owning the integration and tracking upstream security fixes yourself. For most embedded infrastructure that is the pragmatic answer.

Listening for"Is it differentiating?" as the test, and ten-year maintenance as the real cost of building.
A key component goes end-of-life. What do you do?Senior

Immediately: quantify the exposure — how many units remain to be built, over what period, and what stock exists in the channel. That determines whether this is urgent or merely important.

Then the options, in order of disruption. Last-time buy: purchase enough to cover the remaining production plus service spares, which needs a demand forecast and ties up capital and storage — and components have shelf lives, particularly anything with moisture sensitivity or a battery. Drop-in alternative: a pin- and function-compatible part, which needs qualification and possibly a firmware change but no layout work. Redesign: a different part requiring layout and firmware changes, plus re-certification if it affects RF or safety.

The preventive design decisions that make this cheap: choosing families with several pin-compatible members; not designing to the exact limit of a part's capability so substitution stays possible; abstracting the vendor HAL behind your own interface; and checking longevity commitments before selecting anything for a long-lived product.

And the process point: obsolescence notices need to reach engineering, not just purchasing — a PCN that lands in a mailbox nobody reads is how this becomes an emergency.

Listening forLast-time-buy shelf life, and the preventive measures rather than only the reactive ones.
How does the cost of a field failure compare with the cost of preventing it?Senior

Usually by orders of magnitude, and the comparison is what justifies design-for-test and design-for-update spending.

A field failure costs: the support interaction, the return logistics, diagnosis time, the repair or replacement, the replacement shipping, and — the largest and least measurable — the customer's confidence. For a device installed by an engineer, add a site visit, which can exceed the product's value entirely. A fleet-wide issue adds a recall, possible regulatory involvement and reputational damage.

Against that: a debug header costs cents; a persistent crash log costs forty lines of code; a hardware-in-the-loop test rig costs a few weeks once; a staged rollout costs a bit of server logic. Each of those prevents or shortens a class of field failure permanently.

The framing I would use in a design review: not "does this feature justify itself" but "what does one avoided truck roll pay for?" — and the answer is usually all of the above combined. This is also why arguing against removing test points is not pedantry.

Listening forQuantifying the failure cost including the site visit, and using it to justify the observability investment.
What is the hidden cost of supporting many product variants?Senior

It multiplies through every downstream process. Each variant needs its own testing, its own certification in some cases, its own firmware build and release, its own documentation and spares, and its own place in the traceability system — and every combination must be regression-tested on every release, so the test matrix grows faster than the variant count.

The firmware response that contains it: one image for all variants, with the hardware configuration read at runtime from a resistor divider, an ID chip or a data region written at provisioning. That way the factory flashes one file, releases are single artefacts, and a variant is a data change rather than a build. Device tree overlays are the same idea at a larger scale.

Where that breaks down: when variants differ so much that the shared image carries a lot of dead code for each — at which point the memory cost may force separate builds, and the variant count should be questioned commercially rather than solved technically.

The point worth making in a design round: variants are a commercial decision with an engineering cost that is rarely visible to the people making it, and surfacing that cost is part of the job.

Listening forOne image plus runtime configuration, and the test-matrix explosion.
When is it right to over-engineer?Senior

Where the cost of being wrong is asymmetric and the fix is impossible later. Concretely: memory headroom, because running out at 90% completion is a respin; test and debug interfaces, because they cannot be added to a deployed fleet; the update mechanism, because it is what lets you fix everything else; observability, because a field failure you cannot diagnose is permanent; and margin on anything physical — thermal, electrical, mechanical — because the field is harsher than the bench.

Where not to: features nobody asked for; a secure element for a threat model that does not justify it; redundancy where the analysis shows no single point of failure that matters; and premature performance optimisation, which usually costs clarity for a gain nobody measured.

The test I would apply: can this be added later, and what does it cost if it cannot? If it can be added later, defer it. If it cannot and the consequence is severe, spend now — that is not over-engineering, it is correctly pricing an option.

Listening forThe "can it be added later?" test, which is the general principle behind all the specific cases.
How would you present a cost/schedule trade-off to a non-engineer?Senior

As a small number of concrete options with their consequences quantified, and a recommendation — not as a technical explanation and not as a single answer presented as inevitable.

For example: "Option A keeps the current part: $12 BOM, ships in March, but we have no OTA capability so a field bug means a site visit at roughly £150 each. Option B moves to a part with double the flash: $12.80 BOM, ships in May, and we can fix things remotely. At 20,000 units, option B costs $16,000 more in parts and pays for itself if more than about a hundred units ever need a visit. I recommend B."

What makes that work: the options are genuinely different rather than a strawman and a preference; the consequences are in units the audience cares about (money and dates, not kilobytes); the assumption is visible and can be challenged; and there is a recommendation, because "here are the options" without one pushes an engineering decision onto someone unqualified to make it.

And be honest about uncertainty — giving a range with the reason is more credible than a false precision that will be wrong.

Listening forQuantifying in the audience's units and making a recommendation rather than deferring.

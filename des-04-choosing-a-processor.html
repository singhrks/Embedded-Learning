Choosing the processor: MCU, MPU, SoC, FPGA

The processor choice is the decision every other decision hangs off, and it is almost always made by elimination rather than selection — three constraints rule out most of the space, and what remains is a short list you can defend.

Choosing by elimination

   FOUR QUESTIONS, IN THIS ORDER. Each usually removes a
   whole class, and after three you rarely have a choice
   left to agonise over.

   1. IS IT BATTERY POWERED FOR MONTHS OR YEARS?
        yes -> MCU. No application processor idles in the
               microamp range. This one constraint decides
               more designs than any other.

   2. IS THERE A HARD DEADLINE SHORTER THAN ~1 ms?
        yes -> MCU for that part, at least. Linux cannot
               promise it. Note "for that part": the answer
               is often BOTH, with the loop on an MCU and
               everything else on an AP.

   3. DOES IT NEED A NETWORK STACK, A FILESYSTEM, A
      DISPLAY STACK, OR THIRD-PARTY SOFTWARE?
        yes -> application processor running Linux. You do
               not want to own a TCP/IP stack, a USB host
               stack and a font renderer.

   4. IS THERE PARALLEL SIGNAL PROCESSING, NON-STANDARD
      I/O, OR NANOSECOND TIMING ON MANY CHANNELS?
        yes -> FPGA. Otherwise no: the engineering cost is
               large and it is rarely the cheapest answer
               to a problem a processor can solve.

   WHAT IS LEFT is usually a family, and then you pick
   within it on memory, peripherals, package and
   availability - which is a shortlist exercise, not a
   design decision.
The answer is very often "both"

A large fraction of real products contain an application processor and a microcontroller. The AP handles the interface, connectivity and updates; the MCU handles the control loop, the power management and the safety interlocks; they talk over SPI or UART. Proposing this split unprompted — with its costs, which are an extra part, a bus, a protocol to define and two build systems to maintain — is one of the more reliable ways to signal that you have shipped hardware. It also shrinks a safety certification scope to a small auditable binary on a simple core.

Choosing within a family

Once the class is settled, the shortlist criteria, roughly
in the order they eliminate candidates:

  MEMORY        Flash and RAM, with the OTA slot counted
                (chapter 05). Pick the part with headroom -
                running out at 90% through the project is a
                board respin.

  PERIPHERAL    Count what you need and check the pin mux
  COUNT         actually allows that combination
                SIMULTANEOUSLY. Datasheets list peripherals;
                the mux table decides whether you can have
                two SPIs and a UART at once on your pinout.

  CORE FEATURES FPU? DSP instructions? TrustZone-M? A
                hardware divider? A part without an FPU
                turns every float into a library call -
                roughly 40x - which silently destroys a
                port from a bigger part.

  POWER MODES   Not just "has a sleep mode" - how much RAM
                is retained, what can wake it, and how long
                does waking take? A part that loses RAM in
                its deepest mode forces a different software
                architecture.

  PACKAGE       Can your PCB house and your assembler place
                it? A 0.4 mm BGA needs more PCB layers and a
                better assembly process than a QFP.

  AVAILABILITY  Lead time, lifetime commitment, and whether
                a second source exists. This has ended more
                projects in recent years than any technical
                factor, and mentioning it marks you as
                someone who has shipped.

  ECOSYSTEM     Vendor HAL quality, RTOS ports, community,
                debug tooling. A cheaper part with bad
                tooling costs more in engineering time than
                it saves in BOM.

Questions

How do you choose between a microcontroller and an application processor?Junior

By elimination against three constraints, in order. Power: if it runs for months on a battery, it is an MCU — no AP idles in microamps. Determinism: if there is a hard deadline under about a millisecond, that part must be on an MCU, because Linux's latency tail is milliseconds and not bounded. Software need: if it needs TCP/IP, a filesystem, a display stack or third-party software, you want Linux and therefore an AP.

Then note that these are not exclusive: the common answer is both, with the deadline-bearing work on an MCU and everything else on an AP, connected over SPI or UART.

Listening forAn ordered elimination rather than a feature comparison, and the "both" option.
What is the real cost of adding Linux to a design?Senior

Far more than the processor price difference. You acquire: external DRAM and its layout constraints, a larger and more complex PCB, a boot time measured in hundreds of milliseconds, milliamps of idle current, a root filesystem someone must build and maintain (Yocto or Buildroot), a kernel to track for security fixes for the product's lifetime, an update mechanism that must survive power loss, and a much larger attack surface.

You also acquire real benefits: a networking stack you do not write, a filesystem, drivers for almost everything, a language and library ecosystem, and the ability to hire people who already know it.

The framing that scores: Linux is not a component, it is a commitment. On a product with a ten-year field life, somebody owns kernel CVEs for ten years. If the device's function does not need what Linux provides, an MCU is dramatically cheaper across the whole lifecycle, not just in BOM.

Listening forLifecycle and maintenance cost, not just BOM and boot time.
When is an FPGA the right answer, and when is it a mistake?Senior

Right when you need genuine parallelism (many channels processed simultaneously), nanosecond-deterministic timing, a non-standard or very high-rate interface that no processor peripheral implements, or when you are prototyping something destined for an ASIC. Also right when a single FPGA replaces several devices and reduces overall cost and latency.

A mistake when it is chosen for raw throughput that a modern processor with DMA and SIMD could reach. The engineering cost is large: HDL skills are scarcer and more expensive, build times are minutes-to-hours rather than seconds, debugging is harder, verification is a discipline of its own, static power is significant, and the tooling is proprietary. A design that could have been an MCU plus a DMA peripheral and became an FPGA usually ships late.

The middle option worth naming: an MCU with programmable I/O — RP2040 and RP1's PIO blocks — covers a useful slice of "I need exact timing on a few pins" without any of the FPGA cost.

Listening forEngineering cost as a first-class factor, and awareness of the PIO middle ground.
You need two SPI buses, three UARTs and an I2C bus. The datasheet says the part has all of them. What do you still need to check?Mid

The pin multiplexing table — that the part can expose that combination simultaneously on your chosen package. Peripherals share pins; a datasheet listing six UARTs does not promise six usable at once, and each peripheral is available only on specific pins fixed by the silicon mux.

Concretely: build the pin assignment before committing to the part, using the vendor's pin-planning tool. Check the alternate-function table (which lives in the datasheet, not the reference manual), confirm your package has those pins bonded out, and leave a couple of spares.

Related traps: some peripherals collide with the debug pins (losing SWD in production is painful); external interrupt lines are often multiplexed by pin number across ports, so PA0 and PB0 cannot both be interrupts; and the boot-mode pins may be unusable as general I/O.

Listening forKnowing that the mux is a real constraint discovered before layout, not after.
The team wants to reuse code from a Cortex-M4 project on a new Cortex-M0+ part to save $1.50. What do you say?Mid

That the saving is real but the port is not free, and I would want to check four things before agreeing.

Floating point: the M0+ has no FPU, so every float becomes a library call — roughly 40× slower. If the existing code does float maths in a control loop, it must be converted to fixed-point, which is real work and a real source of bugs.

Instruction set: M0+ is ARMv6-M — no BASEPRI (so RTOS critical sections must use PRIMASK, which blocks all interrupts), no bit-banding, no hardware divide, fewer priority bits (typically 2, so 4 levels instead of 16), and a fixed vector table with no VTOR — which breaks a bootloader design that relocates it.

Memory: re-run the flash and RAM budget including the OTA slot. Performance: re-run the CPU utilisation sum at the lower clock.

Then quantify: if it is 10,000 units, $1.50 is $15,000 against perhaps four engineer-weeks. If it is 1,000,000 units, the conversation is entirely different. The answer is arithmetic, not preference.

Listening forConcrete architectural differences rather than "it might be slower", and framing the decision as volume × saving versus engineering cost.
What is the memory headroom you would want when selecting a part?Mid

Rule of thumb: pick a part where the first working version uses no more than about half the flash and about half the RAM. Firmware grows — new features, error handling, logging, a protocol version 2 — and running out at 90% completion means a board respin, which costs months.

Specifically remember to budget: the OTA slot, which typically doubles the flash requirement; per-task stacks with their 50% margin; DMA and protocol buffers; and the .data initialisers, which consume flash and RAM.

Where the headroom rule bends: at very high volume the cost per byte matters enough to size tightly, and you accept the engineering discipline that comes with it. That is a deliberate trade, made knowingly, not the default.

Listening forThe OTA slot, which is the most commonly forgotten term.
How does part availability affect a design decision?Senior

It can override every technical consideration. A perfect part with a 52-week lead time and no second source is not a part you can ship. Recent supply disruptions ended more projects than any technical factor.

What I would check before committing: current lead time and stock across distributors; whether the vendor publishes a longevity or product-longevity commitment (important for industrial and automotive, where a ten-year build is normal); whether pin-compatible alternatives exist within the family so a shortage means a firmware change rather than a respin; and whether a genuinely different second source exists.

The design response: prefer families with a range of pin-compatible members so you can move up or down; avoid designing to the exact limit of a part's memory or peripheral count, so you can substitute; and abstract the vendor HAL behind your own interface so that changing silicon is contained.

Listening forPin-compatible family members as a deliberate risk mitigation — this is a practitioner's answer.
Under what circumstances would you put two microcontrollers in one product?Senior

Four reasons, in rough order of how often they are the real one.

Safety separation: the safety function lives on its own MCU, so the complex, frequently-updated application cannot compromise it. This dramatically shrinks the certification scope — you certify a small binary on a simple core instead of the whole system.

Determinism separation: a control loop that must not jitter, isolated from a communications stack whose timing is unpredictable.

Power domains: a tiny always-on MCU in the microamp range watches a button or a sensor and wakes a much larger processor only when needed. This is how many battery products achieve their life.

Physical distribution: the sensing is somewhere the main board is not, and running a digital bus to it is better than running analog signals.

The costs to state: another part, another build and release process, a protocol between them, a second firmware to update in the field (and the ordering problem when they must be updated together), and a harder debugging story across the boundary.

Listening forCertification scope as a motivation, and the two-firmware update ordering problem as a cost.
The requirement is "must run machine learning inference". How does that change processor selection?Mid

First push back on the requirement, because "ML" spans four orders of magnitude of compute. What model, what input rate, what latency, and what accuracy target? A keyword spotter on 16 kHz audio and a person detector on 1080p video have nothing in common.

Then size it: parameters, operations per inference, inferences per second. A quantised keyword model might be 50 kB and a few MOPS — that runs on a Cortex-M4 with DSP instructions, using CMSIS-NN, within a battery budget. A small vision model is tens of MOPS to GOPS and wants an NPU or at least a DSP; a large model wants an application processor with a dedicated accelerator.

The architectural levers to raise: quantise to int8 (usually 4× smaller and much faster, with small accuracy loss); prune; run the model only after a cheap always-on trigger (a low-power wake word or motion detector gating an expensive classifier) — that gating pattern is often what makes the power budget work.

Listening forRefusing to accept "ML" as a specification, and the cheap-trigger gating pattern.
What would make you choose a part with TrustZone-M or a secure element?Senior

A concrete threat that justifies the cost, not a general wish to be secure. The questions: what asset is being protected, who is the attacker, and what do they gain? A device that holds a per-device key used to authenticate to a cloud service, or that must prove firmware integrity, or that handles payment or medical data, has a real answer.

TrustZone-M buys you isolation on one chip — secure and non-secure worlds, so a bug in the application cannot read the key or bypass the crypto. It is cheap (it is a core feature, not an extra part) but it is only as strong as your secure-world code, and it does not resist physical attack.

A secure element is a separate hardened chip with its own countermeasures against physical and side-channel attack. It costs a part and a bus but the key genuinely never leaves it.

The decision: if the threat is remote software attack, TrustZone-M or an MCU key store is usually proportionate. If the attacker has the device in their hands and extraction compromises more than that one unit, you want a secure element. And if the answer to "what do they gain" is "nothing", say so and spend the money elsewhere.

Listening forThreat-model-first reasoning, and the willingness to say security hardware is not warranted.

Connectivity and protocol selection

On a battery device the radio is usually the largest consumer of energy, so connectivity is not a feature bolted on at the end — it is a constraint that shapes the whole architecture. The right question is never "which radio is best" but "how many bytes, how often, how far, and who initiates".

The four questions

   1. HOW MUCH DATA, HOW OFTEN?
        20 bytes a minute and 4 Mbit/s of video are
        different products. Compute bytes per day - it is
        often startlingly small, and that opens options
        people dismiss.

   2. HOW FAR, AND THROUGH WHAT?
        10 m line of sight, 30 m through two walls, and
        5 km across a field are different radios. Concrete
        and metal are the enemy; 2.4 GHz penetrates worse
        than sub-GHz.

   3. WHO INITIATES, AND HOW QUICKLY MUST IT RESPOND?
        If the device only ever reports, it can sleep with
        the radio OFF and wake on its own schedule - by far
        the cheapest arrangement. If it must RECEIVE a
        command at any moment, something must listen, and
        listening costs energy continuously.

   4. WHAT IS THE POWER BUDGET?
        Compute energy per message, not current. A radio
        that draws 100 mA for 2 ms may beat one drawing
        15 mA for 200 ms.

   THE THIRD QUESTION IS THE ARCHITECTURAL ONE. "The
   device must be controllable from the app instantly"
   and "the device reports every 15 minutes" produce
   completely different designs, and the requirement is
   often negotiable once its cost is shown.

Energy per message is the number, not current

Comparing radios by their transmit current is meaningless. What matters is joules per useful byte delivered, which includes the time spent waking, acquiring, negotiating, transmitting, waiting for an acknowledgement and returning to sleep. A protocol with a long connection setup can cost more in overhead than in payload for a 20-byte message — which is why BLE connectionless advertising, or LoRaWAN's fire-and-forget uplink, can beat a nominally faster link for tiny infrequent messages.

The protocol above the radio

THE LAYERS, and where the decisions are:

  APPLICATION   your message format. CBOR or Protobuf if
                bytes matter (and on a radio, bytes are
                energy); JSON only if they do not.

  TRANSPORT     MQTT   pub/sub, broker-based, tiny client,
                       QoS 0/1/2, retained messages. The
                       default for telemetry.
                CoAP   REST over UDP, designed for
                       constrained devices, much lighter
                       than HTTP.
                HTTP   universal and heavy - a poor fit for
                       a battery device.

  SECURITY      TLS/DTLS. Cost: 20-50 kB of RAM for a
                handshake, several kB of flash, and real
                time and energy per connection. On an MCU
                this is often the largest single consumer
                of both.
                Alternatives: pre-shared keys, or
                application-layer signing with a much
                smaller footprint, if the threat model
                allows it.

THE DESIGN CONSEQUENCE THAT SURPRISES PEOPLE: a TLS
handshake can cost more energy than a day of measurements.
So you either keep the session alive (which costs standby
power) or you batch aggressively so handshakes are rare.
That trade-off IS the architecture for a connected battery
device, and naming it unprompted is a strong signal.

PROVISIONING - how does the device get onto the network?
  BLE from a phone app, NFC tap, a soft AP the user joins,
  a QR code with credentials, or pre-provisioned at the
  factory. This is a product decision with real UX and
  security consequences, and it is routinely left until
  too late.

Questions

A sensor reports 20 bytes every 15 minutes from a field 3 km from the nearest building. What connectivity?Mid

LoRaWAN, or a proprietary sub-GHz link. Compute first: 20 bytes × 96 messages a day is under 2 kB/day, which is nothing — so the constraint is entirely range and energy, not throughput.

That eliminates BLE and Wi-Fi on range immediately. Cellular (NB-IoT or LTE-M) works if there is coverage and you accept a subscription and higher energy per message. LoRaWAN reaches several kilometres, costs microjoules per message, and runs for years on a cell — but needs a gateway, which you may have to deploy.

The follow-up worth pre-empting: if you deploy the gateway anyway, a proprietary sub-GHz protocol gives you more control and no LoRaWAN network-server dependency, at the cost of writing and maintaining the protocol yourself. If there are many sensors across a wide area and someone else already runs a LoRaWAN network, use it.

Listening forComputing the data rate first and noticing it is irrelevant, so range and energy decide.
Why does "the device must respond instantly to an app command" change everything?Senior

Because it forces something to be listening continuously, and listening costs energy whether or not anything arrives. A device that only ever reports can keep its radio off and wake on its own schedule — microamps average. A device that must receive at any moment must either maintain a connection (paying standby current) or advertise/poll frequently (paying per interval).

The design levers, and this is the useful part of the answer: negotiate the latency. "Instant" is rarely required — 1 second, 5 seconds and 30 seconds have very different costs, and a BLE connection interval or a polling period can be traded directly against battery life. Compute the curve and present it: "at 100 ms latency this is 4 months; at 2 seconds it is 2 years — which do you want?"

Alternative architectures: a wake-on-radio scheme where a low-power receiver listens for a simple preamble; a scheduled window where the device listens briefly after each report, so commands are delivered with bounded delay; or accepting that commands are queued and delivered at the next check-in.

Listening forTreating latency as a negotiable parameter with a quantified cost curve, rather than a fixed requirement.
What does TLS cost on a constrained device, and what are the alternatives?Senior

Substantial, in three currencies. RAM: a handshake needs roughly 20–50 kB depending on cipher suite and certificate chain length, which on a 64 kB part may be more than everything else combined. Flash: several kB to tens of kB for the library. Energy and time: a full handshake involves asymmetric crypto and several round trips, and on a slow link it can cost more energy than a day of sensor readings.

The mitigations: session resumption (tickets or session IDs), so reconnects skip the expensive part; ECC cipher suites rather than RSA, which are dramatically cheaper on a small core; DTLS with connection ID over UDP, which survives address changes without a new handshake; and hardware crypto acceleration if the part has it.

The alternatives when TLS genuinely does not fit: pre-shared key cipher suites, which skip certificates entirely; or application-layer security — sign and encrypt the payload with a per-device key and send it over plain transport. That gives you integrity and confidentiality for a few hundred bytes of code, at the cost of no forward secrecy and a key management problem you must solve yourself.

What not to do: ship without transport security because it was inconvenient, and describe the device as secure.

Listening forSession resumption and ECC as first-line mitigations, and honesty about what the alternatives give up.
How does a device get onto a Wi-Fi network with no keyboard or screen?Mid

Several standard patterns, each with a UX and security trade. BLE provisioning: the device advertises, a phone app connects and passes credentials — the most common consumer answer, and it means shipping a second radio. Soft AP: the device becomes an access point the user joins and configures through a captive portal; no second radio, but a clumsy flow and users get lost. NFC: tap to transfer credentials; works even with a flat battery if the tag is passive, and is excellent for industrial provisioning. WPS: simple but widely deprecated for security reasons. QR code: the device shows or ships with a code the app scans to identify it, usually combined with one of the above. Factory pre-provisioning: for a controlled deployment, put the credentials in at manufacture.

The security questions to raise: how do you ensure the person provisioning is authorised (proximity is a weak proof); is the credential encrypted in transit and at rest on the device; and how is the device de-provisioned when it changes hands, which is a real requirement people forget.

Listening forNaming several options with trade-offs, and raising de-provisioning.
The network is unavailable for six hours. What should the device do?Mid

Buffer locally and retry with backoff — and the details are where the design is.

Buffering: size the store from the outage you intend to survive: 6 hours × 4 readings/hour × 20 bytes is trivial; 6 hours of 1 Hz data is not. Decide the policy when the buffer fills — drop oldest (usually right for telemetry, where recent data matters more) or drop newest (right when the first event after a fault is the important one). Say which and why.

Retry: exponential backoff with jitter. Without backoff a device retries continuously and destroys its battery. Without jitter, a thousand devices that all lost connectivity at the same moment reconnect simultaneously and take down the server — a thundering herd, and it is a real outage pattern.

Timestamps: buffered readings must carry the time they were taken, not the time they were sent. That requires a clock that survives, which means an RTC or at least a monotonic counter plus a resynchronisation on reconnect.

And degrade visibly: the device should indicate it is offline, and report the outage duration when it reconnects, so the gap in the data is explained rather than mysterious.

Listening forJitter on the backoff and timestamps at capture time — both are lessons learned from real deployments.
Why might you choose MQTT over HTTP for a telemetry device?Mid

Bytes and connection model. An MQTT publish has a header of a couple of bytes against HTTP's hundreds of bytes of headers per request — on a radio, bytes are energy, and for a 20-byte payload the overhead ratio matters enormously. MQTT also keeps one long-lived connection over which many messages flow, so you pay the TLS handshake once rather than per message.

The publish/subscribe model also fits telemetry: the device does not need to know who consumes its data, and the broker handles fan-out. QoS 1 gives you at-least-once delivery with acknowledgement, and retained messages let a newly-connected consumer see the last known state immediately.

The costs to acknowledge: a broker is infrastructure you must run and scale; a persistent connection costs standby energy and needs keep-alives; and for a device that reports once an hour, holding a connection open may be worse than a single CoAP or HTTP request. So the answer depends on message frequency — frequent messages favour a held connection, rare messages favour connectionless.

Listening forThe handshake amortisation argument, and noting that rare messages can invert the conclusion.
How do you choose a message format for a constrained link?Mid

By counting bytes, because bytes are energy and airtime. JSON is human-readable and typically 3–10× larger than a binary encoding of the same data — for a device sending 20 bytes of actual measurement, JSON's field names can be most of the payload.

The options: CBOR — a binary JSON-equivalent, self-describing, no schema needed, small libraries, and the natural pairing with CoAP. Protobuf — smaller still, schema-driven with generated code and good versioning semantics, at the cost of a build step and a larger library. A hand-rolled packed struct — smallest possible and fastest, but you own the versioning, endianness and alignment problems yourself, which is exactly where bugs live.

Whatever you choose, include a version or type field from day one. Devices in the field outlive the format, and the ability to add a field without breaking older readers is worth the byte it costs.

What is the energy cost of a BLE connection versus advertising?Senior

They are different shapes, and the crossover depends on how often you have something to say.

Advertising costs a short burst per interval — the device wakes, transmits on three channels, optionally listens briefly for a scan request, and sleeps. At a 1-second interval that is a few microamps average; at 100 ms it is tens. Nothing is maintained between bursts, and a connectionless advertisement can carry a small payload, so a beacon-style sensor never connects at all.

A connection costs a wake-up per connection interval for both peers, whether or not there is data — so a 30 ms interval is expensive and a 4-second interval is cheap. Slave latency lets the peripheral skip intervals when it has nothing to send, which recovers much of the cost while keeping responsiveness for the central.

The design consequence: for pure telemetry with no commands, connectionless advertising is usually cheapest and simplest. For bidirectional interaction, a connection with a long interval plus slave latency is the right shape. And the connection parameters are negotiable at runtime — a device can request a fast interval during a firmware transfer and a slow one afterwards, which is exactly what a well-designed product does.

Two thousand devices reconnect after a regional outage. What breaks?Senior

The server, usually — a thundering herd. Every device lost connectivity at the same moment, so their backoff timers are synchronised and they all reconnect together. Two thousand simultaneous TLS handshakes is a CPU spike on the broker; two thousand devices then uploading six hours of buffered data is a bandwidth and storage spike. The server degrades, connections fail, devices back off and retry — and because they are still synchronised, they do it together again. It can oscillate for hours.

The device-side fix is jitter: randomise the backoff (full jitter — a random value between 0 and the current backoff ceiling — rather than a fixed delay plus a small random) so reconnections spread out. Also rate-limit the backlog upload rather than dumping everything at once.

The server-side fixes: accept connections but shed load gracefully, prioritise new data over backlog, and have a way to tell devices to slow down (MQTT allows the broker to close connections; a protocol-level "come back in N minutes" is better).

The design lesson worth stating: a fleet is a distributed system, and behaviours that are harmless in one device are an outage at scale. This is the class of problem that only appears in production.

Listening forNaming the thundering herd, full jitter specifically, and recognising it as an emergent fleet property.
How would you test connectivity behaviour that only fails in the field?Senior

Make the field conditions reproducible on a bench. Concretely: a test rig that can cut the network at arbitrary moments (including mid-handshake and mid-upload), introduce latency and packet loss with tc netem, drop the link for hours, and return it — combined with power cycling the device at arbitrary points, which is exactly what the lab-bench rig from the Raspberry Pi track provides.

Then the scenarios worth automating: connect during a server outage; lose the link mid-upload and verify no data is lost or duplicated; run for days with a 5% packet loss; have the server return errors and malformed responses; let the certificate expire; change the device's IP mid-session; and fill the local buffer completely and confirm the drop policy behaves as designed.

Also instrument the device so field failures are diagnosable: connection attempt counters, failure reasons, time-since-last-success, and buffer occupancy, all reported as telemetry when it does connect. A device that cannot tell you why it was offline for six hours is one you will never debug.

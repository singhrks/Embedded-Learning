Thermal, mechanical and EMC constraints

The physical constraints are the ones firmware engineers most often treat as somebody else's problem, which is exactly why demonstrating awareness of them is a strong differentiator. They also cause bugs that look like software faults and consume weeks if you do not recognise them.

Thermal

THE ARITHMETIC, and it is simple enough to do in a design
round:

    T_junction = T_ambient + (Power x Rth)

  Rth is thermal resistance in degrees per watt, and it
  ADDS along the path:
    junction-to-case + case-to-heatsink + heatsink-to-air

  WORKED: a regulator dropping 3.7 V to 1.8 V at 200 mA
    P = (3.7 - 1.8) x 0.2 = 0.38 W
    SOT-23 package, Rth(j-a) about 250 C/W
    rise = 0.38 x 250 = 95 C
    at 40 C ambient -> 135 C junction. Over limit.

  So either a switching regulator, a bigger package, or
  copper area as a heatsink. THAT calculation, done early,
  prevents a board that works on a bench and fails in an
  enclosure.

WHAT FIRMWARE CAN DO ABOUT HEAT:
  - reduce clock or duty cycle when hot (and know that
    doing so changes your real-time analysis)
  - shed load: disable non-essential functions
  - move work in time: batch it, or run it when cooler
  - and MEASURE: an internal temperature sensor, logged,
    so field thermal behaviour is known rather than assumed

WHAT FIRMWARE MUST NOT DO:
  - assume the datasheet's maximum clock is achievable
    continuously in the product's enclosure
  - benchmark on an open bench and quote the number

TEMPERATURE ALSO AFFECTS THINGS PEOPLE FORGET:
  crystal frequency (ppm drift - matters for UART baud
  and for anything timed), battery capacity (much lower
  when cold) and internal resistance (much higher),
  leakage current (roughly doubles every 10 C - so sleep
  current at 60 C may be several times the datasheet
  figure at 25 C), and flash retention and endurance.
Sleep current at temperature is the trap

A design measured at 25 °C showing 2 µA can be 10–20 µA at 60 °C, because leakage roughly doubles every 10 °C. If the energy budget was built from a bench measurement at room temperature and the product lives in a loft or a vehicle, the battery life prediction can be wrong by a factor of several — and the failure appears months into deployment, in summer, in one geography. Measure sleep current across the specified temperature range, and use the worst case in the budget.

EMC, for firmware engineers

   TWO DIRECTIONS, and both can be affected by firmware
   decisions.

   EMISSIONS - what you radiate. Regulatory testing;
     failing it delays a product launch and the fixes at
     that stage are expensive.

     FIRMWARE LEVERS:
       - SLEW RATE on every GPIO. Set the slowest that
         meets timing. Fast edges radiate; this is free to
         change and often sufficient.
       - clock frequency choice and spread-spectrum
         clocking if the part supports it
       - avoid unnecessary switching: do not toggle pins
         you do not need to

   IMMUNITY - what disturbs you. ESD, surges, conducted
     noise, radiated fields.

     FIRMWARE LEVERS:
       - re-read and validate inputs; never act on a single
         sample of anything electrically exposed
       - CRC on every external interface
       - detect and recover: bus recovery, re-init a
         peripheral whose registers have been corrupted,
         watchdog as the backstop
       - periodically REWRITE configuration registers of
         external devices, because ESD can silently corrupt
         them. This is a real technique in industrial
         designs and worth knowing.

   THE FIRMWARE-VISIBLE SYMPTOMS OF AN EMC PROBLEM:
     - a bus that errors only when a motor runs
     - a GPIO interrupt that fires with no external event
     - a board that resets with no crash log and a reset
       cause of "pin reset" - a glitch on the reset line,
       not your software
     - corruption that correlates with a relay switching

   RECOGNISING THESE AS ELECTRICAL rather than software is
   worth days. The discriminating test is simple: stop the
   other activity and see whether the problem stops.

Questions

Why does a design that works on the bench overheat in its enclosure?Mid

Because the bench has free convection and the enclosure does not. Junction temperature is ambient plus power times thermal resistance, and sealing the board raises the effective ambient inside the box while removing the airflow that carried heat away. A sealed IP-rated enclosure is the worst case — no convection at all, so heat leaves only by conduction through the case.

The consequence for firmware: the part throttles, the clock drops, and your real-time analysis — computed at the nominal clock — is no longer valid. A control loop that met its deadline at 168 MHz may not at 120 MHz.

What I would do: compute the thermal path early rather than measuring it late; measure internal temperature in the product, not on the bench, and log it; design the timing budget at the throttled clock rather than the nominal one; and give firmware a defined behaviour when hot — shed non-essential load in a controlled way rather than letting the hardware throttle arbitrarily.

Listening forThe link from thermal throttling back to the real-time analysis — that is the firmware-relevant consequence.
How does temperature affect a battery life calculation?Mid

In three ways, all of which make cold or hot conditions worse than a bench measurement suggests.

Leakage roughly doubles every 10 °C, so a sleep current of 2 µA at 25 °C can be 10–20 µA at 60 °C. In a duty-cycled design where sleep dominates, that alone can multiply the average current severalfold.

Battery capacity falls when cold — a lithium cell can deliver substantially less of its rated capacity at −20 °C — and internal resistance rises, which worsens the voltage sag under a transmit pulse and can push the rail below the brown-out threshold. So the device may reset when transmitting in winter and work fine indoors.

Self-discharge rises with temperature, which over a ten-year life can be a large fraction of the total capacity.

The practical response: measure current across the specified temperature range and build the budget from the worst case, not from a 25 °C bench figure; and validate at the extremes rather than extrapolating.

Listening forAll three effects, and using the worst case in the budget rather than the nominal.
What can firmware do to help a product pass emissions testing?Mid

More than people expect, and the changes are cheap.

Slew rate: set every GPIO to the slowest setting that meets its timing requirement. Fast edges contain energy at high frequencies — the knee is roughly 0.5 divided by the rise time, so a 1 ns edge radiates up to hundreds of megahertz regardless of how slow the signal is. Setting everything to maximum speed "to be safe" is a common cause of failing emissions, and it costs nothing to fix.

Clock choices: enable spread-spectrum clocking if the part supports it, which smears a narrow peak across a band and can be the difference between passing and failing. Avoid clock frequencies whose harmonics land in a band you must be quiet in.

Reduce unnecessary switching: do not toggle pins you do not need to, disable unused peripherals and their clocks, and avoid high-frequency polling where an interrupt would do.

The honest limit: firmware can help at the margin. If the layout has a split ground plane under a fast trace or a poorly-terminated bus, no firmware change will rescue it — but arriving at the EMC lab having already done the free things is worth a great deal, because time in the chamber is expensive.

Listening forSlew rate first, and honesty that firmware cannot rescue a layout problem.
A bus errors only when a motor is running. How do you approach it?Mid

Treat it as electrical until proven otherwise, because the correlation is the diagnosis. The single discriminating experiment: stop the motor and see whether the errors stop — and then vary the motor's load and speed to see whether the error rate tracks it.

The likely mechanisms: conducted noise through the shared supply (the motor's current pulses modulating the rail); coupled noise into signal lines running near the motor wiring; ground bounce from the return current flowing under the bus traces; or radiated pickup on a long unshielded run.

What firmware can do: enable the peripheral's digital noise filter if it has one (I2C's DNF, CAN's sample point placement); lower the bus speed, which widens timing margin; add a CRC and a retry so a corrupted transaction is detected and repeated rather than acted upon; count the errors so the rate is visible; and avoid running the bus at all during the noisiest phases if the schedule allows.

What actually fixes it is usually hardware — better grounding, separating the motor return path from signal returns, shielding, or a differential bus — so the firmware measures should be understood as mitigation while the real fix is scoped, and I would say so explicitly rather than declaring the problem solved.

A board resets occasionally with no crash log. What are your hypotheses?Senior

The absence of a crash log is the clue: a fault handler would have written one, so this is probably not a software fault at all.

Read the reset cause register first — it distinguishes a watchdog reset, a brown-out reset, a pin reset and a software reset, and each points somewhere completely different. A brown-out reset means the supply sagged: check the peak current draw against the supply and cable, particularly if it correlates with a radio transmission or a motor start. A pin reset with no one pressing anything means a glitch on the reset line — ESD, coupled noise, or a missing pull-up and capacitor. A watchdog reset means the software hung, which is a different investigation. A reset cause of power-on when the device was not power-cycled suggests the supply collapsed entirely.

Then correlate across the fleet and against the environment: time of day, temperature, whether a nearby machine is running, which batch the units come from.

And the design lesson: if the reset cause is not being recorded at every boot into non-volatile memory, that is the first fix, because without it this investigation has no starting point.

Listening forReading the reset cause register as the first step, and knowing what each cause implies.
How does an IP-rated sealed enclosure change your design?Senior

Several ways that reach well beyond mechanical design.

Thermal: no convection, so heat leaves only by conduction through the case. The power budget tightens substantially and the internal ambient rises, which in turn raises leakage and reduces battery capacity.

Connectors: there may be none, so provisioning, firmware update and diagnostics must work without physical access — wirelessly, or through pads used before final assembly. That makes the OTA path a hard requirement rather than a convenience, and it makes a bad update unrecoverable without opening a sealed unit.

Radio: if the enclosure is metal it blocks RF entirely, so you need an external antenna, a plastic window, or a different connectivity approach — and that decision must be made before the enclosure is tooled.

Servicing: if it cannot be opened, the battery is not replaceable, so the life requirement is absolute rather than a target. Pressure: a sealed box with temperature cycling develops a pressure differential, which stresses the seal and can draw in moisture over time — vents with membranes exist for this.

The firmware consequence I would emphasise: the recovery story. A sealed, unopenable device with a single update path needs that path to be exceptionally robust — A/B with confirmation, staged rollout, and an independent fallback.

Listening forThe recovery consequence, which is the firmware-relevant one and the one most often missed.
What is ESD and why should a firmware engineer care?Mid

Electrostatic discharge: a sudden transfer of charge — a person touching a connector can deliver thousands of volts in nanoseconds. Hardware defends with protection diodes, series resistance and layout, but the energy that gets through can still disturb a running system without destroying it.

The firmware-visible effects: a spurious interrupt, a corrupted register in an external peripheral, a bus locked up, a reset with no crash log, or a single corrupted value in RAM. Crucially the device is undamaged and the fault is transient, so it presents as a mysterious intermittent bug rather than a hardware failure.

What firmware can do about it: validate inputs rather than trusting a single reading; CRC anything crossing an external interface; implement bus recovery (I2C in particular); periodically rewrite the configuration registers of external devices so a silently corrupted setting is corrected; keep a watchdog as the backstop; and count these events so their rate is visible rather than being dismissed as noise.

That periodic-rewrite technique is worth knowing — it is standard practice in industrial designs and it surprises people who have only worked on benign environments.

Listening forPeriodic register refresh as a concrete firmware mitigation.
How do you choose an operating temperature range?Mid

From where the product actually lives, plus margin, and then check what that costs. Commercial grade is typically 0 to +70 °C, industrial −40 to +85 °C, automotive −40 to +125 °C, and each step up narrows part availability and raises cost.

The mistake is to specify the enclosure's external environment and forget the internal rise: a device in a 40 °C loft with 15 °C of self-heating inside a sealed box needs parts rated well above 55 °C, and the components must be rated for the junction temperature, not the ambient.

What usually limits the range: batteries first — lithium chemistries have narrow charging temperature windows in particular — then displays (LCDs freeze and become slow), then electrolytic capacitors, then crystals. The MCU is rarely the constraint.

And the firmware consequences: crystal drift across the range affects baud rates and timing; leakage affects the energy budget; and behaviour at the extremes needs testing rather than assuming, which means an environmental chamber and time in the schedule.

Listening forInternal rise on top of external ambient, and knowing that batteries and displays usually bind before the MCU.
Where would you place a temperature sensor, and what would you do with the reading?Senior

It depends what you are protecting, and the answer is usually more than one. The MCU's internal sensor tells you about the die and is essentially free, but it is poorly calibrated and reads the processor rather than the product. A sensor near the hottest external component — a regulator, a power stage, a radio — tells you about the thermal path that will actually fail. A sensor near the battery matters because charging outside the safe window is a safety issue, not just a performance one. And a sensor away from all heat sources gives you something close to true ambient, if the product needs to report that.

What to do with the readings: log them so field thermal behaviour is known rather than assumed; apply graceful degradation before the hardware throttles arbitrarily — reduce duty cycle, disable non-essential functions, reduce transmit power — so the behaviour under heat is designed rather than emergent; refuse to charge outside the battery's window; and report a thermal fault if a reading is implausible, since a sensor stuck at a comfortable value is the dangerous failure mode.

The design point worth making: measuring temperature is cheap and being surprised by it in the field is expensive, so instrument generously even if the current design does not obviously need it.

Listening forMultiple sensors for different purposes, and designed degradation ahead of hardware throttling.
What physical constraint most often surprises a firmware engineer?Senior

That the specified performance is not continuously available. Datasheet clock speeds, radio transmit powers and current draws are achievable in isolation on a bench and often not sustainable in a sealed product at the top of its temperature range — so the real-time analysis, the energy budget and the throughput figures must all be computed at the degraded operating point rather than the nominal one.

The second is how much the environment moves things that seem constant: crystals drift with temperature and break a marginal baud rate; battery capacity and internal resistance change enough to cause resets that only happen in winter; leakage changes sleep current by an order of magnitude across a temperature range; and mechanical stress on a connector produces intermittent faults that look exactly like software races.

The habit that follows: when an intermittent fault correlates with anything physical — time of day, temperature, another machine running, a specific unit or batch — stop debugging the software and go and measure. Recognising the pattern early is worth days, and it is the thing that distinguishes an engineer who has worked with hardware from one who has worked around it.

Listening forDesigning at the degraded operating point, and the instinct to correlate intermittent faults with physical variables.

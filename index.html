The twenty-five tracks

What a system-on-chip is

Nearly everything on this site runs on a SoC — a single piece of silicon holding not just processor cores but the memory controller, the graphics and AI engines, the camera pipeline, the interrupt controller, the security blocks and dozens of peripherals. A desktop PC spreads those across a motherboard. A phone, a car ECU, a router and a smart camera put them on one die, a few millimetres square.

That integration is not merely tidier. It is the reason the field looks the way it does:

Consequence of integrationWhat it forces on you
Wires on a die are thousands of times cheaper in power than wires on a boardBandwidth that would be impossible off-chip, and thermal limits that arrive far sooner
Dozens of blocks share one memory systemCoherency, quality-of-service and contention become your problem, not the hardware's
Every block is a bus master that can read and write DRAM by itselfMemory protection must exist in the interconnect, not only in the CPU — this is why an SMMU exists
The chip is fixed at manufacture; bugs cost a mask setErrata are worked around in firmware and drivers. A large part of systems work is compensating for silicon nobody can change
One vendor integrates blocks licensed from many othersDocumentation is uneven, and the authoritative answer is often a register description rather than a manual
The interconnect is the chip

Beginners picture a SoC as "a CPU with peripherals attached". It is closer to a network with a CPU as one of its nodes. The GPU, the NPU, the ISP and every DMA engine issue their own memory transactions, compete for the same DRAM, and can corrupt anything they are allowed to reach. Once that clicks, the existence of the SMMU, of cache-coherency protocols, of QoS registers and of memory attributes stops feeling arbitrary.

How a chip is made

Two long pipelines sit behind every SoC. The first turns an idea into a set of photographic masks; the second turns those masks into silicon. Together they take two to four years and hundreds of millions of dollars, which is why the errata document is thicker than you expect.

Why this matters to a driver author

Because silicon cannot be patched. When a block misbehaves — a DMA engine that ignores a coherency hint, a controller that needs a dummy read after a register write, an interrupt that fires spuriously at reset — the fix lands in firmware or in your driver, and it stays there for the life of the part. Reading an errata sheet and recognising your own bug in it is an ordinary Tuesday, and it makes far more sense once you know how little freedom the hardware team had after tapeout.

Where to start

  1. Read the prerequisites honestly. If C pointers are shaky, the C track comes before everything else. There is no way around this.
  2. Follow the roadmap for a week-by-week path with realistic time costs.
  3. Set up your workbench — a cross-compiler, QEMU, and one Linux and/or Windows VM. Every project on this site runs without buying hardware.
  4. Then begin at computer architecture, chapter 01, or jump to whichever track your job needs and backfill when you get stuck.

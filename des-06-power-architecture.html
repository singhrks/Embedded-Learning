Power architecture and the energy budget

For a battery device the energy budget is not one consideration among many — it is the design. It decides the processor, the radio, the sample rate and the software architecture, and it is arithmetic you must be able to do out loud in ninety seconds.

The arithmetic

average current = SUM(current_i x time_i) / period
battery life    = usable capacity / average current

WORKED, an environmental sensor reporting every minute:

  phase        time      current     charge
  ------------------------------------------------
  sleep        59.79 s     3 uA      179.4 uA-s
  wake+ADC      0.05 s     6 mA      300   uA-s
  compute       0.01 s     8 mA       80   uA-s
  radio TX      0.15 s    18 mA     2700   uA-s
  ------------------------------------------------
  per 60 s                          3259.4 uA-s
  average = 3259.4 / 60 = 54.3 uA

  CR2032, 220 mAh, usable maybe 180 mAh at this load:
    180 mAh / 0.0543 mA = 3315 h = 138 days

  A ONE-YEAR TARGET NEEDS ~20 uA, so we are 2.7x out.

WHERE THE ENERGY ACTUALLY GOES:
    radio    2700 / 3259 = 83%
    ADC       300 / 3259 =  9%
    compute    80 / 3259 =  2%
    sleep     179 / 3259 =  6%

  THE RADIO IS THE DESIGN. Optimising sleep current from
  3 uA to 1 uA saves 4% and cannot get you to a year.
  Reporting every 5 minutes instead of every minute cuts
  the radio term by 5x and gets you there immediately.

  THIS IS THE HABIT THAT MATTERS: compute the percentages,
  then attack the largest term. Candidates who start
  optimising sleep current without looking have not read
  their own numbers.
Two competing intuitions, and when each applies

"Sleep current dominates" and "the radio dominates" are both true, of different designs. In a very low duty cycle system — waking once an hour — sleep is 99.9% of the time and a few microamps becomes the whole budget. In a moderately active system like the one above, the active phases dominate despite being brief. The only way to know which you have is to compute the percentages, which takes a minute and decides where all your effort should go.

   THE PEAK CURRENT TRAP, which catches people who only
   budget average current:

   A CR2032 has roughly 10-15 ohms of internal resistance
   near end of life. A 20 mA radio transmit pulse then
   drops:

     V = I x R = 0.020 x 15 = 0.3 V

   From 2.9 V that is 2.6 V - possibly below your MCU's
   brown-out threshold, so the device RESETS every time it
   transmits, and only when the battery is half used.

   THE FIX: a bulk capacitor (typically 100 uF or more)
   across the cell, charged slowly between transmissions
   and supplying the pulse. This is a schematic decision
   driven entirely by a firmware behaviour, and noticing
   the interaction is a strong signal.

   ALWAYS BUDGET BOTH:
     average current -> battery life
     peak current    -> whether it works at all

Power architecture

THE DECISIONS, and what each buys:

  LDO vs SWITCHING REGULATOR
    LDO: cheap, quiet, simple; wastes (Vin-Vout) x I as
         heat. From 3.7 V to 1.8 V an LDO is 49% efficient.
    Buck: 85-95% efficient, costs an inductor and creates
         switching noise. Below a few mA the switcher's own
         quiescent current can make it WORSE than an LDO -
         so the crossover matters.

  HOW MANY RAILS
    Each rail costs a regulator, board area and quiescent
    current. But rails you can SWITCH OFF are how you get
    sleep current down.

  POWER GATING
    A load switch or MOSFET in a peripheral's supply.
    Essential when a sensor's own sleep current exceeds
    your entire budget - many do. It also gives you a hard
    reset for a device that has locked up (rpi chapter 07's
    bench, and I2C bus recovery).

  THE SEQUENCING RULE
    Many parts specify a rail order and a maximum voltage
    difference between rails during ramp. Violating it can
    latch up the part on every power-on. This is a small
    note in the datasheet and a real cause of "one board in
    fifty does not start".

  BROWN-OUT DETECTION
    Set the threshold above the voltage at which flash
    writes become unreliable, not just above where the core
    stops. A write in progress during a sag is how
    filesystems get corrupted.

Questions

A device wakes every minute: 50 ms at 6 mA sensing, 150 ms at 18 mA transmitting, 3 µA asleep. Battery life on a CR2032, and what would you change?Mid

Per 60 s: sleep 59.8 s × 3 µA ≈ 179 µA·s; sense 0.05 × 6000 = 300 µA·s; transmit 0.15 × 18000 = 2700 µA·s. Total ≈ 3179 µA·s, average ≈ 53 µA. With ~180 mAh usable that is about 3400 hours ≈ 140 days.

The breakdown is the answer: the radio is 85% of the budget. So the changes, in order of effect: report less often (every 5 minutes is a 5× cut on the dominant term and roughly gets you to a year); batch several readings per transmission so you still sample every minute but transmit rarely; reduce transmit power or use a more efficient protocol; shorten the connection interval negotiation so the radio is on for less time.

What I would not do first: chase sleep current, which is 6% of the total.

Listening forThe percentages, then attacking the largest term. This is the whole skill.
  • Follow-up: "Marketing insists on one-minute reporting." — then the radio itself or the battery must change: a bigger cell, a lower-energy radio mode, or accept a shorter life and say so explicitly.
Your design meets its average current budget but resets when the radio transmits. What is happening?Senior

Peak current against the battery's internal resistance. A coin cell has 10–15 Ω near end of life, so a 20 mA transmit pulse drops 0.2–0.3 V. From an already-depleted 2.9 V that can take the rail below the brown-out threshold, so the device resets — and only once the battery is partly used, which is why it passes bench testing and fails in the field months later.

The fix is a bulk capacitor across the cell, sized to supply the pulse: for 20 mA over 150 ms with an acceptable 100 mV droop, C = I·t/ΔV = 0.02 × 0.15 / 0.1 = 30 mF, which is impractical — so in practice you either shorten the transmit burst, accept more droop, use a supercapacitor, or choose a cell chemistry with lower internal resistance.

The general lesson: budget peak current as well as average. Average tells you battery life; peak tells you whether it works at all.

Listening forInternal resistance as the mechanism, and the recognition that it worsens as the cell depletes.
Where do unexpected microamps come from in sleep?Mid

In order of how often it is the culprit: a debugger attached — SWD keeps clocks alive, so measurements with a probe connected are often 100× too high and meaningless. A floating input pin — an unconnected CMOS input drifts near the switching threshold and both transistors conduct; every unused pin must be an output, or an input with a pull enabled, or configured as analog (which is lowest because it disconnects the Schmitt trigger). A pull-up fighting a driven-low line — 4.7 kΩ to 3.3 V held low is 700 µA continuously. A peripheral clock left enabled. An LED. An external sensor whose own sleep current exceeds your whole budget. The regulator's quiescent current, which can cap what you can achieve before firmware runs at all.

And the method: measure, do not model. You need an ammeter resolving microamps and milliamps in one trace, with the debugger detached and running from the real battery. Plot current against time across a full duty cycle and the unexpected consumption appears as a visible plateau you can align with your code.

Listening forThe debugger point first — it is the most common reason a measurement is wrong.
LDO or switching regulator?Mid

Compute the loss. An LDO dissipates (Vin − Vout) × I as heat, so from 3.7 V to 1.8 V it is 49% efficient — at 100 mA that is 190 mW wasted, which is both battery life and a thermal problem. A buck converter is 85–95% efficient and costs an inductor, more board area, and switching noise that can couple into analog circuits.

The crossover people miss: at very low load a switcher's own quiescent current can exceed the LDO's loss. A switcher drawing 30 µA quiescent is worse than an LDO for a system idling at 10 µA. Many designs therefore use both — a switcher for the active phase, an LDO or a low-quiescent switcher mode for sleep.

Other reasons to prefer an LDO: powering an ADC reference or an RF section, where switching ripple directly degrades performance, and small voltage drops where efficiency is already high.

Listening forThe low-load crossover, which distinguishes a considered answer from a memorised one.
How do you design so a device survives power being cut at any instant?Senior

Assume it will happen mid-write, because it will. Concretely: make persistent writes atomic — write to a temporary location then rename, or use a two-slot scheme with a sequence number and a checksum where the reader picks the newest valid slot. Never update a record in place.

Then reduce the exposure: keep the filesystem read-only in normal operation so there is nothing to corrupt, and put the small amount of mutable state on a separate partition mounted with sync. Use a brown-out detector set above the voltage where flash writes become unreliable — not merely above where the core stops — so the part is held in reset before it can perform a marginal write.

For a firmware update, use A/B slots with a confirmation step, so a power cut at any point leaves a bootable image.

And then test it: pull the plug a hundred times under load, ideally automated on a bench rig. Products that survive this were designed for it; products that were not fail in the field at a rate that looks like bad hardware.

Listening forAtomic rename or two-slot journalling, and the brown-out threshold being set for flash rather than for the core.
When is power gating a peripheral worth the extra part?Mid

When the peripheral's own sleep current is comparable to or larger than your whole system budget — which is common. Many sensors advertise "low power" at tens of microamps asleep, against an MCU budget of two or three. A load switch costing a few cents takes that to genuinely zero.

Two secondary benefits worth mentioning. It gives you a hard reset for a device that has locked up — an I2C sensor holding SDA low can only be recovered by power-cycling it, and without a gate that means an unrecoverable field failure. And it removes the sensor's contribution to leakage paths during deep sleep.

The design detail that catches people: if signal lines to the gated device stay connected, the device can be back-powered through its ESD protection diodes and never fully power down. So either switch the high side, add series resistors in the signal lines, or drive those lines low before removing power.

Listening forBack-powering — it is the failure that makes a naive power gate not work.
What does a sleep mode actually cost you when you wake?Mid

Time and energy, and both must be in the budget. Deeper modes save more current and cost more to leave: a light sleep may resume in a cycle or two; a stop mode takes microseconds plus a PLL relock; a standby mode loses RAM entirely, so waking is a full reboot — hundreds of microseconds at full run current before your code executes, plus re-initialising everything.

The consequence: for a short duty cycle, a shallower sleep can give a lower average current than a deeper one, because you spend less time paying the wake penalty. The crossover is often around a one-second period, and it is worth computing rather than assuming deeper is better.

The software consequence of standby specifically is architectural: since RAM is lost, anything that must survive goes in backup registers or the RTC domain, and your program must be written to resume from a reboot rather than continue.

Listening forThe crossover — that deeper is not automatically better — and the architectural implication of losing RAM.
How would you verify a claimed ten-year battery life before shipping?Senior

You cannot test it directly, so you build the number from measured parts and then defend the model.

Measure each phase's current and duration on real hardware with a proper analyser — not the datasheet figures, which are typical values under conditions that are not yours. Measure across the temperature range, because leakage roughly doubles every 10 °C and a design that works at 20 °C can miss badly at 60 °C. Measure at end-of-life battery voltage, where efficiency and internal resistance are worse.

Then compute average current, apply the battery's usable capacity at your load and temperature (not the headline figure), and add the self-discharge rate — which over ten years can be a large fraction of the total and is routinely forgotten.

Then validate the model: run an accelerated test at a much higher duty cycle, confirm the measured average current matches prediction, and run a long soak at the real duty cycle to catch behaviours the model misses — a retry storm when the network is unavailable, a sensor that occasionally fails to enter sleep, a log that grows.

Listening forSelf-discharge and temperature, plus the idea of validating the model rather than trusting it.
The device must run from either mains or battery. How does that change the design?Mid

You now have two operating modes with different rules, and the software must know which it is in. On mains you can run continuously, keep the radio connected, sample fast and skip aggressive sleep. On battery you duty cycle hard and shed non-essential function.

Hardware: a power-path controller or an ORing arrangement that selects the higher source without letting current flow backwards into the battery, plus charge management if the battery is rechargeable. The MCU needs a way to sense which source is present — a GPIO from the power path — and to measure battery voltage through an ADC with a divider that can itself be switched off so it does not drain the cell.

The behaviours to design: a clean transition when mains is lost, including finishing or abandoning any flash write in progress; a low-battery policy with defined thresholds (reduce function, then warn, then safe shutdown); and not letting the device thrash between modes when the supply is marginal — hysteresis on the thresholds.

Listening forHysteresis and the divider that must be switchable — both are details you only know from having built one.
What is the single most effective thing you can do to reduce average power in firmware?Junior

Make sure the CPU is actually asleep whenever it has nothing to do — which means an event-driven architecture with no polling anywhere. A single loop somewhere spinning on a flag keeps the core at full current and defeats every other optimisation.

Concretely: every wait must block on an interrupt or an RTOS primitive, never a busy loop; the idle task executes WFI; use DMA so transfers complete without the CPU; use hardware peripherals for timing rather than delay loops; and use tickless idle so a periodic tick does not wake the core needlessly hundreds of times a second.

Then verify with an ammeter rather than by reading the code — "tickless is enabled" and "tickless is working" differ by a factor of a hundred in average current, and only a current trace tells you which you have.

Listening for"No polling anywhere" as the organising principle, and verifying by measurement.

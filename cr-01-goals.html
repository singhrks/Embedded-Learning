What cryptography guarantees

Four properties, and the precise threat model each defends. Most cryptographic failures in real systems come not from broken algorithms but from expecting a property the primitive never provided — so this chapter is about being exact.

The four goals

Cryptography is not one thing, and "encrypt it" is not a requirement. There are four distinct properties a system might need, they are provided by different primitives, and getting the wrong one is how systems end up encrypted and still broken.

   CONFIDENTIALITY   an adversary cannot learn the content
                     -> encryption

   INTEGRITY         an adversary cannot modify the message
                     undetected
                     -> MACs, signatures

   AUTHENTICITY      the message came from who it claims
                     -> MACs (shared key), signatures
                        (public key)

   NON-REPUDIATION   the sender cannot later deny it
                     -> signatures ONLY, never MACs

   AND THE ONES CRYPTOGRAPHY DOES NOT PROVIDE:
     availability      - a jammer, a flood, a cut cable
     traffic analysis  - who talked to whom, when, how much
     endpoint security - a compromised device signs whatever
                         it is told to
     correctness of the plaintext
     protection against a user who chooses to leak it

   THE DISTINCTION THAT MATTERS MOST IN PRACTICE:

     A MAC proves the message came from someone holding the
     shared key. Since BOTH parties hold it, EITHER could
     have produced it - so a MAC cannot prove to a third
     party who sent it.

     A SIGNATURE proves it came from the holder of the
     private key, and only they hold it - so a third party
     can verify it, and the signer cannot deny it.

   Choosing a MAC where non-repudiation was needed, or a
   signature where a MAC would have been faster, is a design
   error that no amount of correct implementation fixes.

Encryption alone is not enough

A short list of rules that hold without exception. Every one of them has been violated in a shipped product by a competent engineer who thought their case was special, which is why they are stated as absolutes.

/* THE SINGLE MOST IMPORTANT RULE IN THIS TRACK:

     ENCRYPTION WITHOUT AUTHENTICATION IS BROKEN.

   Confidentiality does not imply integrity. An attacker who
   cannot READ your ciphertext can very often MODIFY it in
   ways that produce a predictable change in the plaintext.  */

/* --- THE BIT-FLIP ATTACK on a stream cipher --- */
        plaintext:   "TRANSFER 0100 TO ACCOUNT 12345"
        keystream:   k0 k1 k2 ...
        ciphertext:  p XOR k

/* The attacker does not know the key. But XOR is linear, so
   flipping bit i of the ciphertext flips bit i of the
   plaintext. They change "0100" to "9100" by XORing the
   right positions with a known delta - WITHOUT DECRYPTING
   ANYTHING.

   This applies to every stream cipher, and to CTR, OFB and
   CFB modes (chapter 05).

/* --- THE PADDING ORACLE on CBC --- */
/* If the receiver distinguishes "bad padding" from "bad
   MAC" - by an error message, a different response, or
   merely a TIMING DIFFERENCE - an attacker can decrypt the
   entire message by submitting modified ciphertexts and
   observing which are accepted. About 128 queries per byte.

   This broke TLS (Lucky 13), ASP.NET, XML encryption, and
   many devices. It is why the answer is not "fix the error
   messages" but "use authenticated encryption".

/* THE FIX, and it is the same in every case:

     USE AN AEAD (chapter 09): AES-GCM, ChaCha20-Poly1305,
     AES-CCM. They encrypt and authenticate together, in a
     construction that has been analysed as a whole.

   NEVER: encrypt then hope
   NEVER: encrypt-and-MAC or MAC-then-encrypt hand-rolled
   ALWAYS: an AEAD, or if you must compose, ENCRYPT-THEN-MAC
           over the ciphertext AND the IV AND the associated
           data.                                            */
The rule that prevents most failures

Do not design cryptography. Do not implement primitives. Use a reviewed library, at the highest level of abstraction it offers.

Every chapter in this track explains how something works so you can reason about it, choose correctly, and read a security review. None of it is an invitation to write your own AES. The gap between "understanding the algorithm" and "implementing it without a side channel" is where the failures are (chapter 19).

Kerckhoffs's principle

The security of a system must rest entirely on the key, never on the design being secret. This is not idealism about openness — it is a practical observation about what actually stays secret once a product ships.

/* THE SYSTEM MUST BE SECURE EVEN IF EVERYTHING ABOUT IT IS
   PUBLIC EXCEPT THE KEY.

   Stated in 1883, and still the property that separates
   cryptography from wishful thinking.

/* WHY: algorithms leak. They are reverse-engineered from
   firmware, extracted from silicon, leaked by employees,
   published by researchers. A system whose security depends
   on the algorithm staying secret has a single point of
   failure that cannot be rotated.

/* THE CASE STUDIES, all of them real:
     · A5/1 (GSM)         - reverse-engineered, then broken
     · CSS (DVD)          - reverse-engineered, 40-bit key
     · MIFARE Classic     - proprietary CRYPTO1, broken in
                            minutes
     · KeeLoq (car remotes) - broken once published
     · numerous vendor "encryption" schemes in IoT devices

   In every case the algorithm was weak, and secrecy delayed
   the discovery rather than preventing it.

/* THE CORRECT POSTURE:
     · use PUBLISHED, ANALYSED algorithms - AES, SHA-2,
       SHA-3, ChaCha20, Ed25519, ML-KEM
     · assume the attacker has your firmware and your
       schematics
     · put the secret in the KEY, and protect the key
       (chapter 18)
     · design so a compromised device does not compromise the
       fleet - per-device keys, not one key everywhere

/* AND THE COROLLARY that people miss: "we obfuscated it" is
   not a security control. It raises the cost of the first
   break and does nothing thereafter, because the result is
   published.                                              */

Threat models

"Is this secure?" has no answer. "Is this secure against an attacker who can do X?" does. Writing down what you are defending against is the step that makes every later decision answerable.

/* A guarantee is meaningless without saying against WHOM.
   The standard adversary models, in increasing strength: */

   CIPHERTEXT-ONLY       sees ciphertexts
   KNOWN-PLAINTEXT       sees some plaintext/ciphertext pairs
   CHOSEN-PLAINTEXT      can get any plaintext encrypted
                         (CPA)
   CHOSEN-CIPHERTEXT     can also get chosen ciphertexts
                         DECRYPTED (CCA)
   ADAPTIVE CHOSEN-      can choose based on previous
   CIPHERTEXT (CCA2)     answers

/* MODERN SCHEMES TARGET CCA2, because that is what a network
   attacker actually has: they can send whatever they like to
   your server and observe the response.

   IND-CPA   "indistinguishable under chosen plaintext" - an
             attacker cannot tell which of two chosen
             plaintexts was encrypted. Requires
             RANDOMISATION: the same plaintext must not
             produce the same ciphertext, which is why every
             mode needs an IV or nonce.
   IND-CCA2  the same, with a decryption oracle available.
             AEADs provide this; unauthenticated modes do
             not.

/* AND THE PHYSICAL MODELS, which matter for embedded: */
   PASSIVE REMOTE        network observation only
   ACTIVE REMOTE         can inject and modify
   LOCAL SOFTWARE        code on the same machine - cache
                         timing (chapter 19)
   PHYSICAL, NON-        power and EM measurement
   INVASIVE
   PHYSICAL, INVASIVE    decapsulation, probing, laser fault
                         injection

/* THE EMBEDDED QUESTION: your threat model must state where
   on that scale you stop. "Resists a remote attacker" and
   "resists an attacker holding the device with an
   oscilloscope" are entirely different products
   (isolation chapter 16), and pretending otherwise is how
   security reviews go badly.                              */

Key sizes and security levels

Key sizes are quoted constantly and compared wrongly — a 256-bit symmetric key and a 256-bit elliptic-curve key offer similar security, while a 256-bit RSA key offers essentially none. The unifying measure is the security level in bits.

128 bits — the baselineAES-1283072256SHA-256

/* READ THE TABLE ROW-WISE: those entries are EQUIVALENT.
   AES-128, RSA-3072 and P-256 all give about 128 bits of
   security. Pairing AES-256 with RSA-2048 wastes the AES
   strength; the system is as strong as its weakest link.

/* WHAT "128 BITS" MEANS: roughly 2^128 operations to break.
   That is not merely large - it is beyond any conceivable
   physical computation. Counting to 2^128 at one operation
   per Planck time, using the entire energy output of the
   sun, does not finish.

/* SO KEY SEARCH IS NOT THE THREAT. Real breaks come from:
     · implementation bugs
     · side channels (chapter 19)
     · key management failures (chapter 18)
     · protocol errors
     · bad randomness (chapter 02)
     · using a broken primitive after it was broken

/* THE ASYMMETRIC SIZES ARE LARGER because the attacks are
   better than brute force: the number field sieve for
   RSA/DH factoring and discrete log, Pollard's rho for
   elliptic curves. ECC needs only 2n bits for n bits of
   security, which is why it dominates on embedded
   (chapter 14).

/* AND THE QUANTUM CAVEAT (chapter 20):
     Grover's algorithm halves symmetric security - AES-128
     becomes ~64 bits of quantum security, which is why
     AES-256 is recommended for long-term secrets.
     Shor's algorithm BREAKS RSA, DH and ECC entirely. The
     replacements are standardised (ML-KEM, ML-DSA) and
     deployment has begun.                                */

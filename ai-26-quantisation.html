Quantisation and efficient inference

Representing weights in 8 or 4 bits instead of 16. This is fixed-point arithmetic, scale factors and dynamic range — territory an embedded engineer already knows, applied to a new problem.

Why it works

Dropping from sixteen bits to four sounds like it should destroy a model and largely does not. Why it works is worth understanding, because it tells you when it will not.

   THE MOTIVATION IS BANDWIDTH, NOT ARITHMETIC
   (chapters 04, 25).

     70B model in fp16   140 GB   -> ~14 tokens/s at
                                     2 TB/s
     the same in int8     70 GB   -> ~28 tokens/s
     the same in int4     35 GB   -> ~56 tokens/s

   THE SPEEDUP IS PROPORTIONAL TO THE BYTE REDUCTION,
   because generation reads every weight once per token and
   the multipliers are idle anyway. It also determines
   whether the model FITS AT ALL - which for a device is
   usually the real question.

   AND WHY IT DOES NOT DESTROY THE MODEL:

     · WEIGHTS ARE ROUGHLY GAUSSIAN and tightly clustered
       around zero. A well-chosen scale covers the range
       with little error.
     · NEURAL NETWORKS ARE ROBUST TO NOISE. They were
       trained with dropout, augmentation and stochastic
       gradients; small perturbations to weights behave
       like more of the same.
     · THE ERROR AVERAGES OUT. A dot product over 4096
       terms sums many independent rounding errors, which
       partially cancel (chapter 08).
     · AND THE INFORMATION IS REDUNDANT. Superposition
       (chapter 17) means features are spread across many
       weights, so no single weight is critical.

   THE FORMATS, and note what varies:

     FORMAT   BITS   RANGE           WHERE USED
     fp32       32   +-3.4e38        training master
                                     weights
     fp16       16   +-65504         older training, some
                                     inference
     bf16       16   +-3.4e38        MODERN TRAINING -
                                     fp32's range, fewer
                                     mantissa bits, so NO
                                     LOSS SCALING NEEDED
     fp8         8   two variants    newest accelerators
                     (E4M3, E5M2)
     int8        8   -128..127       THE EMBEDDED WORKHORSE
     int4        4   -8..7           LLM weights
     int2/
     ternary   2-1.6 {-1,0,1}        research, and
                                     BitNet-style models

   THE PATTERN: FEWER BITS MEANS LESS RANGE OR LESS
   PRECISION, AND THE SCALE FACTOR IS WHAT BUYS THE RANGE
   BACK.

The arithmetic

The arithmetic is simple, and the details of where the scale factors live are what separate a method that holds accuracy from one that does not.

/* --- THE BASIC MAPPING --- */

    QUANTISE:    q = round(x / scale) + zero_point
    DEQUANTISE:  x = (q - zero_point) * scale

/* SYMMETRIC (zero_point = 0): */
    scale = max(|x|) / 127                # int8
    q     = clamp(round(x / scale), -128, 127)

    Simpler and faster - no zero-point term in the inner
    loop. USE FOR WEIGHTS, which are roughly symmetric
    about zero.

/* ASYMMETRIC: */
    scale = (max - min) / 255
    zero_point = round(-min / scale)

    Uses the full range for skewed distributions. USE FOR
    ACTIVATIONS after ReLU, which are all non-negative -
    symmetric quantisation would waste half the codes.

/* --- WHY THE ACCUMULATOR IS WIDER (chapter 02) --- */
    int8 * int8 -> up to 16 bits, and summing 4096 of them
    needs ~28 bits. So hardware accumulates int8 x int8 into
    INT32, then rescales once at the end:

        acc = 0                          # int32
        for i: acc += a_q[i] * b_q[i]    # int8 x int8
        out = acc * (scale_a * scale_b)  # ONE rescale

    THE SCALES FACTOR OUT OF THE SUM. That is the whole
    trick that makes quantised matmul exact-ish and fast:
    the inner loop is pure integer MAC, and the floating
    point appears once.

/* --- GRANULARITY: the single most important choice --- */
    PER-TENSOR    one scale for the entire weight matrix.
                  Simplest, worst accuracy.
    PER-CHANNEL   one scale per output channel/row. MUCH
                  better, negligible extra cost.
    PER-GROUP     one scale per group of 32-128 weights
                  ALONG a row. What int4 LLM quantisation
                  uses.
                  Costs ~0.5 bits/weight in scale overhead
                  and is worth every bit of it.

    A SINGLE OUTLIER WEIGHT FORCES A LARGE SCALE, which
    coarsens every other weight sharing it. Finer groups
    contain the damage. THIS IS THE ENTIRE REASON GROUPED
    QUANTISATION EXISTS.

/* --- THE OUTLIER PROBLEM IN ACTIVATIONS --- */
    Transformer activations contain a small number of
    dimensions with values 10-100x larger than the rest,
    consistently in the same channels. Quantising naively
    destroys everything else.

    THE SOLUTIONS:
      LLM.int8()   keep the outlier dimensions in fp16, the
                   rest in int8. A mixed-precision matmul.
      SMOOTHQUANT  MIGRATE the difficulty from activations
                   into weights by scaling:
                       Y = (X / s) @ (s * W)
                   mathematically identical, and the
                   scaling is folded into the previous
                   layer's weights so it is free at
                   runtime. Weights are much easier to
                   quantise than activations, so this is a
                   genuinely elegant fix.
      AWQ          identify the ~1% of weight channels that
                   matter most (by activation magnitude) and
                   protect them with per-channel scaling.  */

The methods

The named methods differ mainly in how much work they do up front and what they need access to. Choosing between them is usually a question of what you can afford to run.

/* --- PTQ: POST-TRAINING QUANTISATION --- */
    Quantise an already-trained model. No retraining.

    DYNAMIC   weights quantised offline, activations
              quantised at runtime from their observed
              range. Easy, no calibration data, some
              runtime cost.
    STATIC    activation ranges determined in advance from
              a CALIBRATION SET of a few hundred
              representative samples. Faster at runtime.
              THE CALIBRATION DATA MUST RESEMBLE PRODUCTION
              DATA, or the ranges are wrong.

    THE MODERN LLM METHODS:
      GPTQ    layer-by-layer, using second-order information
              to choose roundings that minimise the output
              error. int4 with small degradation.
      AWQ     activation-aware; protects salient channels.
              Fast, and generally excellent at int4.
      GGUF /  llama.cpp's family (Q4_K_M, Q5_K_M, Q8_0...).
      K-QUANTS Mixed precision per tensor type - attention
              and the output layer get more bits. THE
              PRACTICAL DEFAULT FOR RUNNING MODELS LOCALLY.
      HQQ,
      SqueezeLLM, and a steady stream of others

/* --- QAT: QUANTISATION-AWARE TRAINING --- */
    Simulate quantisation DURING training so the model
    learns weights that survive it.

      forward:  x_q = dequant(quant(x))   # "fake quant"
      backward: STRAIGHT-THROUGH ESTIMATOR - pass the
                gradient through as if quantisation were
                the identity, because round() has zero
                gradient everywhere (chapter 07)

    · better accuracy than PTQ, especially below 8 bits
    · needs training infrastructure and data
    · ESSENTIAL FOR VERY SMALL MODELS on MCUs, where PTQ
      at int8 can lose several percent of accuracy that QAT
      recovers

/* --- WHAT ACCURACY ACTUALLY COSTS (large models) --- */
    fp16      baseline
    int8      essentially lossless with per-channel scales
    int4 GPTQ/AWQ    perplexity +1-3%; usually
                     imperceptible in use
    int3      noticeable degradation
    int2      substantial, though active research
              continues

    AND THE RULE THAT MATTERS FOR CHOOSING:
      A 4-BIT 70B MODEL BEATS AN 8-BIT 34B MODEL AT THE SAME
      MEMORY BUDGET. Given a fixed number of gigabytes,
      prefer a larger model more aggressively quantised -
      up to about 4 bits, below which the relationship
      inverts.

/* --- THE CODE --- */
    # llama.cpp - the practical local path

Quantise the KV cache too

At long context the KV cache exceeds the model itself (chapter 25). Quantising it to int8 halves that, and to int4 quarters it. Keys tolerate quantisation less well than values — keys feed a dot product whose result goes through a softmax, so errors are amplified — so a common configuration is int8 keys with int4 values. For long-context serving this is often a larger win than quantising the weights.

The other compression techniques

Quantisation is the compression technique that works most reliably, but it is not the only one, and the others have their own trade-offs worth knowing.

/* --- PRUNING --- */
    UNSTRUCTURED   zero individual weights. High sparsity
                   achievable (50-90%), and NO SPEEDUP on
                   normal hardware, because the memory
                   access pattern is destroyed (chapter 03).
    STRUCTURED     remove whole channels, heads
                   (chapter 21), or layers. Lower achievable
                   sparsity, REAL SPEEDUP - the matrices are
                   genuinely smaller.
    2:4 SEMI-      exactly 2 of every 4 weights zero.
    STRUCTURED     Supported in hardware by recent NVIDIA
                   parts for a ~2x gain. The compromise that
                   actually ships.

    THE LESSON, which generalises far beyond this: SPARSITY
    ONLY HELPS IF THE HARDWARE CAN EXPLOIT ITS PATTERN.

/* --- DISTILLATION --- */
    Train a small STUDENT to match a large TEACHER.

      L = alpha * CE(student, hard_labels)
        + (1-alpha) * T^2 * KL(student_soft || teacher_soft)

    THE SOFT TARGETS CARRY MORE INFORMATION than the labels:
    a teacher saying "80% cat, 15% dog, 5% fox" tells the
    student about the similarity structure, which a one-hot
    label cannot. This is called DARK KNOWLEDGE, and it is
    why distillation beats training the small model
    directly.

    (The T^2 factor compensates for the gradients shrinking
    as 1/T^2 when the logits are softened.)

    DistilBERT: 40% smaller, 60% faster, 97% of the
    performance. And most good small models today are
    distilled from larger ones - which is why the small-model
    frontier moves whenever the large-model frontier does.

/* --- ARCHITECTURE-LEVEL CHOICES --- */
    · GQA / MQA           smaller KV cache (chapter 19)
    · sliding-window attention
    · fewer, wider layers - better for parallel hardware
    · WEIGHT SHARING across layers (ALBERT-style) - large
      memory saving, some quality cost
    · and simply TRAINING A SMALL MODEL LONGER
      (chapter 23), which is now the mainstream answer

/* --- COMPILATION AND KERNELS --- */
    torch.compile, TensorRT, ONNX Runtime, OpenVINO,
    TVM, ExecuTorch.

    WHAT THEY ACTUALLY DO - and it will be familiar from any
    embedded optimisation work:
      · OPERATOR FUSION: LayerNorm + Linear + GELU as one
        kernel, so intermediates never leave registers or
        SRAM. This is the single biggest win, and it is a
        MEMORY TRAFFIC optimisation.
      · constant folding
      · memory planning and buffer reuse
      · selecting the best kernel per shape
      · removing framework overhead entirely

    2-5x SPEEDUP FOR NO ACCURACY LOSS is typical, and it
    should be the FIRST thing you try - before quantising,
    before pruning, before anything that costs accuracy. */

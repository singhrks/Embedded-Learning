The codec landscape: H.264 to AV1 and beyond

Choosing a codec is rarely a technical decision. Compression efficiency is the easy part; what actually decides it is hardware decode support, patent licensing, and how much CPU you can afford to spend on encoding. This chapter is the map.

Those percentages are marketing until you name the conditions

"AV1 is 50% better than H.264" means: at a slow preset, on 4K content, measured by a specific metric, at a specific quality point. At fast presets the gap shrinks dramatically; at 480p it is far smaller; and x264 --preset veryslow beats SVT-AV1 --preset 10 at equal encoding time. Always ask which encoder, which preset, which resolution, which metric — the format sets a ceiling, the encoder decides how close you get (chapter 11).

Why HEVC lost the web

   HEVC is technically excellent and commercially poisoned.

   H.264's licensing:  ONE pool (MPEG LA), a published rate
                       card, a cap on annual fees, and free
                       for internet video that is free to
                       end users. Everyone knew the cost.

   HEVC's licensing:   MPEG LA -> then HEVC Advance appeared
                       -> then Velos Media -> plus companies
                       holding patents outside every pool.
                       Different terms, some demanding
                       CONTENT royalties (a fee per stream,
                       not per device), and no way to know
                       your total exposure.

   THE RESULT
     * no browser shipped HEVC for years; Safari eventually
       did because Apple pays anyway
     * Google, Mozilla, Amazon, Netflix, Cisco, Intel, ARM
       and others formed AOMedia and built AV1 specifically
       to escape this
     * HEVC still won in one place - BROADCAST AND PHYSICAL
       MEDIA - where device makers already pay pool fees and
       hardware support was mandated

   THE LESSON, and it repeats with VVC: a codec's success is
   decided by whether an implementer can determine the cost
   in advance. VVC is technically ahead of AV1 and is
   repeating HEVC's licensing structure.

   AV1's counter-position: AOMedia members grant a royalty-free
   licence, and the patent defence is collective. It is not a
   guarantee of zero risk - Sisvel formed an AV1 pool in 2019 -
   but the practical outcome so far is that AV1 ships.

Choosing, in practice

/* THE DECISION TREE THAT ACTUALLY APPLIES */

  Must it play EVERYWHERE, including 2012 hardware?
      -> H.264 High profile, 4:2:0 8-bit. No exceptions.
         Still ~80% of internet video for this reason alone.

  Web video, modern browsers, you control the encode?
      -> AV1 for the top rungs, H.264 fallback for the rest.
         This is what YouTube and Netflix do.

  Broadcast, set-top, Blu-ray, or a TV app?
      -> HEVC. Hardware decode is mandated and the licensing
         is already paid by the device maker.

  Apple ecosystem?
      -> HEVC. Hardware encode AND decode on every device
         since 2017, and HDR support is well integrated.

  Real-time conferencing?
      -> H.264 (universal, low latency, cheap hardware
         encode) or AV1 if both ends support it. VP8 still
         appears in WebRTC fallbacks.

  Editing / mastering / archival?
      -> ProRes, DNxHR, or FFV1. Intra-only, 4:2:2 or 4:4:4,
         10-bit+. NOT a delivery codec - you want every frame
         independently decodable and no generation loss
         (chapter 3).

  Screen recording, text-heavy?
      -> H.264 4:4:4 if your decoder supports it, or AV1 with
         palette and intra-block-copy. 4:2:0 destroys text.

/* AND THE ONE THAT CATCHES PEOPLE OUT

   HARDWARE ENCODE IS NOT HARDWARE DECODE. A phone from 2019
   decodes AV1 in software only, but has HEVC decode in
   silicon. A GPU may encode H.264 and HEVC but only decode
   AV1. Check both directions before committing.          */

Profiles, levels and tiers

/* A codec is a menu. A PROFILE says which tools you may use;
   a LEVEL says how big and fast. A decoder claims support for
   a profile AND a level, and both must be satisfied.       */

  H.264 PROFILES
    Baseline    no B-frames, no CABAC. Videoconferencing.
    Main        + B-frames, CABAC, interlacing.
    High        + 8x8 transform, custom quant matrices.
                THE ONE YOU WANT. Universally supported and
                ~10% better than Main.
    High10, High 4:2:2, High 4:4:4  more bit depth and chroma.
                Poor hardware support - check before using.

  LEVELS cap: resolution, frame rate, bitrate, DPB size, and
  the number of motion vectors per second.

    Level 3.1   720p30       14 Mbit/s
    Level 4.0   1080p30      20 Mbit/s
    Level 4.2   1080p60      50 Mbit/s
    Level 5.1   4K30        240 Mbit/s
    Level 6.2   8K120       800 Mbit/s

  HEVC adds TIERS: Main tier for consumer, High tier for
  professional, with much higher bitrate caps at the same
  level.

/* WHY THIS MATTERS PRACTICALLY: a hardware decoder implements
   a level literally. Exceed the DPB size or the bin rate and
   it REFUSES the stream or produces corruption - even though
   ffmpeg plays it fine on your desktop. A stream that is
   "1080p at 20 Mbit/s" but declares level 4.0 while actually
   using 6 reference frames will fail on devices that took the
   declaration at face value.                              */

# Check what a file declares:
$ ffprobe -v error -select_streams v:0 \
    -show_entries stream=codec_name,profile,level,width,height \
    -of default=nw=1 input.mp4
codec_name=h264
profile=High
level=40

# Constrain the encoder to a target device:
$ ffmpeg -i in.mov -c:v libx264 -profile:v high -level:v 4.0 \
    -maxrate 20M -bufsize 25M out.mp4
# x264 will then refuse settings that would violate the level,
# rather than silently producing an unplayable file.
The 80/20 of codec selection

If you are unsure: H.264 High profile, level 4.0, 4:2:0 8-bit, CRF 21, closed 2-second GOP plays on essentially every device made since 2010 and is good enough for almost everything. Add an AV1 rendition when you have measured that the bandwidth saving is worth the encoding cost. Reaching for a newer codec before you have measured is how projects acquire compatibility bugs they cannot reproduce.

# A fair comparison, which is harder than it looks.
# Same source, same target quality metric, measure BOTH size
# and time.

$ time ffmpeg -i src.y4m -c:v libx264  -preset veryslow -crf 23 h264.mp4
$ time ffmpeg -i src.y4m -c:v libx265  -preset slow     -crf 28 hevc.mp4
$ time ffmpeg -i src.y4m -c:v libsvtav1 -preset 4 -crf 35        av1.mp4

# THE CRF SCALES ARE DIFFERENT. Roughly equivalent quality:
#   x264 crf 23  ~  x265 crf 28  ~  SVT-AV1 crf 35
# Comparing "crf 23" across all three is the most common
# benchmarking error and makes AV1 look terrible.

$ for f in h264 hevc av1; do
    printf "%-6s %8s bytes  VMAF " $f $(stat -c%s $f.mp4)
    ffmpeg -v error -i $f.mp4 -i src.y4m \
      -lavfi libvmaf -f null - 2>&1 | grep -o 'score:.*'
  done

# AND REPORT THE ENCODING TIME. A 40% bitrate saving that
# costs 20x the CPU is a business decision, not a technical
# win - it may be right for a title streamed a million times
# and clearly wrong for a security camera.

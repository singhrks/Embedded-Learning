Storage, filesystems and data integrity

Flash does not behave like memory: it wears out, it must be erased in large blocks before it can be written, and a power cut during a write leaves a block in an indeterminate state. Every storage design decision follows from those three facts.

What flash actually does

   THREE PROPERTIES that shape everything above them.

   1. ERASE BEFORE WRITE, AND ERASE IS BLOCK-SIZED
        A write can only turn 1 bits into 0 bits. To turn
        a 0 back into a 1 you must ERASE, and erase works
        on a whole block - 4 kB, 64 kB, sometimes 256 kB.
        So "change one byte" means: read the block, erase
        it, write it back modified. That is slow and it
        costs a full erase cycle.

   2. FINITE ERASE CYCLES
        NOR flash    ~10,000 - 100,000 per block
        SLC NAND     ~60,000 - 100,000
        MLC NAND     ~3,000 - 10,000
        TLC NAND     ~500 - 3,000
        eMMC/SD      varies wildly; consumer cards are the
                     worst and lie about it

        A log that rewrites the same block once a minute
        exhausts a 10,000-cycle block in SEVEN DAYS.

   3. ERASE AND WRITE TAKE A LONG TIME AND CAN BE
      INTERRUPTED
        Erase: 10s to 100s of milliseconds. During it the
        bank may be unreadable - which is why a flash
        routine cannot execute from the bank it is
        erasing. A power cut mid-erase leaves the block
        indeterminate: it may read as anything, and it may
        read DIFFERENTLY on successive reads.

   PLUS, ON NAND SPECIFICALLY:
     - blocks arrive from the factory already bad, and
       more go bad in service. You need bad-block
       management.
     - bit errors are EXPECTED, not exceptional. NAND
       requires ECC to be usable at all.
     - read disturb: reading a page repeatedly can flip
       bits in neighbouring pages.

FRAM is the answer to a specific, common problem

If your design needs to write a small amount of state frequently — an energy meter's running total, a run-hours counter, a position that must survive power loss — flash is the wrong medium at any price, because you will exhaust it. FRAM is byte-writable, needs no erase, writes at bus speed, and survives 10¹²–10¹⁴ cycles. It costs more per byte than flash and you only need a few hundred bytes. Reaching for it unprompted when the question involves a frequently-updated counter is a strong signal.

Filesystems

ON AN MCU - usually no filesystem at all. A fixed layout in
flash with a small record manager is simpler, smaller and
easier to make power-fail safe. If you do want one:

  LittleFS   designed for MCUs: power-fail safe by design,
             wear levelling built in, bounded RAM. The
             default choice.
  SPIFFS     older, no true directories, slower as it
             fills. Being superseded.
  FatFS      when the media must be readable by a PC. NOT
             power-fail safe: FAT has no journal, and an
             interrupted write can corrupt the allocation
             table and lose unrelated files.

ON LINUX:

  ext4       the default. Journals METADATA by default,
             not your file's contents - so a power cut can
             leave a file that exists, is the right size,
             and contains garbage.
  F2FS       flash-friendly, log-structured. Good on eMMC.
  UBIFS      for RAW NAND. Sits on UBI, which does the
             wear levelling and bad-block management. This
             is the correct stack for bare NAND; putting
             ext4 on raw NAND is a mistake.
  SquashFS   read-only, compressed. Excellent for a root
             filesystem you never write - which is what
             you want (chapter 14).
  overlayfs  read-only lower + tmpfs upper. Writes go to
             RAM and vanish at reboot, so the flash is
             never written and cannot be corrupted.

THE ARCHITECTURE THAT AVOIDS MOST PROBLEMS:
  read-only root (SquashFS or ext4 ro)
  + tmpfs for /tmp, /var/log, /run
  + a small separate rw partition for state, mounted sync
  Nothing writes to the main filesystem in normal
  operation, so a power cut cannot corrupt it.

Making a write survive a power cut

THE PATTERN, and it is the same at every scale:

  NEVER UPDATE IN PLACE. Write the new version somewhere
  else, then switch to it in one atomic step.

--- ON LINUX ---------------------------------------------
    fd = open("state.tmp", O_WRONLY|O_CREAT|O_TRUNC);
    write(fd, data, len);
    fsync(fd);                 /* data actually on media  */
    close(fd);
    rename("state.tmp", "state.json");   /* ATOMIC        */
    /* and fsync the DIRECTORY too, or the rename itself
       may not be durable:                                */
    dirfd = open(".", O_RDONLY); fsync(dirfd); close(dirfd);

  The fsync calls are the part people omit. Without them
  the rename can be durable while the data is not, leaving
  you with a correctly-named empty file.

--- ON AN MCU, NO FILESYSTEM -----------------------------
  Two slots plus a sequence number and a CRC:

    struct record {
        uint32_t seq;        /* increments each write     */
        uint8_t  payload[N];
        uint32_t crc;        /* over seq + payload        */
    };
    /* slot A and slot B in different erase blocks */

  WRITE:  erase the OLDER slot, write the new record with
          seq = newer_seq + 1.
  READ:   read both, validate CRCs, take the valid one
          with the higher sequence number.

  A power cut while writing a slot leaves that slot
  invalid (bad CRC) and the OTHER slot intact and valid.
  There is no window where both are bad. Handle sequence
  wraparound, or use a 64-bit counter.

  This is roughly forty lines and it is the single most
  useful piece of embedded storage code there is.

Questions

A device logs one line per second to internal flash. What is wrong with that?Mid

Wear, if it is implemented naively. Flash erases in blocks, so appending a line to the same block means read-modify-erase-write of the whole block. At one write per second against a 10,000-cycle endurance you exhaust that block in under three hours if every write erases; even at one erase per minute it is about seven days.

The fixes, in order: buffer in RAM and write a full page at a time, which cuts erase count by the number of records per page; append sequentially through the region so each block is erased once per pass rather than per record (a log-structured layout); wear-level across all available blocks; and use a filesystem that does this for you — LittleFS on an MCU, F2FS or UBIFS on Linux.

Then question the requirement: does the log need to be persistent, or is it diagnostic? If diagnostic, write it to RAM and only persist on a fault — that is the tmpfs pattern, and it eliminates the problem entirely.

How do you make a configuration write survive a power cut at any instant?Mid

Never update in place. On an MCU: two slots in different erase blocks, each holding a sequence number, the payload and a CRC. To write, erase the older slot and write a record with sequence+1. To read, validate both CRCs and take the valid record with the higher sequence. A power cut during a write leaves that slot invalid and the other intact — there is no window where both are bad.

On Linux: write to a temporary file, fsync it, close, rename over the target — which is atomic on ext4 — and then fsync the containing directory so the rename itself is durable. Omitting the fsyncs is the common bug and leaves you with a correctly-named empty file.

Then verify by testing: automated power cuts under load, a hundred times, is the only way to know. Products that survive this were designed for it.

Listening forThe two-slot scheme with sequence and CRC, and the directory fsync on Linux.
Why is an SD card a bad idea in a shipped product?Mid

Because you are trusting an undocumented controller you cannot qualify. Consumer cards do wear levelling and bad-block management internally with proprietary firmware whose behaviour on power loss is unspecified — and several are known to corrupt data outside the block being written, or to brick entirely. Endurance varies by an order of magnitude between cards sold under the same part number, manufacturers change the internals without changing the marking, and counterfeits are common.

They are also mechanically unreliable — a socket is a connection that vibrates loose and oxidises — and removable, which is a security and support problem.

Use eMMC instead: soldered down, industrial grades available with specified endurance and power-loss behaviour, and a supply chain you can qualify. If cost forces raw NAND, use UBI/UBIFS which handles ECC and bad blocks explicitly. Keep SD cards for development, where reimaging in ninety seconds is exactly what you want.

What does ext4's journal actually protect?Senior

Metadata, by default — the filesystem structure: inodes, directory entries, the allocation bitmaps. That means after a power cut the filesystem is consistent and mountable, and you will not have cross-linked files or a corrupt directory tree.

It does not protect your file's contents. The default data=ordered mode guarantees that data blocks are written before the metadata that references them — so you will not see stale data from a deleted file — but a file being rewritten when power was lost can legitimately end up existing, being the right size, and containing a mixture of old and new bytes or zeros.

data=journal journals contents too, at a substantial write-amplification cost, which on flash is also a wear cost. Usually the better answer is not to rely on the filesystem at all: use atomic rename for anything that matters, and keep the root filesystem read-only so the question rarely arises.

Listening forThe metadata/data distinction — this is the misunderstanding that causes people to trust a journal too much.
You need to store a counter updated every second, forever. What do you use?Mid

FRAM or MRAM. Flash is the wrong medium at any price: one write per second is 31 million writes a year, and even with perfect wear levelling across a large region you are burning through endurance for no reason. FRAM is byte-writable with no erase step, writes at bus speed, and survives 10¹²–10¹⁴ cycles — effectively unlimited at this rate. You need a few bytes, so the cost is small in absolute terms.

If FRAM is not available: keep the counter in RAM and persist it rarely — on a threshold change, on shutdown, or on a brown-out interrupt using the energy stored in the bulk capacitor. Combine with a conservative estimate on recovery (assume the maximum possible progress since the last save), so the counter never goes backwards.

Alternatively, spread the writes: a ring of N slots in flash, each written in turn, multiplies endurance by N — which is what a wear-levelling layer does, and is worth implementing explicitly if the pattern is this simple.

How much flash do you budget for a device that must support firmware updates?Mid

Roughly double the application, plus the bootloader and a small metadata region — because A/B updating means holding a complete second copy. This is the term most often forgotten in a memory budget and it frequently moves you to the next part size up.

The alternatives when flash is tight: a single-bank update with a recovery bootloader — smaller, but there is a window during which the device has no valid application and must be able to re-download, which requires the bootloader to contain enough of a communication stack to do so; or external flash for the staging area, which costs a part and pins but keeps the internal flash for code; or compressed images, which reduces the download and the staging area but not the final size.

Also budget the erase time: writing a 512 kB image to flash takes seconds and during that period the device must remain responsive enough not to trip its own watchdog.

What is write amplification and why should you care?Senior

The ratio of physical bytes written to the medium against logical bytes your application wrote. If you append 100 bytes to a file and the layer beneath must read, erase and rewrite a 128 kB block, the amplification is over a thousand — and your endurance budget is consumed a thousand times faster than the naive calculation.

Sources: erase-block granularity, filesystem metadata updates (each write may also update an inode and a bitmap), journaling (which writes the data twice), and the FTL inside an eMMC doing garbage collection and block relocation.

Why it matters: it is the difference between an endurance calculation that says ten years and reality that says ten months. To reduce it — batch writes so each erase block is filled before it is closed; align writes to the erase block size; use a log-structured filesystem which is designed for exactly this; avoid sync mounts for high-rate data; and disable atime, since otherwise every read becomes a metadata write.

Listening foratime as a hidden write source — it is a small detail that indicates real experience.
A field unit's filesystem is corrupt. Walk me through your response.Senior

Immediate: does the device still boot? A read-only root plus a separate data partition means corruption of the data partition is survivable — the device boots, reports the fault, and continues with defaults. If the root is corrupt and there is no recovery image, the unit is dead and that is a design failure, not an operational one.

Diagnosis: what was writing at the time? Check the power history first — an under-voltage record or a brown-out counter usually explains it, and the fix is electrical rather than software. Then check whether the write path was atomic, whether the filesystem was mounted with the options you think, and whether the medium is at end of life (SMART data on eMMC, or your own erase-cycle counter).

Design response: make the root read-only; make every persistent write atomic; set the brown-out threshold above the voltage where flash writes become unreliable; keep a recovery image or A/B rootfs so a corrupt bank is not fatal; and add a boot-time integrity check that can fall back rather than fail.

And the honest note: if this is happening across a fleet, it is a systemic design issue and fixing the individual units is not the response.

Listening forChecking power history before blaming software, and treating a fleet-wide pattern as a design failure.
Do you need a filesystem at all on a microcontroller?Mid

Usually not, and not having one is simpler and safer. A fixed layout — a config region using the two-slot atomic scheme, a log region written sequentially, and a firmware staging region — covers most needs in a few hundred lines, is easy to reason about, and has no failure modes you did not design.

You want a filesystem when: the data is genuinely file-shaped and variable (several independent records whose sizes are not known in advance), when a PC must read the medium (which forces FAT and its power-fail weakness), or when a third-party component expects one.

If you do want one on an MCU, LittleFS is the default: designed for this environment, power-fail safe by construction, with wear levelling and bounded RAM usage. What to avoid is FAT for internal state — no journal, and an interrupted write can corrupt the allocation table and take unrelated files with it.

Listening forDefaulting to "no filesystem" and justifying when one is warranted, rather than assuming one is needed.
How do you detect that flash is wearing out before it fails?Senior

Instrument it. Count erase cycles per block (or per region) in your own metadata and expose the maximum as telemetry — you know the rated endurance, so you can report a percentage consumed and predict end of life. That is the only reliable approach on raw flash, because the medium will not tell you.

On eMMC, the device reports it: the health status registers give estimated lifetime consumed in 10% increments for SLC and MLC regions, plus a pre-EOL indicator. Read them periodically and report.

Behavioural indicators worth monitoring regardless: write or erase operations that take longer than usual, rising ECC correction counts on NAND, verify-after-write failures, and blocks being retired by the bad-block manager. Any of those trending upward is an early warning.

And the design response to the prediction: if a fleet is going to hit end of life at three years and the product is warranted for five, that is a design change now — reduce write rate, increase the region, or change medium — not a support problem later.

Listening forCounting cycles yourself on raw flash, and treating the prediction as an input to design rather than to support.

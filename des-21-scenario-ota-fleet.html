Scenario: OTA updates for a fleet

A systems design round rather than a board design one. It tests whether you think about failure at scale, and it has a characteristic trap: the mechanism on one device is the easy half.

The prompt

"We have 500,000 battery-powered devices in the field on cellular connections. Design the firmware update system. Assume the devices are already deployed and cannot be recalled."

1 · Clarify

  WHAT DO THE DEVICES ALREADY SUPPORT? -> THE FIRST
        QUESTION. "Already deployed" means the on-device
        mechanism may be fixed. If they have A/B and
        signature verification, this is a server and
        process problem. If they do not, the answer is
        very different and much more constrained.
        ASSUME: A/B slots, signature verification, and a
        bootloader that can revert. If they lack it, say
        clearly that the first update must be the one that
        adds it, and that update is the riskiest one you
        will ever ship.

  IMAGE SIZE?          ~400 kB.
  CONNECTIVITY?        NB-IoT, intermittent, expensive per
                       byte, and the device chooses when to
                       connect.
  BATTERY?             the update itself costs energy that
                       is not in the original budget.
  HOW OFTEN?           a few times a year, plus emergency
                       security fixes.
  ACCEPTABLE ROLLOUT TIME? -> weeks is fine for a feature
                       release; a security fix may need
                       days, which is a different design
                       point.
  CAN A DEVICE BE VISITED?  -> assume no, or at prohibitive
                       cost. That makes brick-avoidance the
                       overriding requirement.

RESTATE:
  "500k devices, no physical access, cellular and
  intermittent, energy-constrained, 400 kB images. The
  device-side mechanism is largely solved by A/B; the hard
  parts are the rollout process, the energy cost, and
  making sure a bad image cannot take out the fleet. I'll
  focus there."

2 · The device side

   THE SEQUENCE, and each step has a failure it exists to
   handle.

   1. CHECK IN. On its normal schedule, the device reports
      its version and asks whether an update is available.
      The DEVICE initiates - nothing can reach a sleeping
      device on a cellular link.

   2. DECIDE. The server answers based on the rollout
      cohort, the device's battery state, and its signal
      quality. A device at 15% battery should not attempt
      a 400 kB download.

   3. DOWNLOAD IN CHUNKS, RESUMABLE. A single long session
      is both an energy spike and a failure risk; a
      dropped connection must resume, not restart. Each
      chunk is verified on arrival.

   4. VERIFY THE WHOLE IMAGE: hash, then signature. Only
      then mark slot B valid.

   5. FLAG AND REBOOT at a moment the device chooses -
      not mid-measurement, and not when it has unsent
      data it would lose.

   6. CONFIRM. B must reach the server and report its new
      version within a window. Only then is B marked
      permanent.

   7. If confirmation does not happen, the watchdog
      resets and the bootloader reverts to A - which
      then reports the failure on its next check-in, so
      the SERVER LEARNS the image is bad.

   STEP 7 IS THE ONE THAT MAKES THE SYSTEM SAFE AT SCALE:
   a failed update is not just survived, it is REPORTED,
   which is what lets an automatic halt work.

   THE CONFIRMATION CRITERION MATTERS ENORMOUSLY. "The
   application started" would pass an image that boots and
   cannot connect - which is precisely the failure that
   bricks a fleet. It must be "reached the server", because
   that is the property you actually need to preserve:
   MANAGEABILITY.

3 · The energy budget nobody plans for

A 400 kB DOWNLOAD OVER NB-IoT, at perhaps 20-60 kbit/s
effective:

    400 kB = 3.2 Mbit
    at 40 kbit/s = 80 seconds of radio time
    at, say, 100 mA average while active
    = 80 x 100 = 8000 mA-s = 2.2 mAh

  Against a device budgeted at, say, 30 uA average (about
  0.72 mAh per day), a single update costs roughly THREE
  DAYS of normal operation. Two or three updates a year is
  perhaps 1% of the annual budget - acceptable, and worth
  stating explicitly so nobody is surprised.

  BUT: a device that retries a failed download repeatedly
  can burn far more. So the design needs
    - a bounded retry count per update attempt
    - a minimum battery threshold below which it refuses
    - backoff between attempts
    - and the server should stop offering an update to a
      device that has failed it several times, rather than
      letting it drain itself trying

  THE LEVERS if the energy cost is too high:
    compress the image (typically 2-3x on firmware)
    delta updates against the known previous version -
      much smaller, at the cost of per-device state on the
      server and a patch applier that must itself be
      power-fail safe (chapter 12)
    schedule downloads when signal is strong, since poor
      signal means retransmissions and much higher energy
      per byte

4 · The rollout — where fleets are actually saved

   STAGED ROLLOUT WITH AUTOMATIC HALT.

     cohort 0   internal test devices        ~50
     cohort 1   1% of the fleet            5,000
     cohort 2   10%                       50,000
     cohort 3   remainder                445,000

   BETWEEN COHORTS, WAIT AND WATCH. The metric is not
   "did the download succeed" - it is CHECK-IN RATE.
   Devices that took the update and then went quiet are
   the signal that matters, and they are invisible if you
   only count successful downloads.

     halt if: check-in rate for updated devices falls
              below baseline by more than a threshold
              revert rate exceeds a threshold
              crash reports rise
              any device fails to confirm more than once

   THE WAIT PERIOD must exceed the devices' natural
   check-in interval by a comfortable margin - if devices
   report daily, a two-hour soak proves nothing.

   THIS IS THE MECHANISM THAT TURNS A FLEET-WIDE OUTAGE
   INTO 5,000 AFFECTED DEVICES. Everything else on this
   page is about surviving on one device; this is about
   surviving as a fleet, and it is what candidates most
   often omit.

   PLUS:
     - a KILL SWITCH: the ability to stop a rollout
       immediately and to roll back devices that have
       already taken it
     - version pinning per cohort, so a device that
       reappears after months does not jump to an
       untested version
     - and the ability to hold specific devices back -
       there is always a customer who must not be touched

Follow-up questions

The devices in the field do not have A/B or signature verification. Now what?Senior

Then the first update is the most dangerous one the company will ever ship, and it should be treated accordingly.

What is possible depends entirely on what the existing bootloader can do. If there is a bootloader that can receive an image over the air at all, the first update replaces the application with one that adds A/B and verification for the future — accepting that this particular update has no fallback. That means: an extremely small and heavily reviewed change; extensive testing on hardware identical to the field units, including at low battery and poor signal; a very slow staged rollout starting with tens of devices; and a willingness to stop permanently if the early cohort shows any problem.

If flash space does not allow adding a second slot, the honest answer may be that full A/B is impossible on this hardware and the best achievable is a minimal recovery loader that can always re-download — which still leaves a window requiring connectivity.

And I would say plainly that this is a design failure being paid for later, and that the fix for the next product is to ship the update mechanism before shipping the product.

Listening forRecognising the first update has no safety net, and proportionate caution rather than confidence.
What is the right confirmation criterion, and why does it matter so much?Senior

"Successfully connected to the server and reported the new version." Not "the application started", and not "a self-test passed".

The reason is that the property you must preserve above all others is manageability — the ability to update the device again. An image that boots perfectly and cannot connect is functionally a brick on a device you cannot visit, and a weaker criterion would confirm it as good and then propagate it to the rest of the fleet.

The complication is legitimate outages: a device in a cellular dead spot or during a network incident will fail to connect through no fault of the new image, and reverting it unnecessarily wastes energy and churns the fleet.

So a practical scheme uses two windows: confirm provisionally on a local self-test so the device does not reboot-loop, but keep the ability to revert until a successful server contact within a much longer window — days rather than minutes. Devices in a genuine outage then survive; an image that broke connectivity still rolls back eventually.

Listening forManageability as the property being protected, and handling the legitimate-outage case with two windows.
How do you avoid all 500,000 devices downloading at once?Mid

Several layers, and they compose.

Cohorts: only a fraction of the fleet is eligible at any time, which caps concurrency by design.

Server-side admission: the device asks, and the server can say "yes", "no", or "come back in N hours" — so the server controls the rate directly and can shed load. This is much better than devices deciding for themselves, because it adapts to actual capacity.

Jitter: devices check in on randomised schedules, not on the hour, so there is no natural synchronisation. This matters most after any event that synchronises them — a network outage or a mass reboot — because otherwise they all return together.

Content delivery: serve images from a CDN so the bandwidth is not a single origin's problem, and use range requests so resumed downloads do not re-fetch.

The failure this prevents is the thundering herd, and it is worth naming: without jitter and admission control, a regional outage causes every affected device to reconnect and request simultaneously, the server degrades, connections fail, devices back off in lockstep and do it again — oscillating for hours.

Listening forServer-side admission control, and naming the thundering herd explicitly.
A security vulnerability needs patching in days, not weeks. How does that change things?Senior

It compresses the staged rollout, which is exactly the safety mechanism you would rather not compress — so the decision is a risk trade and should be made explicitly rather than by default.

What I would do: shorten but do not skip the stages — even a few hours at 1% catches a catastrophic image, and catching it is worth more than the hours lost. Increase the observation sensitivity so a halt triggers on weaker evidence. Prioritise the most exposed devices first if the vulnerability's exploitability varies. And prepare the rollback path before starting, rather than after.

The levers that genuinely speed things up without reducing safety: a smaller patch (delta or a minimal targeted fix rather than a full release), so downloads complete faster and fewer devices are mid-update; asking devices to check in more frequently for the duration, accepting the energy cost; and having the server actively offer to every device that appears rather than waiting for a schedule.

And the honest framing for the business: shipping a fix to 500,000 devices in days carries real risk of a worse outcome than the vulnerability, and that comparison should be made with the security team rather than assumed either way.

Listening forRefusing to skip staging entirely, and framing it as an explicit risk trade.
A device has been offline for a year and reappears on an old version. What happens?Mid

It must be able to update from whatever version it is on to current, which has several consequences that are easy to miss.

Keep every released image indefinitely, and their signing metadata, because you cannot predict which version a returning device will report. Test the oldest-to-newest path — not just the last-to-current one — because migration logic (a changed configuration format, a new key derivation, a different data layout) may only be correct for adjacent versions.

If updates must be sequential — which delta updates force, and which stepwise data migrations may also force — then the device may need several update cycles to catch up, each costing energy and each a chance to fail. That argues for keeping full images available even if deltas are the normal path, so a far-behind device can jump directly.

Also: the server must still speak the old protocol version the device is using, which is why the device-server protocol needs versioning and long-term backward compatibility. And the device's certificate may have expired while it was away, so there must be a path to re-establish trust that does not require the very connectivity being authenticated.

Listening forTesting the oldest path, and the expired-certificate problem.
How do you know the rollout is going well?Senior

By watching the devices that took it, not the downloads that completed — and the distinction is the whole point.

The primary metric is check-in rate among updated devices compared with baseline. A device that downloaded successfully, rebooted and then went quiet is exactly the failure mode that matters, and a "download success" counter shows it as a success.

Supporting metrics: revert rate (devices that confirmed nothing and fell back); crash and watchdog reset counts reported after update; error counters per subsystem compared before and after; battery drain rate, since a regression that keeps the radio awake shows here first; and the version distribution across the fleet, which should move as expected.

The operational requirements: a defined baseline measured before the rollout, because "normal" varies by cohort and by season; a wait period longer than the natural check-in interval, or the data is meaningless; and automatic halt thresholds set in advance rather than judged in the moment.

And keep the ability to compare cohorts directly — updated versus not-yet-updated devices in the same population is a far stronger signal than comparing against last week.

Listening forCheck-in rate rather than download success, and the cohort comparison.
What could still take out the whole fleet despite all of this?Senior

Several things, and being able to name them is the point of the question.

A server-side change — the fleet is fine, but an API change, an expired server certificate or a DNS misconfiguration makes every device unable to connect. This is often more likely than a bad firmware image and receives far less scrutiny, because it does not go through the staged rollout process. The mitigation is treating server changes with the same discipline, plus devices that tolerate failures gracefully rather than entering a bad state.

A certificate expiring on the device side, with no path to update the trust store.

A time-based bug — a counter wrapping, a leap-second or date-handling error — which fires on every device simultaneously regardless of firmware version, because they share the trigger rather than the rollout.

A compromised signing key, which allows an attacker to push a malicious image through the legitimate mechanism — hence revocation.

A common environmental cause — a cellular operator changing network configuration, or retiring a technology the modems depend on.

The general lesson: staged rollout protects against changes you make to firmware. It does not protect against changes to everything else, and those need their own discipline.

Listening forServer-side and time-based failures, which bypass the rollout protection entirely.
What would you insist on before the first product ships, knowing this?Senior

A short list, all of which are cheap before shipping and impossible after.

A/B slots with confirmation, and the flash budgeted for it from the start — this is the single most important item and the one most often sacrificed to save part cost. Signature verification anchored in ROM and fuses, with a revocation counter, so a leaked key does not end the product. An updatable trust store, so certificate expiry is survivable.

Telemetry designed in: version, battery, error counters, reset causes and check-in reliability, because without them a rollout cannot be monitored and a fleet cannot be diagnosed. A protocol version field in everything, device-to-server and between processors, so incompatible versions degrade instead of breaking.

Archived build artefacts and symbols per release, so a crash log from a three-year-old version can still be decoded.

And organisationally: an end-of-support policy stated up front, and the update mechanism tested — including the revert path and the oldest-version path — as a release gate, not as a feature someone will get to.

Listening forPrioritising A/B with the flash budget, and treating the revert path as something to be tested rather than assumed.

Safety, reliability and failure analysis

Safety is about not hurting anyone; reliability is about continuing to work. They are different goals with different techniques, and a device can be very safe and very unreliable — a machine that shuts down at the slightest doubt is exactly that.

Safety is not reliability

   SAFETY       the system does not cause harm. The
                measure is: can it reach a dangerous
                state? A device that fails SAFE - motor
                off, valve closed - is safe even if it
                fails constantly.

   RELIABILITY  the system keeps doing its job. Measured
                as MTBF, availability, or failures per
                million hours.

   THEY CAN CONFLICT. A redundant channel improves
   availability and adds a component that can fail. An
   aggressive fault response improves safety and reduces
   availability. Which you favour depends entirely on the
   consequence of each kind of failure:

     an airbag        must not deploy spuriously (safety)
                      AND must deploy when needed (both)
     a pacemaker      availability IS safety
     a factory robot  fail-safe is stopping
     a flight control fail-safe is NOT stopping - you need
                      continued control, so redundancy and
                      graceful degradation rather than
                      shutdown

   NAMING THIS DISTINCTION EARLY in an answer is a strong
   signal, because it shows you know the fail-safe state
   is a domain question rather than a technical default.

Two analyses worth knowing

FMEA - Failure Modes and Effects Analysis. Bottom-up:
for each component, how can it fail, and what happens?

  component     failure mode     effect          detection      mitigation
  ------------- ---------------- --------------- -------------- ----------------
  temp sensor   reads high       under-heating   plausibility   dual sensor,
                                                 range check    compare
  temp sensor   stuck at last    over-heating    value not      timestamp,
                value            (dangerous)     changing       stale detection
  MOSFET        shorted on       heater always   current sense  hardware over-
                                 on (dangerous)                 temp cutout
  MCU           hangs            no control      watchdog       reset to safe
                                                                state
  connector     intermittent     spurious        CRC + counters debounce, fault
                                 readings                       counter

  THE STUCK-AT-LAST-VALUE ROW is the one candidates miss.
  A sensor that fails to a plausible constant is far more
  dangerous than one that fails to an obvious extreme,
  because range checks do not catch it. The detection is
  that the value stops CHANGING when it should - which
  means you need a timestamp and a staleness check, not
  just a range check.

FTA - Fault Tree Analysis. Top-down: start from the
hazard and work backwards through AND/OR gates to find
what combinations cause it.

  HAZARD: heater stays on with no water
       |
     [AND]
      |  \
      |   +-- water level sensor fails to detect empty
      |
      +------ over-temperature cutout fails

  An AND gate is good news: two INDEPENDENT things must
  fail. The critical follow-up is whether they really are
  independent - a shared power rail, a shared MCU, a
  shared ground, or a common design error defeats the
  independence and turns your AND into an OR.

  COMMON-CAUSE FAILURE is the thing that makes redundancy
  less valuable than it looks, and raising it unprompted
  is a senior signal.
The question to ask about any redundancy

"What could make both channels fail at once?" A shared supply, a shared clock, a shared sensor, the same software bug in both, the same batch of components with the same defect, a common environmental cause (temperature, vibration, EMI), or the same engineer making the same mistake twice. Genuine redundancy requires diversity — different sensing principles, different suppliers, independent supplies, sometimes independently developed software. Redundancy without diversity mostly protects against random hardware failure, which is often not the dominant failure mode.

THE ORDER I WOULD APPLY THEM, for a device with a real
hazard and a normal budget:

  1. HARDWARE INTERLOCK for the actual hazard. Independent
     of software entirely - it survives a hung CPU, a
     corrupted variable and a bad firmware update. This is
     almost always the highest-value single measure.
  2. WATCHDOG, fed only from a place that proves progress.
  3. SAFE STATE reachable without working firmware.
  4. PLAUSIBILITY AND STALENESS CHECKS on every input.
  5. MPU guard regions, so a software fault is caught
     where it happens.
  6. CRC on anything persistent.
  7. Redundancy, only where the analysis shows a single
     point of failure that matters and diversity is
     achievable.

STEP 1 BEFORE STEP 7. Candidates often reach for
redundancy first because it sounds serious; a hardware
cutout is cheaper, simpler and stronger for most
hazards.

Questions

What is the difference between safety and reliability?Mid

Safety is about not causing harm; reliability is about continuing to function. They are separate properties and can conflict: a machine that halts at the first sign of doubt is very safe and very unreliable, and adding a redundant channel improves availability while adding a component that can itself fail.

The design consequence is that you must know which one the domain prioritises, and that determines the fail-safe state. For a factory robot, stopping is safe. For a heater, off is safe. For an aircraft flight control system, stopping is not safe — you need continued control, so the architecture is redundancy and graceful degradation rather than shutdown. For a pacemaker, availability essentially is safety.

So "what is the safe state?" is a domain question, and getting it wrong produces a correct-looking failure response that makes the situation worse.

Listening forThe conflict between them, and the fail-safe state being domain-determined rather than a default.
Walk me through an FMEA for a temperature-controlled heater.Mid

Bottom-up, component by component, asking how each fails and what results.

The sensor can read high (heater under-runs — annoying, not dangerous), read low (heater over-runs — dangerous), go open or short circuit (detectable as an out-of-range value), or stick at its last plausible value — which is the dangerous one, because a range check does not catch it and the controller keeps heating toward a target it will never see reached.

The MOSFET can fail open (no heat) or fail shorted (heater permanently on, regardless of what software wants). The MCU can hang. The connector can go intermittent.

Detections and mitigations: range and rate-of-change checks plus a staleness timestamp for the stuck-sensor case; a second sensor of a different type for diversity; a current sense to detect the shorted MOSFET; a watchdog for the hang; and — most importantly — an independent hardware over-temperature cutout, typically a thermal fuse or a bimetallic switch in series with the heater, that operates with no software involvement at all.

That last one is the point: for the dangerous failure modes, the mitigation should not depend on the software that might itself be the problem.

Listening forThe stuck-at-last-value mode and the independent hardware cutout.
How do you detect a sensor that has failed to a plausible constant value?Senior

Range checks cannot help, so you need checks on behaviour rather than value.

Staleness: timestamp each reading and require that it changes. A real temperature sensor in a real environment shows noise of at least a fraction of a degree; a value identical to the last N readings, bit for bit, is a fault. This is the primary detection and it is a few lines of code.

Rate of change: a physical quantity has bounded slew. A reading that jumps impossibly fast is a fault; one that does not move when a control output has changed substantially is also a fault.

Cross-check: against a second sensor, ideally of a different type, or against a model — "we have been applying full heat for two minutes and the temperature has not risen; either the heater is disconnected or the sensor is lying".

Active test: where the hardware allows, periodically inject a known stimulus and confirm the expected response — some ADCs let you read an internal reference through the same path.

The general principle worth stating: every input should be validated for range, rate and liveness, and the liveness check is the one most often missing.

Listening forLiveness as a distinct category from range checking, and the model-based cross-check.
Why is a hardware interlock better than a software check?Mid

Because it is independent of the thing most likely to be wrong. A hardware cutout — a comparator driving a gate driver's enable, a thermal fuse in series with a heater, a break input forcing PWM outputs to a safe state — continues to work when the CPU has hung, when a variable has been corrupted, when a task missed its deadline, and when a bad firmware update has been installed.

It is also faster: a comparator responds in nanoseconds to microseconds, where a software path is bounded by interrupt latency plus scheduling. For an over-current condition that destroys a bridge in microseconds, software is simply not a candidate.

And it simplifies the safety argument: instead of proving that all your firmware is correct, you prove that one small circuit is correct and that it cannot be defeated by software. That is a dramatically smaller verification problem, which is why safety standards lean on it.

The cost is small — often a comparator, a resistor divider and a transistor — which is why it should usually be the first measure considered rather than the last.

Listening forIndependence from software as the core argument, plus the smaller verification burden.
Where should a watchdog be petted, and what does it not catch?Mid

Only from a place that proves real progress — after a complete control cycle, or when every critical task has checked in since the last feed. A watchdog fed from a timer interrupt or a dedicated thread will keep a completely stuck application alive indefinitely, which is worse than having no watchdog because it hides the fault.

What it does not catch: anything where the software keeps running but does the wrong thing. A control loop computing a wrong output, a corrupted calibration constant, a state machine in a legal but incorrect state — all of those keep petting the watchdog happily. For those you need plausibility checks and, for the dangerous cases, a hardware interlock.

Design details worth adding: record the reset cause at boot into non-volatile memory, or a field unit that resets tells you nothing; use a windowed watchdog if available, which also detects petting too often (a runaway loop); and set the timeout from the worst-case cycle time with margin, not from a guess.

Listening forThe petting rule and the honest scope — that a watchdog catches hangs, not wrongness.
Two redundant sensors disagree. What does the system do?Senior

With two sensors you can detect disagreement but not resolve it — you do not know which one is wrong. So the response must be a defined fail-safe action, not a guess: enter the safe state, report the fault, and require intervention. Silently picking one — the higher, the lower, the average — is a decision that must be justified by the physics, and averaging two values one of which is nonsense is usually the worst option.

If continued operation matters, you need a third channel so you can vote two-out-of-three, or an independent means of adjudication such as a model-based estimate of what the value should be.

The design questions this raises: what disagreement threshold counts as a fault, given both sensors have tolerance and noise? How long must they disagree before you act, so that transient noise does not trip it? And are the two sensors genuinely independent — same type, same batch, same supply rail, same wiring loom means a common cause defeats the redundancy entirely.

Listening for"Detect but not resolve" with two channels, and questioning whether the redundancy is genuinely independent.
What makes redundancy less effective than it looks?Senior

Common-cause failure. Two identical channels fail together when the cause is shared: a common power rail, a common clock, a common ground, the same environmental stress (temperature, vibration, EMI, a surge), the same component batch with the same latent defect, or — most importantly for anything software-controlled — the same software bug running on both.

That last point matters because for a system whose complexity lives in software, duplicating the software duplicates its bugs. Two identical MCUs running identical firmware protect against random hardware faults and nothing else.

The response is diversity: different sensing principles (a thermocouple and a thermistor rather than two thermistors), different suppliers, independent supplies, physically separated wiring, and in high-integrity systems independently developed software. Diversity is expensive, which is why it is reserved for the hazards that justify it — and identifying which those are is what the fault tree analysis is for.

Listening forSoftware common-cause specifically, and diversity as the response.
How would you approach a project that must meet a functional safety standard?Senior

I would be careful about clause-level claims — the specific requirements differ by standard and by integrity level, and I would want the actual standard and a competent assessor rather than working from memory. What I can speak to is the shape of the work, which is consistent across them.

It is process-driven: a hazard analysis and risk assessment that assigns an integrity level to each safety function; requirements traceable from that analysis down to code and test; design and code constrained by the standard (typically no dynamic allocation, no recursion, bounded loops, a coding standard such as MISRA, static analysis); verification proportionate to the integrity level, including structural coverage; tool qualification for compilers and analysers; and documented evidence for all of it.

The architectural consequences are the interesting part for a design round: you partition so that the safety function is as small and simple as possible, often on its own microcontroller, because the verification burden scales with the size and complexity of what is in scope. A 2,000-line safety binary on a simple core is a tractable certification; the same function embedded in a Linux application is not.

And it must be planned from the start — retrofitting the process onto finished code is close to impossible, because the evidence is generated by the process.

Listening forPartitioning to shrink the certification scope, and honesty about not reciting standards from memory.
A field unit fails once every three months with no pattern. How do you investigate?Senior

The rate is the problem: you cannot reproduce it, so you need the device to record what happened.

First, exploit what should already be there: the reset cause register, a persistent crash log with the faulting PC and fault status registers, error counters per subsystem, and the power history — an under-voltage record explains a great many "random" failures and points at hardware rather than software.

If those are absent, adding them is the first fix, and I would deploy an instrumented build to a subset of the fleet rather than waiting for a return.

Then look for correlation across the fleet: is it time-based (a counter wrapping, a certificate expiring, a log filling), environment-based (temperature, a specific site), load-based, or associated with a particular hardware batch? A single unit gives you nothing; a hundred units with timestamps gives you a pattern.

And the classic candidates for a low-rate intermittent failure: an integer or timestamp wrap; a rare race that needs a specific interleaving; memory exhaustion or fragmentation after long uptime; a marginal supply or connector that fails with thermal cycling; and cosmic-ray or noise-induced bit flips, which is why safety systems CRC their persistent data and periodically self-test RAM.

Listening forChecking power history early, and fleet-level correlation rather than single-unit debugging.
What is the safe state for a device that needs power to be safe?Senior

This is the awkward case, and it needs designing rather than asserting. If the safe state requires energy — a brake that must be actively released, a valve that must be held closed, a control surface that must be positioned — then losing power is itself the hazard, and "fail safe by de-energising" does not apply.

The approaches: passive safety where possible — a spring-return actuator, a normally-closed valve, a fail-safe brake that engages when de-energised, so the safe state is the unpowered state by mechanical design. That is much stronger than any electronic solution and should be the first question asked of the mechanical designer.

Where that is not possible: an energy reserve sized to reach the safe state — a capacitor bank or battery with enough stored energy to complete the transition, plus a supervisor that detects supply loss early enough to use it. That reserve must be tested periodically, because a capacitor that has degraded silently is a defeated safety mechanism.

And the design obligation: whatever the mechanism, it must be verifiable in service. A safety feature nobody tests is a safety feature that may not exist.

Listening forPushing toward passive mechanical safety first, and periodic testing of the reserve.

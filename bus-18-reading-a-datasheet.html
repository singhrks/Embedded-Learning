Reading a datasheet and a timing diagram

A datasheet is not a manual and is not meant to be read in order. It is a reference organised by an internal logic that nobody explains, and knowing that logic — plus how to read a timing diagram — is a skill that saves more time than any amount of coding ability.

What is in one, and the order to read it

   THE STANDARD STRUCTURE:

   1. Features / description     marketing. Skim.
   2. Pinout and pin functions   check package variants
   3. Absolute maximum ratings   DESTRUCTION limits
   4. Recommended operating      the ranges you must
      conditions                 actually design within
   5. Electrical characteristics DC: voltages, currents
   6. Timing characteristics     AC: the numbers that
                                 decide your driver
   7. Functional description     how it works
   8. Register map               what you write
   9. Application information    reference schematic
  10. Package / ordering         which part to buy

   THE ORDER TO ACTUALLY READ IT, first time:

   1. BLOCK DIAGRAM (in section 7). Five minutes, and it
      orients everything else.
   2. PIN TABLE. Which pins must be tied, which float,
      which are supplies. Note every pin marked "must be
      connected to ground" - these are usually thermal pads
      or unused supplies and omitting them is fatal.
   3. RECOMMENDED OPERATING CONDITIONS. Not absolute
      maximums - those are where it BREAKS, not where it
      WORKS.
   4. THE INTERFACE TIMING DIAGRAM for your bus.
   5. THE REGISTER MAP, only for the registers you need.
   6. APPLICATION SCHEMATIC. Copy it. The decoupling and
      the pull-ups shown there are requirements, not
      suggestions.

   ABSOLUTE MAXIMUM vs RECOMMENDED, because this confusion
   destroys parts:

     Absolute max VIN:     -0.3 V to VDD + 0.3 V
       Beyond this the part may be damaged. Not "may
       misbehave" - damaged, permanently, possibly slowly.

     Recommended VIH:      0.7 x VDD minimum
       Below this a logic high may not be read as high.

   A 3.3 V signal into a 1.8 V part exceeds absolute maximum
   by 1.2 V. It will appear to work. It will fail in
   weeks.

Reading a timing diagram

   A typical SPI write, with the symbols that matter:

           |<-tCSS->|                    |<-tCSH->|
   CS   ---+        +--------------------+        +-------
           |________|                    |________|
                    :                    :
   SCLK  ___________:__|--|__|--|__|--|__:____________
                    :  ^     ^     ^     :
                    :  |<tSU>|<tH>|     :
   MOSI  XXXXXXXXXXX:XX===X=====X=====XXX:XXXXXXXXXXXX
                    :                    :
   MISO  ----------- ZZZ==X=====X=====ZZZ ------------
                        |<tV>|

   THE FIVE SYMBOLS YOU MEET EVERYWHERE:

   tSU  SETUP TIME. How long data must be STABLE BEFORE the
        clock edge. YOU must guarantee this.

   tH   HOLD TIME. How long data must remain stable AFTER
        the edge. YOU must guarantee this.

   tV   VALID / OUTPUT DELAY. How long AFTER the edge before
        the device's output is valid. THE DEVICE imposes
        this on you, and it is what limits clock speed
        (chapter 04).

   tCSS/tCSH  Chip select setup and hold, around the
        transaction.

   tPD  PROPAGATION DELAY, generally.

   THE ASYMMETRY IS THE KEY INSIGHT:
     - SETUP and HOLD are YOUR obligations to the device.
     - VALID DELAY is the device's obligation to you, and
       it constrains how fast you may clock.

   WAVEFORM NOTATION:
     ===X===   valid data, value irrelevant
     XXXXXX    don't care / undefined
     ZZZZZZ    high impedance, nobody driving
     dashed    optional or conditional

   MIN, TYP, MAX - and which one to design against:

     For times YOU must meet (setup, hold): use the MAX
       required value. Meeting the typical is not enough.
     For times the DEVICE promises (valid delay): use the
       MAX, because that is the worst case you must
       tolerate.
     TYP is for estimating average behaviour. NEVER design
       a timing budget from typical values - the part that
       fails will be the one at the limit, and there will
       be one in every thousand.

A worked timing check

/* QUESTION: can I clock this ADC at 20 MHz?

   FROM THE DATASHEET:
     tV  (SCLK falling to MISO valid)   max 25 ns
     tSU (MISO setup at the MCU)        min  5 ns
     board propagation, both ways       ~2 ns

   AT 20 MHz the period is 50 ns, so half a period - the
   time between the driving edge and the sampling edge - is
   25 ns.

   BUDGET:
     tV                    25 ns
     propagation out+back   2 ns
     MCU setup requirement  5 ns
                          -------
     total                 32 ns   needed
     available             25 ns

   DOES NOT FIT. 32 > 25.

   MAXIMUM WORKABLE CLOCK:
     half period must be >= 32 ns
     period >= 64 ns
     f <= 15.6 MHz

   So 12.5 MHz (the next prescaler step down) is the answer.

   WHAT IF I NEED 20 MHz ANYWAY? Three options, in order of
   preference:
     1. Use the MCU's "sample at the falling edge instead"
        option if it has one - some SPI peripherals allow
        delaying the sample point by half a clock, which
        doubles the available window.
     2. Shorten the traces.
     3. A faster ADC.

   THIS CALCULATION TAKES TWO MINUTES and it is the
   difference between choosing a clock rate by reasoning
   and choosing it by trying values until one works. The
   second approach leaves you at an unknown margin, and the
   failures appear in production across temperature.       */
The three documents, and which answers what

For a microcontroller the information is split across at least three files, and reaching for the wrong one wastes an hour: the datasheet holds pinout, electrical limits, timing and package — anything about a specific part number; the reference manual holds peripheral descriptions and register maps, shared across a whole family; the errata sheet holds the bugs. Read the errata before debugging anything that "should work" — silicon bugs in I2C peripherals, DMA engines and low-power modes are common, documented, and invisible unless you look.

What datasheets do not say plainly

   THINGS THAT ARE THERE BUT EASY TO MISS:

   "This pin must be left unconnected"
     Really means it. Often an internal test or regulator
     node; grounding it can damage the part.

   A CONDITIONS COLUMN on every specification
     "IOL = 4 mA, VDD = 3.0 V, TA = 25 C". Your conditions
     differ, so the number differs. Check the conditions
     before trusting the value.

   "Guaranteed by design" / "not tested"
     This parameter is not measured in production. Treat it
     with more caution than a tested one.

   POWER SEQUENCING
     Often a small note, occasionally a diagram: "VDDIO
     must not exceed VDDCORE by more than 0.3 V during
     ramp". Violating it can latch up the part on every
     power-on, and the symptom is a board that sometimes
     does not start.

   The RESET timing
     Minimum reset pulse width and minimum time after
     release before the first transaction. Both are
     routinely violated by init code that runs too soon.

   FOOTNOTES UNDER THE TABLES
     Genuinely important information lives there:
     "(3) Not available in the 20-pin package."

   THINGS THAT ARE NOT THERE AT ALL, and you must ask the
   vendor or find out empirically:
     - the full register map, on some sensors
     - initialisation sequences (chapter 16's panel blob)
     - real power consumption in your exact use case
     - behaviour outside the specified ranges
     - how the part fails

How it shows up

SymptomCause
Part works for weeks then diesAbsolute maximum exceeded — usually an input above VDD
Works on the bench, fails in productionDesigned against typical rather than worst-case timing
Works at room temperature onlySpecification conditions differ from your operating range
One board in fifty does not startPower sequencing or reset timing violated
Pin has an unexpected functionPackage-variant footnote under the pin table
Peripheral behaves against the manualCheck the errata sheet before your own code
Bus fails above a certain clockOutput valid delay — do the timing budget
Measured current far above the datasheetDifferent conditions: clock, temperature, or a pin loaded

Check yourself

  • Give the order to read a datasheet for the first time and why the block diagram comes first.
  • Distinguish absolute maximum from recommended operating conditions.
  • Define setup, hold and valid delay, and say which are your obligations.
  • Explain why valid delay limits clock speed but setup time does not.
  • Say which of min, typ and max to design against, for each kind of parameter.
  • Work a timing budget to find the maximum clock for a given valid delay.
  • Name the three documents for a microcontroller and what each answers.
  • Explain what "guaranteed by design" implies.
  • Give two things a datasheet often does not tell you at all.
  • Explain why the conditions column matters as much as the value.

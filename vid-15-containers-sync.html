Containers, timestamps and A/V sync

A codec produces a stream of compressed frames with no notion of time, language or what audio goes with them. The container supplies all of that — and the reason lip sync drifts, seeking misses, and files play in one player but not another is almost always here rather than in the codec.

What a container does

   A CONTAINER (or "format", or "wrapper") provides:

     MULTIPLEXING     interleave video, audio, subtitles so a
                      player can read them in one pass without
                      seeking
     TIMING           a timestamp for every packet
     INDEXING         "the keyframe nearest 3:42 is at byte
                      offset N" - what makes seeking possible
     METADATA         title, language, chapters, cover art
     CODEC SETUP      the "extradata" a decoder needs before
                      the first frame: SPS/PPS for H.264,
                      sequence header for AV1

   THE CODEC AND CONTAINER ARE INDEPENDENT. "MP4" says nothing
   about the video codec inside it. A .mp4 can hold H.264,
   HEVC, AV1 or MPEG-4 Part 2; an .mkv can hold anything at
   all. "It is an MP4" is not a statement about
   compatibility.

   AND THE CONFUSION THAT FOLLOWS: a device that "supports
   MP4" means it supports SOME codecs in MP4. Nearly every
   playback failure reported as "this MP4 does not work" is a
   codec, profile or level problem (chapter 14), not a
   container problem.

Timestamps and timebases

/* A timestamp is an INTEGER in a container-defined TIMEBASE.
   It is not seconds.                                       */

    time_in_seconds = timestamp * timebase

  MP4        timebase = 1 / timescale, chosen per track.
             90000 is traditional; 1000 (milliseconds) common.
  MPEG-TS    always 1/90000. PCR at 27 MHz.
  MKV        1/1000000000 by default (nanoseconds).

/* WHY NOT JUST USE SECONDS: 30000/1001 fps (NTSC's 29.97)
   cannot be represented exactly in any decimal. With a
   timescale of 30000 each frame is exactly 1001 ticks, and
   there is no accumulating rounding error. Floating point
   here loses sync over a long file, slowly, in a way that is
   very hard to debug.                                      */

/* TWO TIMESTAMPS PER PACKET (chapter 10): */

    PTS   when to PRESENT it
    DTS   when to DECODE it

  With B-frames, PTS != DTS and DTS may start negative:

    packet:   I     P     B     B     P     B     B
    DTS:     -2    -1     0     1     2     3     4
    PTS:      0     3     1     2     6     4     5

  DTS is always monotonically increasing. PTS is not.
  A muxer that writes PTS where DTS belongs produces a file
  that plays wrong or not at all.

/* EDIT LISTS - MP4's way of saying "start playback at PTS
   0.0666 even though the first sample has PTS 0". Used to
   trim, to compensate for encoder delay, and for that
   negative-DTS offset.

   MANY TOOLS IGNORE THEM, which is why a file can be in sync
   in one player and 66 ms out in another. If audio and video
   are offset by exactly one or two frames, suspect an edit
   list.                                                    */

# Look at the real numbers:
$ ffprobe -select_streams v -show_entries \

packet,0,-1024,0.000000,-0.021333,512

Every sync bug is a timestamp bug

Audio and video are decoded by independent decoders at independent rates. The only thing keeping them together is their timestamps and a common clock. If lip sync drifts, nothing is wrong with either codec — a timestamp is being generated, converted, or ignored incorrectly somewhere in the chain.

How playback actually stays in sync

   THREE POSSIBLE MASTER CLOCKS:

   1. AUDIO MASTER   (what almost everyone uses)
        The audio device consumes samples at a fixed, known
        rate, so its playback position IS a clock. Video is
        presented to match.
        If video falls behind: DROP frames.
        If video runs ahead:   REPEAT or wait.
        WHY: humans notice an audio glitch instantly and a
        dropped video frame barely at all. Audio must never
        stutter, so audio wins.

   2. EXTERNAL CLOCK
        The system clock, or a PCR from a broadcast stream.
        Used when both audio and video must follow a third
        party - live broadcast, or synchronised playback
        across devices.

   3. VIDEO MASTER
        Rare. Used when frame-accurate video matters more than
        audio continuity - editing, frame-stepping.

   THE DRIFT PROBLEM, and it is physical:
     the sound card's crystal runs at 47,999.6 Hz while
     claiming 48,000. That is 8 ppm - about 30 ms per hour.
     Over a two-hour film, a minute of accumulated error if
     nothing corrects it.

     THE FIX: RESAMPLE the audio slightly - 48000 in,
     47999.6 out - so it tracks the master clock. This is
     what every serious player does, and it is inaudible.
     The naive alternative (drop or duplicate a sample block)
     is audible as a click.

   ACCEPTABLE ERROR, from ITU-R BT.1359:
     audio LEADING video:   -40 ms is detectable
     audio LAGGING video:   +60 ms is detectable
     Asymmetric because in the real world sound always
     arrives after light, so we tolerate lag better.
     Aim for +/-20 ms.

Fragmented MP4 and streaming

/* A normal MP4 has one 'moov' box holding the index for the
   entire file. Two consequences:

   1. If moov is at the END (which is where a muxer naturally
      writes it, since it needs the whole file first), a
      player must download the whole file before it can start.
      -> +faststart moves it to the front. THIS IS THE MOST
         COMMON MP4 MISTAKE.                                */

$ ffmpeg -i in.mov -c copy -movflags +faststart out.mp4

/*  2. It cannot be produced live, because you do not know
       the index until you are finished.
       -> FRAGMENTED MP4: a small moov with no sample table,
          then a sequence of (moof + mdat) fragments, each
          self-describing.                                  */

    [ftyp][moov]  [moof][mdat] [moof][mdat] [moof][mdat] ...
                  \___________/
                   one fragment - independently decodable if
                   it starts with a keyframe

$ ffmpeg -i in.mp4 -c copy \
    -movflags "frag_keyframe+empty_moov+default_base_moof" \
    out_frag.mp4

/* CMAF is fMP4 with a specific set of constraints, so that
   ONE set of segments serves both HLS and DASH. Before CMAF
   you encoded twice - MPEG-TS for HLS, fMP4 for DASH - and
   stored both. Now you store one.

   THE REQUIREMENTS for segmented streaming, all of which
   must hold (chapters 10 and 13):
     * closed GOP, keyframe at every segment boundary
     * segment duration an exact multiple of the GOP
     * identical timestamps across every rendition
     * scene-cut keyframes DISABLED, or alignment breaks   */

# Verify segments really do start with a keyframe:
$ for s in seg_*.m4s; do
    printf "%s " $s
    ffprobe -v error -select_streams v -read_intervals "%+#1" \
      -show_entries frame=key_frame -of csv=p=0 "$s"
  done
seg_001.m4s 1
seg_002.m4s 1
seg_003.m4s 1        # all 1 = correct. Any 0 = a broken
                     # switch point.
Variable frame rate breaks assumptions everywhere

Screen recordings, phone cameras and browser capture routinely produce VFR: frames arrive when something changed, so durations vary. Tools that assume constant frame rate — many editors, naive muxers, anything computing frame_number / fps — will produce drifting sync. Convert to CFR at ingest with -vsync cfr -r 30, or carry the real timestamps end to end. Choosing halfway produces a file that is subtly wrong and very hard to diagnose.

Diagnosing sync

# 1. Are the streams offset at the start?
$ ffprobe -show_entries stream=index,codec_type,start_time \
    -of csv input.mp4
stream,0,video,0.000000
stream,1,audio,0.021333        # audio starts 21 ms later

# A small offset is normal - encoder delay. A large one, or
# one that GROWS, is the bug.

# 2. Does the gap grow over time? This distinguishes a fixed
#    offset from drift.
$ ffprobe -select_streams v -show_entries packet=pts_time \
    -of csv=p=0 input.mp4 | tail -1
7200.033333
$ ffprobe -select_streams a -show_entries packet=pts_time \
    -of csv=p=0 input.mp4 | tail -1
7203.115000          # 3 SECONDS out after 2 hours -> drift,
                     # not an offset. Suspect a clock or a
                     # wrong timebase.

# 3. Fix a fixed offset:
$ ffmpeg -i in.mp4 -itsoffset 0.15 -i in.mp4 \
    -map 0:v -map 1:a -c copy fixed.mp4

# 4. Check for VFR:
$ ffprobe -select_streams v -show_entries frame=pkt_duration_time \
    -of csv=p=0 in.mp4 | sort | uniq -c | sort -rn | head
   4021 0.033333
    118 0.041667        # mixed durations = VFR
     37 0.025000

# 5. The empirical test that beats all of the above: encode a
#    clapperboard. A visible flash and a simultaneous click,
#    then step through frame by frame and count.
$ ffmpeg -f lavfi -i "testsrc=size=640x480:rate=30" \
         -f lavfi -i "sine=frequency=1000:duration=10" \
         -t 10 synctest.mp4

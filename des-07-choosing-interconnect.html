Choosing interconnects and buses

Every line on a block diagram is a decision with a cost, and an unlabelled line is a decision you have not made. This chapter is how to choose, and how to defend the choice with the three numbers that actually decide it.

Three numbers decide it

   1. RATE       bytes per second, sustained and peak
   2. DISTANCE   centimetres on one board, or metres
                 between enclosures
   3. TOPOLOGY   two devices, or many? who initiates?

   Everything else - pin count, cost, complexity - is a
   tiebreak once those three have eliminated the
   impossible options.

   THE DISTANCE CUT IS THE SHARPEST:

     < 30 cm, one board   SPI, I2C, parallel, MIPI
     ~1-15 m              UART, USB, Ethernet
     < 1 km               RS-485, CAN, Ethernet
     unbounded            wireless, or fibre

   I2C AND SPI ARE CHIP-TO-CHIP BUSES. Running them
   between boards, through a connector, or down a cable is
   the single most common interconnect mistake, and it
   produces intermittent faults that get blamed on
   software for weeks. If the answer to "how far?" is
   "another enclosure", those two are already eliminated.

Count the interrupt lines before claiming I2C saves pins

I2C's two-wire claim assumes no interrupt lines and no address conflicts. SPI and I2C slaves cannot initiate, so a sensor with new data needs a separate INT pin. Three interrupt-driven sensors on I2C is 2 + 3 = 5 pins against SPI's 3 + 3 = 6 — and if two of them share a fixed address you also need a multiplexer, which costs a part, board area and two more wires. Do this arithmetic with the actual devices before the schematic is committed, not after.

The interconnect inside a system

WHEN TWO PROCESSORS SHARE A BOARD, the link between them is
a real design decision with a protocol you must define.

  SPI          fastest simple option; the AP is master, so
               the MCU cannot initiate - add an INT line so
               it can say "I have something".
  UART         simplest, bidirectional, symmetric; either
               side can start talking. Slower.
  SHARED RAM   if the parts support it (some SoCs have a
               mailbox and shared SRAM). Fastest, and needs
               careful cache and barrier handling.
  USB          if one side is naturally a host; brings
               enumeration and class drivers.
  ETHERNET     over-the-top for two chips on one board, but
               right if they might later be separate boxes.

WHATEVER YOU CHOOSE, THE PROTOCOL ON TOP IS THE REAL WORK:
  - framing: how does the receiver find message boundaries?
    (length prefix, delimiter, or a timing gap)
  - integrity: a CRC, because none of SPI, UART or shared
    memory gives you one
  - flow control: what happens when one side is slower?
  - versioning: the two firmwares WILL be updated
    separately at some point, so the protocol needs a
    version field from day one
  - liveness: how does each side know the other is alive?

THE VERSIONING POINT IS THE ONE CANDIDATES MISS, and it is
the one that causes real field problems: an update that
changes the message format and bricks the pair because
they can no longer talk.

Questions

You need to connect a sensor board 3 metres away from the main board. What do you use?Mid

Not I2C or SPI — those are chip-to-chip buses. At 3 metres you are outside I2C's 400 pF capacitance budget (a metre of ribbon is 50–100 pF, plus connectors), and SPI's timing budget will not survive the round trip at any useful clock.

The options: RS-485 if you want a simple multi-drop digital link — differential, so it rejects common-mode noise, and it reaches a kilometre. CAN if you want error detection, automatic retry and multiple nodes with priority. Ethernet if the sensor board is complex enough to warrant it or you need routability. UART with a differential transceiver for the simplest case.

If the answer must be I2C — because the sensor only speaks I2C — then put a small MCU on the sensor board that talks I2C locally and RS-485 over the cable, or use a bus extender such as a P82B715. Never just run the I2C down the cable and hope.

Listening forEliminating I2C/SPI by distance immediately, and the local-MCU bridge as the pragmatic fix.
What must a protocol between two processors on one board include?Senior

Framing (how the receiver finds message boundaries — a length prefix, a delimiter with escaping, or a timing gap); a CRC, because none of SPI, UART or shared memory provides integrity checking; flow control or at least a bounded queue with a defined overflow policy; a version field; a liveness mechanism so each side knows the other is alive; and a defined behaviour on resynchronisation after an error.

The version field is the one that matters most in the field and is most often omitted. The two firmwares will be updated separately at some point — an OTA that updates the application MCU but not the safety MCU, or a service replacement of one board. Without a version negotiation, a format change bricks the pair. With one, the newer side can speak the older dialect or refuse cleanly and report why.

Second most-forgotten: what happens when one side reboots. The other must detect it, reset its own state machine, and not deliver a message assembled from two different sessions.

Listening forVersioning and reboot handling — these separate people who have maintained a two-processor product from those who have built one.
Why does an SPI link work at 1 MHz and fail at 20 MHz?Mid

A round-trip timing budget, not a software problem. The master drives a clock edge; the signal propagates to the slave; the slave takes its output-valid time (tV, often 20–40 ns) to put data on MISO; that propagates back; and it must arrive before the master's setup time at the next sampling edge. All of that must fit inside half a clock period.

With tV = 30 ns and a couple of nanoseconds of propagation, half a period must exceed ~35 ns, so the maximum clock is around 14 MHz regardless of what the master can generate. Add a connector or 10 cm of ribbon and it falls further.

How to confirm: halve the clock and see if the errors vanish — that immediately distinguishes timing from logic. Then check the slave's tV in its datasheet, and check whether your MCU offers a sample-delay option, which effectively doubles the available window.

Listening forOutput valid delay as the binding term, and halving the clock as the discriminating experiment.
When would you choose CAN over RS-485?Mid

When you want the error handling and arbitration for free. CAN gives you: non-destructive arbitration, so several nodes can start transmitting and the highest-priority message completes without any bandwidth being lost; a 15-bit CRC with automatic hardware retry; error confinement, so a faulty node removes itself from the bus rather than destroying all traffic; and acknowledgement from any receiver.

RS-485 gives you a differential physical layer and nothing else — no arbitration, so the protocol above must guarantee only one transmitter at a time (which is why Modbus is strictly master-polls-slave), and no error detection beyond whatever CRC you add yourself.

Choose RS-485 when the topology is naturally one master polling slaves, when you want the simplest possible implementation, or when you need higher raw throughput over long distance. Choose CAN when nodes must speak autonomously, when message priority matters, or when the environment is electrically hostile and you want the retry behaviour — which is exactly why vehicles use it.

Listening forNon-destructive arbitration as CAN's distinguishing property, not just "CAN is for cars".
A design has an I2C sensor that occasionally locks the bus. How do you handle it?Mid

Design for it rather than hoping. The failure: the master resets mid-read while the slave is transmitting a zero bit, so the slave holds SDA low waiting for clocks that never come. SDA is stuck, and the master cannot even generate a START because that requires SDA to fall — and it is already low.

The recovery, from the I2C specification: switch SCL and SDA to open-drain GPIO, clock SCL up to nine times while watching SDA, and once SDA is released generate a manual STOP. Run this unconditionally at every boot, not only after a failure — it costs 100 µs and eliminates the entire "only works after a power cycle" class of bug.

Then: every I2C wait needs a timeout, every timeout calls recovery, and every recovery failure is counted. And if recovery does not free the bus, the slave is crashed rather than mid-byte — which is why a power gate on the sensor's rail is worth its few cents, since that is the only remaining fix.

Listening forBoot-time recovery as unconditional, and the power gate as the escalation.
How do you decide whether a link needs a CRC?Mid

Ask what the link's own error detection is, and what a corrupted message would cause. SPI has none. I2C has per-byte acknowledgement but no data integrity — a flipped bit arrives as valid data with a valid ACK. UART has framing and parity, which catch some single-bit errors and miss bursts. CAN and Ethernet have strong CRCs already, so adding another is usually redundant.

So: on SPI, I2C and raw UART, add a CRC to any message whose corruption matters. CRC-16 is cheap (a table or a hardware unit on many MCUs) and catches all single and double bit errors, all odd numbers of errors, and all bursts up to 16 bits.

The consequence question is the important half: if a corrupted sensor reading just produces one wrong data point that is smoothed away, a CRC may not be worth it. If it produces a command that moves an actuator, it absolutely is — and you may want an acknowledgement and a sequence number as well, so a lost or duplicated command is detectable.

Listening forReasoning from consequence rather than adding a CRC reflexively.
What is the cost of adding USB to a product?Mid

More than the connector. You need an accurate clock — full speed requires 0.25%, so an internal RC oscillator is usually insufficient unless the part has crystal-less USB that trims against the host's frames; that means a crystal whose frequency divides to exactly 48 MHz. You need controlled-impedance differential routing (90 Ω, length-matched, no stubs), which constrains the PCB. You need a stack — use TinyUSB or the vendor's, never your own — and descriptors that are correct enough for Windows, which is less forgiving than Linux.

Then the product questions: which class? Choosing a standard class (CDC, HID, MSC) means every operating system already has the driver, which is worth a great deal. A vendor-specific interface needs WinUSB descriptors to avoid shipping a signed driver. And a Vendor ID from the USB-IF costs several thousand dollars if you want to use the logo and avoid collisions.

The benefit that usually justifies it: universal connectivity, power delivery, and a debugging and provisioning path on the production line.

Listening forThe clock accuracy requirement and the class choice — both are practical gates people hit late.
Your block diagram has a line between two blocks. What must the label say?Junior

Which bus, at what rate, who is master, and who initiates. "I2C" is not a label; "I2C1, 400 kHz, MCU is master, sensor asserts INT on GPIO17 when a sample is ready" is.

That level of detail forces the decisions that matter: the rate forces you to check the bus can carry the data; naming the master forces you to notice that the slave cannot initiate and therefore needs an interrupt line, which costs a pin; and stating the initiator determines whether the CPU can sleep between events or must poll.

An unlabelled line in a design round reads as a decision not yet made, and interviewers will ask about exactly those lines.

Listening forThe initiator question, because it is the one that decides whether the system can sleep.
How much bandwidth does an audio path need, and does that change the bus choice?Junior

Compute it: sample rate × channels × bytes per sample. Telephony-quality 8 kHz mono 16-bit is 16 kB/s; CD-quality 44.1 kHz stereo 16-bit is 176 kB/s; 48 kHz stereo 24-bit is 288 kB/s.

None of those trouble SPI or I2S. What they do trouble is per-byte interrupt handling — at 176 kB/s with 30 cycles of overhead per byte you are burning about 5 MHz of a core doing nothing but copying, plus 176,000 interrupts per second. So the bus choice is easy and the transfer mechanism is the real decision: DMA with double buffering, so the CPU handles two interrupts per buffer rather than one per sample.

The other consequence is that audio has a hard deadline — a buffer must be refilled before it drains or you get an audible click — so it belongs in the real-time budget with a defined worst-case latency, not treated as best-effort throughput.

Listening forSeparating the bus question from the transfer-mechanism question, and noticing the hard deadline.
When would you put a bus in a design purely for debugging or manufacturing?Senior

Almost always, and it is one of the cheapest good decisions available. A UART brought to a test pad or a tag-connect footprint costs two pins and gives you a console during bring-up, a diagnostic channel for a returned unit, and a provisioning path on the production line for writing serial numbers, calibration constants and keys.

Likewise SWD or JTAG: without it, a board that will not boot is unrecoverable and you learn nothing. And a sense resistor in the battery path so the line can measure sleep current as a pass/fail test.

The design discipline is to decide at schematic time what the production test needs to do — every unit must be tested, and the test has a cost per unit and a cycle time — and then to make sure the hardware supports it. Retrofitting is impossible.

The counter-consideration is security: a debug port left enabled in production is an attack surface, so the design should include disabling it (or requiring authentication) as part of the provisioning step, while keeping the pads for factory use.

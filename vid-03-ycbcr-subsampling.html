YCbCr and chroma subsampling

The first and largest compression step in every video pipeline happens before the codec runs: separate brightness from colour, then throw away three quarters of the colour. It halves the data for almost no visible loss — and it is why red text on video looks smeared.

Why separate luma from chroma

   The eye has ~6 million cones and ~120 million rods, and the
   cones are concentrated in the fovea. Spatial acuity for
   BRIGHTNESS is several times higher than for COLOUR.

   So: transform RGB into one brightness channel and two
   colour-difference channels, and then store the colour ones
   at lower resolution.

     Y   luma          brightness. Keep at full resolution.
     Cb  blue-difference   B - Y, scaled
     Cr  red-difference    R - Y, scaled

   In 4:2:0 this saves 50% of the data before any codec has
   done anything, and in a blind test almost nobody sees it on
   natural imagery.

   IT ALSO DECORRELATES. R, G and B in a photograph are highly
   correlated - a bright pixel is bright in all three. Y, Cb
   and Cr are much less so, and a transform codec compresses
   uncorrelated channels far better (chapter 6).

   NOTE THE NAMES, because people use them interchangeably and
   they are not the same:
     Y'CbCr   digital, gamma-encoded. What video uses.
              The prime means "non-linear".
     YUV      strictly the analogue PAL/SECAM version.
              Universally misused to mean Y'CbCr. Every API
              you will meet says "YUV" and means Y'CbCr.
     YCoCg    a cheaper, reversible variant used in some
              codecs and GPU texture formats.
     Luma vs LUMINANCE: luma (Y') is computed from
              gamma-encoded RGB; luminance (Y) is a linear
              light quantity. They are not the same thing, and
              conflating them is why some "greyscale"
              conversions look wrong.

The matrix, and why there are several

/* BT.709 (HD), full range, gamma-encoded R'G'B' in [0,1]. */

    Y' =  0.2126 R' + 0.7152 G' + 0.0722 B'
    Cb = (B' - Y') / 1.8556
    Cr = (R' - Y') / 1.5748

/* Note the coefficients sum to 1 and green dominates - the
   eye is most sensitive there. */

/* BT.601 (SD) uses DIFFERENT coefficients: */
    Y' =  0.299  R' + 0.587  G' + 0.114  B'

/* BT.2020 (UHD): */
    Y' =  0.2627 R' + 0.6780 G' + 0.0593 B'

/* USING THE WRONG ONE produces a colour shift that is subtle
   on most content and obvious on saturated reds and greens -
   faces go slightly wrong, skies shift. It is the classic
   "why does my SD clip look off after upscaling" bug
   (chapter 2).                                             */

/* THE INTEGER FORM, which is what code actually runs.
   8-bit, LIMITED range: Y in [16,235], Cb/Cr in [16,240]
   with 128 as neutral.                                     */

static inline void rgb_to_ycbcr_bt709(uint8_t r, uint8_t g, uint8_t b,
                                      uint8_t *y, uint8_t *cb, uint8_t *cr)
{
        /* fixed point, 16-bit fractions, limited range */
        int Y  = ( 4735 * r + 15934 * g +  1608 * b + 32768) >> 16;
        int Cb = (-2607 * r -  8773 * g + 11380 * b + 32768) >> 16;
        int Cr = (11380 * r - 10336 * g -  1044 * b + 32768) >> 16;

        *y  = (uint8_t)clamp(Y  + 16,  16, 235);
        *cb = (uint8_t)clamp(Cb + 128, 16, 240);
        *cr = (uint8_t)clamp(Cr + 128, 16, 240);
}

/* THE FOUR THINGS THAT MUST AGREE between encoder and
   decoder, or the colour is wrong:
     1. the matrix     (601 / 709 / 2020)
     2. the range      (limited / full)
     3. the transfer   (chapter 2)
     4. the primaries  (chapter 2)
   All four are carried in the bitstream's VUI, and all four
   are routinely missing.                                    */

The subsampling notation

   J : a : b   over a conceptual 4x2 block of pixels

     J = width of the region                    (always 4)
     a = number of chroma samples in the TOP row
     b = number of chroma CHANGES in the BOTTOM row
         (b = 0 means "same as the top row", i.e. vertical
          subsampling too)

   4:4:4     no subsampling. Chroma at full resolution.
       Y Y Y Y        C C C C
       Y Y Y Y        C C C C
       -> 24 bits/pixel at 8-bit. Screen capture, graphics,
          professional capture, chroma keying.

   4:2:2     horizontal only. Half the chroma columns.
       Y Y Y Y        C   C
       Y Y Y Y        C   C
       -> 16 bits/pixel. Broadcast, editing formats, most
          capture cards. Survives colour correction well.

   4:2:0     horizontal AND vertical. A quarter the chroma.
       Y Y Y Y        C   C
       Y Y Y Y        (shared with the row above)
       -> 12 bits/pixel. H.264/HEVC/AV1 baseline, every
          streaming service, every phone camera. THE default.

   4:1:1     quarter horizontal, full vertical. DV, NTSC
             legacy. Rare now.

   THE SAVING
     4:4:4 -> 4:2:2   33% less data
     4:4:4 -> 4:2:0   50% less data
   before the codec has compressed anything at all.
Chroma siting is a real thing and it is why formats disagree

When you keep one chroma sample for four luma samples, where does it sit? MPEG-2 and H.264 place it left-aligned, vertically centred between the two rows. JPEG and MPEG-1 place it in the centre of the 2×2 block. Getting it wrong shifts colour half a pixel relative to luma — invisible on photographs, clearly visible as coloured fringes on sharp graphics and text. It is signalled by chroma_sample_loc_type, and most converters ignore it.

What subsampling actually costs

   4:2:0 IS NEARLY FREE ON NATURAL IMAGES and expensive on
   everything else.

   FINE ON              BAD ON
     photographs          text and UI, especially coloured
     faces, skin          thin saturated lines
     natural scenes       screen recordings
     film                 chroma keying / green screen
                          anything to be colour graded
                          red and blue detail specifically

   THE RED TEXT PROBLEM, concretely:
     red is almost entirely in Cr. Subsample Cr by 2 in each
     direction and a one-pixel red line becomes a two-pixel
     smear with dark edges. This is why:
       * a screen recording of a terminal looks soft
       * subtitles burned in before encoding look worse than
         subtitles rendered by the player
       * you record screencasts at 4:4:4 if you can, and
         upscale the resolution if you cannot

   THE GENERATION-LOSS PROBLEM:
     decode 4:2:0 -> edit -> re-encode 4:2:0 subsamples
     again, on data that was already interpolated. Colour
     detail degrades every pass. Editing formats use 4:2:2 or
     4:4:4 for exactly this reason, and you only go to 4:2:0
     on the final delivery encode.

   CHROMA KEYING on 4:2:0 gives ragged edges, because the key
   is computed from chroma that only exists at half
   resolution. Broadcast shoots 4:2:2 minimum, often 4:4:4.

Converting, correctly

# The command lines that actually matter, and the flags people
# leave out.

# Encode 4:4:4 - for screen content, if your decoder supports it
$ ffmpeg -i in.mov -c:v libx264 -profile:v high444 \
         -pix_fmt yuv444p -crf 18 out.mp4
# NOTE: many hardware decoders and most browsers do NOT
# support 4:4:4 H.264. Check your target before shipping it.

# The safe compromise for screen content: 4:2:0 at double
# resolution, downscaled on playback. Ugly, and it works.

# Convert 4:2:0 -> 4:2:2 for editing, with a good chroma
# upsampler rather than nearest-neighbour:
$ ffmpeg -i in.mp4 -vf "scale=in_color_matrix=bt709:\
out_color_matrix=bt709,format=yuv422p10le" -c:v prores_ks out.mov

# TAG IT. An untagged file will be guessed at (chapter 2):
$ ffmpeg -i in.y4m -c:v libx264 \
    -color_primaries bt709 -color_trc bt709 \
    -colorspace bt709 -color_range tv \
    -pix_fmt yuv420p out.mp4

# Inspect what you produced:
$ ffprobe -show_streams -select_streams v:0 out.mp4 | \
    grep -E 'pix_fmt|color_'
pix_fmt=yuv420p
color_range=tv
color_space=bt709
color_transfer=bt709
color_primaries=bt709

# THE CHROMA UPSAMPLER MATTERS. ffmpeg's default swscale
# chroma interpolation is fast and mediocre; -sws_flags
# +full_chroma_int+accurate_rnd is measurably better for
# anything you will look at closely.
Do not colour-correct 4:2:0 footage aggressively

Chroma only exists at quarter resolution, so any operation that amplifies colour — saturation boosts, secondary colour keys, heavy grading — amplifies the interpolation artefacts too, producing blocky colour patches that were invisible in the original. If the material will be graded, capture 4:2:2 or better. This is not a preference; it is the reason professional formats exist.

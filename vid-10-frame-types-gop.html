Frame types, GOPs and reference management

Once frames predict from other frames, order matters — and the order they are stored in is not the order they are shown. Every seeking bug, every "the first two seconds are broken" streaming complaint, and the whole design of adaptive streaming comes out of this chapter.

The frame types

   I-FRAME   Intra. Coded from itself alone, like a JPEG.
             Large - 5 to 20x a P-frame. Decodable with no
             other frame.

   P-FRAME   Predicted. References EARLIER frames.
             Small. Useless without its references.

   B-FRAME   Bi-predicted. References earlier AND later
             frames, and can average the two.
             Smallest - often half a P-frame.

   AND THE ONE THAT MATTERS OPERATIONALLY:

   IDR       Instantaneous Decoder Refresh. An I-frame that
             also RESETS the reference list - nothing after
             it may reference anything before it.

   A PLAIN I-FRAME IS NOT A SEEK POINT. Later frames may
   still reference frames from before it, so starting there
   gives you garbage. Only an IDR (or an HEVC CRA/BLA, or an
   AV1 keyframe) is a guaranteed entry point.

   This distinction is the source of a great many bugs. When
   someone says "I set keyint=250 but seeking is still
   broken", they emitted I-frames and not IDRs.

     $ ffmpeg ... -x264-params "keyint=48:min-keyint=48:\
       scenecut=0:open-gop=0"
                                        ^^^^^^^^^^^ closed GOP
                                        forces IDR

Display order versus decode order

   A B-frame needs a FUTURE frame, so that future frame must
   be DECODED FIRST. The bitstream is reordered.

   DISPLAY ORDER (what the viewer sees)
     I0   B1   B2   P3   B4   B5   P6
      |    ^    ^   ^     ^    ^    ^
      |    |    |   |     |    |    |
      +----+----+---+     +----+----+
        B1,B2 need I0 AND P3

   DECODE ORDER (what is in the file)
     I0   P3   B1   B2   P6   B4   B5

   P3 arrives before the B-frames that need it.

   THE CONSEQUENCES

   1. TWO TIMESTAMPS PER FRAME
        PTS  Presentation Time Stamp - when to show it
        DTS  Decode Time Stamp       - when to decode it
      For B-frames, PTS != DTS, and DTS can even be negative
      at the start of a stream. Container muxers must carry
      both (chapter 15).

   2. LATENCY. The decoder cannot output frame B1 until it
      has decoded P3. With 3 B-frames that is 4 frames of
      structural delay - 133 ms at 30 fps. THIS IS WHY
      VIDEOCONFERENCING DISABLES B-FRAMES ENTIRELY.

   3. A REORDER BUFFER is required, sized by
      max_num_reorder_frames in the stream. Getting it wrong
      gives you frames displayed out of order - a stutter
      that looks like dropped frames but is not.

   4. Seeking must land on an IDR and decode forward to the
      target, discarding output until the PTS matches.
Why B-frames are so cheap

Two reasons, and the second is the underrated one. First, occlusion: something hidden in the past frame may be visible in the future one, so bi-prediction finds a match where forward prediction cannot. Second, averaging two references halves the noise — the prediction is closer to the truth than either reference alone, so the residual is smaller. That is why a B-frame is often half the size of a P-frame even on content with simple motion.

GOP structure

/* A Group Of Pictures runs from one keyframe to the next. */

  IBBPBBPBBPBBI...           classic MPEG-2, GOP of 12
  IBBBPBBBPBBBP...           H.264, 3 B-frames
  I P P P P P P P...         low latency, no B-frames

/* CLOSED vs OPEN GOP

     CLOSED  no frame references across the boundary. Every
             GOP is independently decodable. Slightly less
             efficient; MANDATORY for adaptive streaming,
             because you must be able to splice segments.

     OPEN    B-frames at the start of a GOP may reference the
             previous GOP's last P-frame. A little more
             efficient, and it breaks clean splicing.

   Streaming: ALWAYS closed, with the GOP length equal to the
   segment length.

/* GOP LENGTH is a three-way trade:

     SHORT (1-2 s)     fast seeking, quick channel change,
                       fast recovery from packet loss, and
                       cheap ABR switching.
                       COSTS BITS - I-frames are expensive.

     LONG (5-10 s)     better compression, 10-20% smaller at
                       the same quality.
                       Slow seek, slow start, and a lost
                       packet corrupts until the next
                       keyframe.

   TYPICAL CHOICES
     streaming VOD        2 s, matching the segment
     live streaming       1-2 s (latency)
     broadcast            0.5-1 s (channel change)
     videoconference      infinite, with periodic refresh
                          instead (see below)
     archival             10 s+, or all-intra for editing

/* SCENE-CUT DETECTION: a good encoder inserts a keyframe at a
   cut anyway, because prediction across a cut is useless.
   Forcing a fixed interval AND allowing scene cuts gives you
   both - but for segmented streaming you must disable scene
   cuts so segment boundaries stay aligned:
     -x264-params "keyint=48:min-keyint=48:scenecut=0"        */

Reference management

   H.264 onward keep a DECODED PICTURE BUFFER of several
   frames, and each block chooses which one to predict from.

     DPB, up to 16 frames:
       [ I0 ] [ P3 ] [ P6 ] [ P9 ] ...

     block A -> reference index 0 (the most recent)
     block B -> reference index 2 (three frames back)

   WHY MULTIPLE REFERENCES HELP
     * periodic motion - a walking figure, a rotating fan -
       matches a frame several back better than the last one
     * occlusion - something briefly hidden reappears
     * noise - an older, cleaner frame may match better
     * a flash or a dropped frame is skipped over

   Typical gain: 5-10% for 4 references over 1. Diminishing
   returns beyond that, and the DPB costs MEMORY -
   16 references at 4K 10-bit is about 400 MB, which is why
   levels cap it.

   HIERARCHICAL B-FRAMES / PYRAMID - the modern structure:

     display:  I0  b1  B2  b3  P4
     decode:   I0  P4  B2  b1  b3

     temporal layer 0:  I0 ........... P4     never discarded
     temporal layer 1:        B2             references L0
     temporal layer 2:    b1     b3          references L0,L1
                          ^^     ^^
                     lowercase b = NOT used as a reference

   THIS GIVES YOU TEMPORAL SCALABILITY FOR FREE: drop layer 2
   and you have a valid 15 fps stream. Drop layer 1 too and
   you have 7.5 fps. Used by:
     * videoconferencing, to shed frame rate under congestion
       (SVC), without re-encoding
     * streaming, for trick modes
     * and it compresses BETTER than a flat IBBP structure,
       because the layer-0 frames are coded at higher quality
       and everything else predicts from them.

   LONG-TERM REFERENCES: pin a frame in the DPB indefinitely -
   a clean background, or the last known-good frame. WebRTC
   uses these to recover from loss without a full keyframe.

Low latency, and intra refresh

/* Videoconferencing cannot afford B-frames (reordering
   latency) or periodic I-frames (a bitrate spike that blows
   the buffer and causes a stall).

   INTRA REFRESH solves the second: instead of one huge
   I-frame, refresh a COLUMN of blocks each frame. After N
   frames the whole picture has been refreshed.             */

     frame 0:  [I][ ][ ][ ][ ]     I = intra column
     frame 1:  [ ][I][ ][ ][ ]
     frame 2:  [ ][ ][I][ ][ ]
     frame 3:  [ ][ ][ ][I][ ]
     frame 4:  [ ][ ][ ][ ][I]     fully refreshed

   Plus a constraint: blocks may not predict from the
   not-yet-refreshed region, or the refresh is undone.

   RESULT: every frame is roughly the same size - no spikes,
   no buffer stalls - and a decoder joining at any point has
   a correct picture after N frames.

$ ffmpeg ... -c:v libx264 -tune zerolatency \
    -x264-params "intra-refresh=1:bframes=0:sliced-threads=1"

/* -tune zerolatency sets, among other things:
     bframes=0            no reorder delay
     sync-lookahead=0     no lookahead buffering
     sliced-threads=1     threads work within a frame, not
                          across frames - frame-level
                          threading adds N frames of latency
     rc-lookahead=0

   THE COST: roughly 10-20% worse compression. That is the
   price of the latency, and it is not negotiable.        */
Frame-threading is a hidden source of latency

By default x264 and x265 use frame-level threading: thread N works on frame N while thread N+1 works on frame N+1. With 8 threads that is up to 8 frames of encoder delay — a third of a second at 25 fps — before a single byte emerges. Any real-time use needs sliced-threads or an explicit thread limit. This surprises people who have already disabled B-frames and cannot work out where the latency is coming from.

# Are they real IDRs? Look at the NAL types:

How it shows up

SymptomCause
Seeking lands on a broken frameI-frames but not IDRs — use a closed GOP
First seconds of a stream are garbageSPS/PPS not repeated before each keyframe
Frames displayed out of orderReorder buffer too small, or PTS/DTS mishandled
Video and audio drift apartPTS used where DTS was needed, or vice versa (chapter 15)
133 ms of unexplained latencyB-frames — disable them for real-time
Latency persists after disabling B-framesFrame-level threading; use sliced-threads
Bitrate spikes every GOP, buffer stallsPeriodic I-frames; use intra refresh
ABR switching produces a glitchGOP boundaries not aligned across renditions, or an open GOP
Packet loss corrupts several secondsLong GOP — nothing recovers until the next keyframe
4K decode runs out of memoryDPB size — 16 references at 4K is hundreds of megabytes

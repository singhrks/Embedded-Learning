Signal integrity for firmware engineers

You will not design the board, but you will be handed bugs that live in the copper, and you need to recognise them before spending a week in the debugger. This chapter is the minimum electrical model that lets you say "this is not a software problem" with evidence.

It is the edge rate, not the clock rate

   The intuition that misleads everyone: "this bus is only
   100 kHz, so signal integrity does not apply."

   IT IS THE RISE TIME THAT MATTERS, NOT THE FREQUENCY. A
   100 kHz square wave with a 1 ns edge contains energy up
   to hundreds of megahertz - and it is that energy which
   radiates, reflects and couples.

     f_knee ~ 0.5 / t_rise

     t_rise = 1 ns   ->  f_knee = 500 MHz
     t_rise = 10 ns  ->  f_knee =  50 MHz

   WHEN IS A TRACE A TRANSMISSION LINE?

     When its propagation delay exceeds about a sixth of
     the rise time.

     Signals travel at roughly 15 cm/ns on FR4.

     t_rise = 1 ns   -> critical length ~2.5 cm
     t_rise = 5 ns   -> critical length ~12 cm

   So a 1 ns edge on a 5 cm trace IS a transmission line and
   will reflect, whatever the clock rate. THIS IS WHY SLEW
   RATE CONTROL EXISTS on GPIO pins (mcu chapter 10), and
   why setting everything to maximum speed causes problems
   that setting it to minimum does not.

   THE FIRMWARE LEVER: you control the slew rate setting.
   Use the slowest that meets timing. It costs nothing and
   it removes an entire class of problem.

Reflections and termination

   A signal reaching an impedance change reflects part of
   its energy back.

     reflection coefficient = (Z_load - Z_line) /
                              (Z_load + Z_line)

   OPEN END (Z_load infinite): coefficient = +1, the wave
   reflects entirely and ADDS - overshoot, then ringing as
   it bounces.

     ideal            actual
      ___              _  _
     |                | \/ \____
     |                |
   __|              __|

   Overshoot above VDD + 0.3 V exceeds absolute maximum
   (chapter 18) and stresses the input protection diodes.
   Ringing that crosses the input threshold several times
   gives you MULTIPLE CLOCK EDGES from one transition -
   which a counter or a shift register will faithfully
   count.

   THE FIRMWARE-VISIBLE SYMPTOM: an SPI device that
   occasionally reads one bit too many, or a counter that
   increments by two. Before assuming your code is wrong,
   put a scope on the clock.

   SERIES TERMINATION is the usual fix and the one you will
   see on schematics: a resistor at the SOURCE, sized so
   R_series + R_driver = Z_line (typically 50 ohms, so a
   22-33 ohm resistor).

     driver --[33R]--------------------- receiver
              ^
        absorbs the reflection on its return

   If a bus is marginal and there are footprints for series
   resistors that were left at 0 ohms, fitting the right
   value is often the whole fix.

Ground is the part nobody thinks about

   CURRENT FLOWS IN LOOPS. The return path is as real as
   the outgoing signal, and it takes the path of LEAST
   INDUCTANCE - which at speed is directly under the trace,
   not the shortest path on the plane.

   GOOD:                    BAD:
   +----------+             +----------+
   |  signal  |             |  signal  |
   +==========+             +==========+
   |  ground  |             |   SPLIT  |  <- the return
   |  plane   |             |  ______  |     current must
   +----------+             +----------+     detour around

   A split in the ground plane under a fast signal forces
   the return current to detour, creating a large loop that
   radiates and picks up noise. This is the single most
   common cause of a board that fails EMC testing.

   FOR FIRMWARE ENGINEERS, THE PRACTICAL VERSIONS:

   1. CONNECT THE GROUNDS. Two boards linked by a UART with
      no shared ground reference do not have a defined
      signal level. This is the number one cause of
      "serial works on the desk, not in the rack".

   2. SCOPE PROBE GROUNDING. The long ground lead on a
      probe is an inductive loop that ADDS ringing to what
      you measure. If you see 1 GHz ringing on a 1 MHz
      signal, suspect the probe before the board - use the
      short ground spring.

   3. DECOUPLING IS A GROUND ISSUE. A 100 nF capacitor next
      to every supply pin gives switching current a short
      local loop instead of a trip across the board. When a
      chip misbehaves under load and the schematic shows
      one shared capacitor, that is a real finding.

   4. CURRENT RETURNS THROUGH THE GROUND. A motor's ground
      current flowing under your I2C traces couples into
      them. "I2C fails when the motor runs" (chapter 07) is
      usually a ground-routing problem, not a filtering
      one.
What you can actually change from firmware

Four levers, and they are worth trying before escalating: slew rate — set every pin to the slowest that meets timing; clock rate — halve it and see whether the errors vanish, which localises the problem to timing or integrity immediately; digital filters — enable the peripheral's noise filter where it has one (I2C's DNF, CAN's sample point); and error counters — instrument every bus so "unreliable" becomes a number you can correlate with events like a motor starting.

Crosstalk and what it looks like in software

   Two parallel traces couple capacitively and inductively.
   A fast edge on one induces a pulse on its neighbour.

   aggressor  ____|-----
                  |
   victim     ____/\_____   a glitch at the aggressor's edge

   COUPLING FALLS OFF FAST WITH SEPARATION. The usual rule
   is 3x the trace width between traces, and more for a
   clock. Coupling also grows with the parallel run LENGTH -
   two traces side by side for 10 cm couple far more than
   for 1 cm.

   RIBBON CABLES ARE THE WORST CASE: every conductor
   parallel to every other, for the whole length, with no
   ground plane. A ribbon carrying a clock next to an I2C
   line is a reliable source of intermittent faults. The
   standard fix is alternating signal and ground conductors
   in the ribbon, which halves your conductor count and is
   worth it.

   SOFTWARE-VISIBLE SYMPTOMS OF CROSSTALK:

     - a bus that only errors when ANOTHER bus is active.
       Correlate: stop the other traffic and see if the
       errors stop. This single experiment identifies
       crosstalk faster than any measurement.

     - an interrupt that fires with no cause. An EXTI pin
       next to a switching signal will trigger on the
       neighbour's edge. If a GPIO interrupt count climbs
       when a motor or display is active, it is coupling.

     - a reset line glitching. Reset inputs are usually
       high impedance and unfiltered; a coupled pulse
       reboots the board with no crash log. If a board
       resets with no fault recorded and the reset-cause
       register says "pin reset", suspect the reset line
       rather than your firmware.

  • Compute the knee frequency for a 1 ns rise time.
  • Give the critical trace length for a 5 ns edge and say what it means.
  • Explain what reflects at an open-ended trace and the software-visible symptom.
  • Describe series termination and how the resistor value is chosen.
  • Explain where return current flows at speed and why plane splits matter.
  • Give the most common cause of serial failing between two chassis.
  • Describe the experiment that identifies crosstalk in one step.
  • Explain why a scope can show ringing that is not really there.
  • List the four things a firmware engineer can change to improve integrity.

Boot, provisioning and firmware update

A device you can update is a product; a device you cannot is a liability with a ten-year support obligation. This chapter covers the boot chain, how each unit gets its identity on the production line, and an update scheme that cannot brick the fleet.

The boot chain

   EACH STAGE VERIFIES THE NEXT BEFORE RUNNING IT. That is
   the whole idea of secure boot, and the chain only means
   anything if it starts somewhere immutable.

   +--------------------------------------------------+
   | ROM BOOTLOADER          in mask ROM, unchangeable |
   |   the ROOT OF TRUST. Holds (or hashes) a public   |
   |   key burned into OTP fuses.                      |
   +--------------------------------------------------+
                    | verifies signature
                    v
   +--------------------------------------------------+
   | FIRST-STAGE BOOTLOADER                            |
   |   sets up DRAM and clocks; small and rarely       |
   |   changed. On an MCU this stage often is not      |
   |   separate.                                       |
   +--------------------------------------------------+
                    | verifies signature
                    v
   +--------------------------------------------------+
   | APPLICATION BOOTLOADER                            |
   |   chooses slot A or B, handles update, falls back |
   +--------------------------------------------------+
                    | verifies signature
                    v
   +--------------------------------------------------+
   | APPLICATION                                       |
   +--------------------------------------------------+

   THE QUESTIONS AN INTERVIEWER WILL ASK ABOUT THIS:

   Where is the root of trust? If the answer is "flash",
   it is not a root of trust - an attacker who can write
   flash replaces both the key and the check. It must be
   ROM plus OTP fuses.

   What happens when verification FAILS? Refuse to boot
   and enter a recovery mode - not "log a warning and
   continue", which makes the whole chain decorative.

   Who signs, and where does the private key live? An HSM
   in a controlled build environment, not a developer's
   laptop and not in the repository.

   How do you REVOKE a key? If a signing key leaks and
   there is no revocation path, every device ever shipped
   trusts it forever. Usually a monotonic version counter
   in fuses that the ROM checks.

The update scheme

A/B (DUAL BANK) - the default answer.

  +---------+----------+----------+--------+
  | boot    | slot A   | slot B   | data   |
  +---------+----------+----------+--------+

  1. running from A. Download the image into B.
  2. VERIFY B: checksum, then signature. Do not skip
     straight to step 3 - a truncated download that
     happens to be flashable is exactly the failure this
     catches.
  3. Set a flag: "try B next".
  4. Reboot.
  5. B boots and must CONFIRM ITSELF within N minutes -
     by reaching the network, passing a self-test, or
     simply running long enough.
  6. If it does not confirm, the watchdog resets and the
     bootloader reverts to A.

  THE PROPERTY THAT MATTERS: a power cut at ANY point
  leaves a bootable system. During step 1 you are still
  running A; after step 3 either B confirms or you revert.
  There is no window where neither bank is valid.

  STEP 5 IS THE ONE CANDIDATES OMIT, and without it A/B is
  only half the mechanism - it protects against a corrupt
  download but not against an image that flashes perfectly
  and then crashes on boot.

COST: roughly doubles the flash requirement. That is the
term most often missing from a memory budget.

ALTERNATIVES WHEN FLASH IS TIGHT

  RECOVERY BOOTLOADER   a minimal loader that can always
                        re-download. Smaller, but there is
                        a window where the device has no
                        valid application and needs
                        connectivity to recover. Fine if it
                        is on a wired network; risky for a
                        battery device on a flaky radio.

  EXTERNAL STAGING      download into external flash, then
                        copy into internal on next boot.
                        Costs a part, keeps internal flash
                        for code.

  DELTA UPDATES         send only the difference. Saves
                        bandwidth and energy - which on a
                        cellular or LoRa device may be the
                        deciding factor - at the cost of
                        needing the exact base version and
                        a patch applier that must itself be
                        power-fail safe.
Two questions that separate a considered update design

"What if the update is interrupted?" is the obvious one and A/B answers it. "What if the new firmware is bad?" is the one people miss — the image is valid, correctly signed, flashes perfectly, and crashes on boot or breaks connectivity so the device can never be updated again. That is a fleet-wide brick, and the only defences are the self-confirmation step, a staged rollout (1% of the fleet, then 10%, then the rest), and the ability to roll back. Mention the staged rollout: it is what actually saves a company from a bad release.

Provisioning: how each unit gets its identity

EVERY DEVICE NEEDS, AT MANUFACTURE:
    a unique serial number
    a device key pair (and a certificate)
    calibration constants
    possibly network credentials, a region code, a model
    variant

THE DESIGN QUESTIONS, and this is a place candidates rarely
go unprompted:

  WHERE IS THE KEY GENERATED?
    Best: ON THE DEVICE, so the private key never exists
    anywhere else and never crosses a wire. The device
    generates a key pair, exports the PUBLIC key, and the
    factory signs a certificate for it.
    Worse: generated centrally and injected - now there is
    a machine on a factory floor that has held every
    device's private key.

  HOW IS IT STORED?
    Secure element, or an MCU key store where the key is
    usable but not readable, or internal flash with readout
    protection as the weakest acceptable option.

  HOW LONG DOES IT TAKE?
    Provisioning is a per-unit cost in factory time. Key
    generation on a small MCU can take seconds - multiply
    by a million units and it is a real production
    constraint that may drive the part choice.

  WHAT IF IT FAILS HALFWAY?
    A unit part-provisioned is scrap unless the process is
    idempotent and resumable. Design it so it can be
    re-run.

  HOW IS THE DEVICE LOCKED AFTERWARDS?
    Debug port disabled, readout protection set, bootloader
    entry restricted. And note this is IRREVERSIBLE on many
    parts, so the sequence must be right and it must be the
    last step - otherwise a failed later step leaves a
    locked, unusable unit.

  AND HOW DO YOU STILL DIAGNOSE A RETURNED UNIT?
    An authenticated diagnostic mode, not an open debug
    port. This tension - locked against attackers, open to
    you - is a real design problem worth naming.

Questions

Design a firmware update mechanism that cannot brick the device.Mid

A/B slots with confirmation. Two application banks; run from A, download into B, verify B by checksum and signature, set a "try B" flag, reboot. B must then confirm itself within a timeout — by passing a self-test or reaching the server — and if it does not, the watchdog resets and the bootloader reverts to A.

The property to state explicitly: a power cut at any instant leaves a bootable system, because during the download you are still running A and after the flag flip you either confirm or revert. There is no window where neither bank is valid.

The costs: roughly double the flash. The alternatives if that does not fit are a recovery bootloader that can always re-download (smaller, but there is a window needing connectivity) or external staging flash.

And the failure A/B alone does not cover: a valid, correctly-signed image that crashes on boot. The confirmation step catches it on one device; a staged rollout catches it before it reaches the fleet.

Listening forThe confirmation step and the flash doubling — both are frequently omitted.
Where should the root of trust live, and why not in flash?Senior

In mask ROM, with the public key (or its hash) in one-time-programmable fuses. The root of trust must be immutable — if an attacker who can write flash can replace both the verification code and the key it checks against, the entire chain verifies nothing.

So the ROM bootloader, which cannot be changed after manufacture, hashes the first-stage bootloader and compares against a key hash burned into OTP. That stage then verifies the next, and so on. Each link is only as strong as the one below it, and the bottom link must be in silicon.

The related design points: fuses are one-time, so the provisioning sequence must be correct — burning the wrong key bricks the unit permanently. And you need a revocation path: a monotonic counter in fuses that the ROM checks against a version field in the image, so a leaked signing key can be retired without replacing hardware. Without revocation, one key compromise permanently affects every device ever shipped.

Listening forImmutability as the requirement, and raising revocation unprompted.
A firmware update goes out and 40,000 devices stop connecting. What should have prevented it?Senior

A staged rollout, primarily. Release to 1% of the fleet, watch the check-in rate for a defined period, then 10%, then the rest — with an automatic halt if the check-in rate drops. That converts a fleet-wide outage into 400 affected devices and a stopped rollout.

Supporting mechanisms: the self-confirmation step in A/B, so a device that cannot reach the server reverts to the previous image by itself — which is exactly the failure described here, and it means most of those devices should have recovered automatically. If they did not, the confirmation criterion was wrong: confirming on "the application started" rather than "the application reached the server" would pass a build that boots but cannot connect.

And the deeper question: how were 40,000 devices reachable only through an update path that the update itself could break? A device should have an independent recovery route — a local interface, a factory reset, a fallback network configuration — so that connectivity is not a single point of failure for its own maintainability.

Listening forStaged rollout, and the insight that the confirmation criterion must test the thing that matters.
How does each device get a unique key on the production line?Senior

The strongest scheme: the device generates the key pair itself during provisioning, exports only the public key, and the factory station sends that to a signing service which returns a certificate. The private key never exists outside the chip and never crosses a wire, so there is no machine on the factory floor holding every device's secret.

The practical constraints that shape it: key generation takes time on a small MCU — potentially seconds for ECC — and that is a per-unit production cost multiplied by the volume, which can influence the part choice. The station needs network access to the signing service, or an offline signing appliance, and that service needs its own key protection (an HSM) and audit trail.

Also design: idempotency, so a unit interrupted mid-provisioning can be re-run rather than scrapped; a record linking serial number to certificate for later support; and the locking step (disable debug, set readout protection) strictly last, because it is irreversible.

The weaker alternative — central generation and injection — is sometimes chosen for speed, and then the injection machine and its logs become the crown jewels.

Listening forOn-device generation as the goal, and production time as a real constraint.
How do you update two microcontrollers that must speak the same protocol?Senior

The core problem is that they cannot be updated simultaneously, so there is always a window where versions differ — and if the protocol changed, they cannot talk, and you may lose the ability to update either.

The mechanisms: a version field in the protocol from day one, with each side able to speak at least the previous version, so a mismatch degrades rather than breaks. A defined update order — usually the one that is not the update path goes first, so the device retains the ability to recover. Atomic pairing where possible: download both images, verify both, then apply and reboot both, with each able to revert independently.

And the design decision that avoids much of this: have one processor own the update path and be able to reflash the other over its own bus (SWD, or a bootloader protocol over UART). Then the second MCU is not independently networked, cannot be half-updated by a network failure, and can always be recovered by the first — which is why this arrangement is common.

Listening forVersion negotiation, and the "one processor owns the update path" architecture.
What is the trade-off with delta updates?Mid

They save bandwidth and energy — sending 40 kB of difference instead of a 400 kB image, which on a cellular or LoRa link may be the difference between feasible and not, and on a battery device is a direct battery-life saving.

The costs: you must know the device's exact current version to generate the right patch, so the server needs per-device state and you need a patch per source version — which grows combinatorially unless you force devices to update sequentially. The patch applier must itself be power-fail safe, which is harder than copying a whole image because the intermediate state is neither the old version nor the new one. And a corrupted patch produces a corrupted image, so verification of the result (not just the patch) is essential.

The pragmatic middle ground: compress the full image rather than diffing it, which gets a good fraction of the saving with none of the state management. Reach for true delta only when the bandwidth constraint is genuinely binding.

Listening forPer-device state as the hidden cost, and compression as the cheaper alternative.
Should the bootloader be updatable?Senior

Ideally no, or as rarely as possible — because updating the bootloader is the one operation with no fallback. If power is lost while rewriting the code that performs recovery, the device is unrecoverable without physical access.

So the design principle: keep the bootloader small, simple and stable. It should do exactly three things — verify, select a slot, and provide a recovery path — and should not contain protocol stacks or features that will need changing. A bootloader that needs updating is usually one that was given too much responsibility.

When it genuinely must be updated (a security fix in the verification code, for instance), the safe approaches are: a small immutable first stage that can update a larger second stage, so there is always something that survives; or an A/B arrangement for the bootloader itself, with the ROM choosing between them; or accepting that it requires physical access and doing it during service.

And the related point: keep the bootloader's flash region write-protected during normal operation, so an application bug or an attacker cannot corrupt it.

Listening for"No fallback" as the reason, and keeping the bootloader minimal as the design response.
How long should a device wait before deciding an update failed?Mid

Long enough for a legitimate slow boot, short enough that a bricked device recovers before anyone notices. That means the criterion matters more than the number: confirm on a meaningful event rather than a timer.

Good criteria, in increasing strength: the application reached its main loop; it passed a self-test; it successfully read its sensors; it connected to the server and reported its version. The last is strongest because it proves the device is still manageable — which is the property you actually care about — but it requires connectivity that may legitimately be unavailable for hours.

So a practical scheme uses both: confirm quickly on a local self-test to avoid a reboot loop, and separately track "has reached the server since the update" with a much longer window, reverting if that never happens. Devices in a genuine network outage then do not revert unnecessarily, but a build that broke connectivity does eventually roll back.

And whatever the scheme, log the outcome so that a fleet-wide pattern of near-misses is visible before it becomes a failure.

Listening forConfirming on a meaningful event rather than a timer, and handling the legitimate-outage case.
What does a production line actually have to do to each unit?Senior

Flash the firmware; provision identity (serial number, keys, certificate); write calibration constants; run a functional test that exercises every interface and every connector; verify current consumption in both active and sleep states; record all of it against the serial number in a traceability database; and finally lock the device (disable debug, set readout protection).

The design implications, which is what the question is really about: every one of those steps needs hardware support that must exist in the schematic — test pads for SWD and UART, a sense resistor for current measurement, a way to drive and observe each interface, and a connector or fixture the tester can engage. They must be designed in; they cannot be added later.

And they have a cost: cycle time per unit multiplied by volume is a real manufacturing expense, so a provisioning step taking thirty seconds may be unacceptable at a million units and force a different approach. The locking step must be last and is irreversible, so the sequence has to be right.

Listening forDesign-for-test as a schematic-time obligation, and cycle time as a cost.
A device has been in the field for eight years. What obligations does that create?Senior

Several that are easy to underestimate at design time. Security maintenance: if it runs Linux, someone tracks kernel and library CVEs for the whole period and ships fixes — which means the update mechanism must still work, the signing keys must still be available and uncompromised, and the build environment must be reproducible eight years later. Certificate expiry: device certificates and the server certificates they trust have lifetimes; a device that hard-codes a CA which expires becomes unable to connect, and there are real products that have failed this way.

Protocol and service changes: the cloud service it talks to will be rewritten; the device must tolerate that, which argues for a versioned, stable interface. Component availability: spares and repairs need parts that may be obsolete. Cryptographic agility: algorithms considered fine today may not be in eight years, so the design should allow changing them.

The design responses: build reproducibility (pinned toolchains, archived artefacts and symbols), a long-lived and updatable trust store, generous margins on anything that cannot be changed, and an honest end-of-support policy communicated to customers.

Listening forCertificate expiry and build reproducibility — both are concrete and both bite real products.

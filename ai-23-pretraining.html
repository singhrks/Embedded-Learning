Pretraining and scaling laws

Predict the next token on a very large amount of text. The objective is trivial; the engineering is not, and the empirical laws governing how performance improves with scale are the most practically useful result in the field.

The objective

The training objective for a model that appears to do everything is startlingly simple, and the gap between that simplicity and the resulting behaviour is the central puzzle of the field.

   TRAINING DATA:  raw text. No labels. No annotation.

     input   [The, cat, sat, on, the]
     target  [cat, sat, on, the, mat]

   SHIFT BY ONE. Cross-entropy at every position
   (chapter 10). With causal masking, one forward pass over
   a sequence of length n yields n training examples
   (chapter 19).

   THAT IS THE ENTIRE PRETRAINING OBJECTIVE OF EVERY LARGE
   LANGUAGE MODEL.

   WHY IT WORKS AT ALL - and this is the genuinely
   surprising part:

     "The capital of France is ___"      needs a FACT
     "2 + 2 = ___"                       needs ARITHMETIC
     "def fib(n): return ___"            needs CODE
     "Q: ...  A: ___"                    needs REASONING
     "Translate: le chat -> ___"         needs TRANSLATION
     "... therefore the answer is ___"   needs INFERENCE

   PREDICTING THE NEXT TOKEN WELL ENOUGH, ACROSS ENOUGH
   TEXT, REQUIRES MODELLING WHATEVER PRODUCED THE TEXT.
   Nobody designed for translation or arithmetic; those
   capabilities are means to an end for a compression
   objective (chapter 10).

   THE HONEST FRAMING: this is an EMPIRICAL result, not a
   predicted one. In 2018 essentially nobody expected that
   scaling this objective would produce in-context learning
   or chain-of-thought reasoning. The theory is still
   catching up.

   AND THE ALTERNATIVE OBJECTIVES, for context:
     MASKED LM (BERT)   hide 15% of tokens, predict them.
                        Bidirectional context, better
                        representations - but you only train
                        on 15% of positions, and it does not
                        generate.
     SPAN CORRUPTION    (T5) mask contiguous spans.
     NEXT-TOKEN won because it trains on EVERY position, it
     generates natively, and it scales.

The data

Data is the part of pretraining that is least discussed publicly and most determines the result. What goes into the corpus, and what is filtered out, is a large fraction of the engineering.

/* A MODERN PRETRAINING CORPUS: 2-20 TRILLION TOKENS.
   Roughly the sources, in order of contribution:          */

    WEB CRAWL          Common Crawl, FineWeb, RefinedWeb.
                       The bulk, and the most in need of
                       filtering.
    CODE               GitHub, The Stack. Improves reasoning
                       measurably, even on non-code tasks.
    BOOKS              long-form coherence, narrative
                       structure
    WIKIPEDIA          dense, high-quality facts. Small but
                       heavily upweighted.
    ACADEMIC           arXiv, PubMed
    CURATED / SYNTHETIC increasingly significant: textbook-
                       style generated data, filtered by
                       quality models

/* --- THE PIPELINE, and it matters more than the
       architecture --- */

  1. EXTRACT text from HTML. Boilerplate removal is
     surprisingly hard and surprisingly important.

  2. LANGUAGE IDENTIFICATION and filtering.

  3. QUALITY FILTERING
       · heuristics: mean word length, punctuation ratio,
         proportion of stopwords, lines ending in "..."
       · a CLASSIFIER trained to recognise "reference-like"
         text
       · perplexity filtering against a small reference
         model

  4. DEDUPLICATION - THE HIGHEST-VALUE STEP.
       exact-match, then FUZZY (MinHash / LSH) at document
       and paragraph level.
       Duplicated data causes memorisation without
       generalisation, and it wastes compute. Removing
       duplicates reliably improves models at fixed
       compute - one of the few interventions that is
       almost always positive.

  5. DECONTAMINATION - remove anything matching benchmark
     test sets (chapter 12). Frequently done badly, which
     is why published benchmark numbers should be read
     sceptically.

  6. PII AND TOXICITY FILTERING.

  7. MIXING AND UPWEIGHTING. Wikipedia might be repeated
     several times; low-quality web crawl seen once. The
     mixture weights are a real hyperparameter and are
     usually not published.

/* --- HOW MUCH DATA IS THERE, HONESTLY --- */
    High-quality English text on the public internet is
    estimated at a few tens of trillions of tokens. Frontier
    models already train on a meaningful fraction of it.

    THE RESPONSES: multilingual data, code, multiple epochs
    (up to ~4 epochs costs little quality), synthetic data,
    and non-public licensed corpora. "Running out of data"
    is a real constraint on the current recipe, and it is
    part of why attention has shifted toward post-training
    (chapter 24) and inference-time compute.               */

Scaling laws

Scaling laws turned model building from craft into something closer to planning: you can predict the outcome before spending the money. That predictability is why the field looks the way it does.

   THE CENTRAL EMPIRICAL RESULT (Kaplan 2020, Hoffmann
   2022):

     LOSS FALLS AS A POWER LAW IN MODEL SIZE, DATA AND
     COMPUTE, SMOOTHLY, OVER MANY ORDERS OF MAGNITUDE.

        L(N) = (Nc / N)^alpha        N = parameters
        L(D) = (Dc / D)^beta         D = tokens
        L(C) = (Cc / C)^gamma        C = compute

   On a log-log plot these are STRAIGHT LINES:

     log
     loss
       |  \
       |    \
       |      \
       |        \
       |          \
       +------------------ log compute

   THE PRACTICAL CONSEQUENCE, and it is why these laws
   matter: YOU CAN TRAIN SMALL MODELS AND PREDICT THE LOSS
   OF A LARGE ONE BEFORE SPENDING THE MONEY. Frontier runs
   are planned this way - a series of small runs fits the
   curve, and the big run's loss is forecast in advance and
   usually lands close.

   THE CHINCHILLA RESULT (2022), which corrected the field:

     For a COMPUTE BUDGET C, the optimal split is
     approximately

        20 TOKENS PER PARAMETER

     GPT-3: 175B parameters, 300B tokens
            -> 1.7 tokens/parameter. SEVERELY
               UNDERTRAINED.
     Chinchilla: 70B parameters, 1.4T tokens
            -> a 70B model BEAT the 175B one, at the same
               compute.

   THE LESSON WAS THAT EVERYONE HAD BEEN BUILDING MODELS
   TOO BIG AND TRAINING THEM TOO LITTLE.

   AND THE MODERN CORRECTION TO THE CORRECTION:

     Chinchilla optimises TRAINING compute. But a deployed
     model is trained ONCE and run BILLIONS of times, so
     INFERENCE cost dominates total cost.

     So current practice deliberately OVERTRAINS SMALL
     MODELS far past Chinchilla-optimal:
       Llama 3 8B: ~15T tokens -> ~1,875 tokens/parameter,
       nearly 100x Chinchilla.

     A smaller model trained much longer costs more to
     train and far less to serve. FOR ANYTHING THAT WILL
     RUN ON A DEVICE (chapter 27), THIS IS THE RIGHT TRADE
     AND IT IS WHY GOOD SMALL MODELS EXIST NOW.

   THE COMPUTE FORMULA (chapter 04):

        C  ~=  6 * N * D     FLOPs

     Llama-3-8B: 6 * 8e9 * 15e12 = 7.2e23 FLOPs.
     On 16,000 H100s at ~400 TFLOP/s effective:
        7.2e23 / (16000 * 4e14) = ~112,000 seconds of pure
        compute, and in practice weeks of wall-clock once
        efficiency, restarts and data loading are counted.

The engineering

Training at this scale is a distributed-systems problem at least as much as a machine-learning one, and the parallelism strategies each trade a different resource.

/* NO SINGLE ACCELERATOR HOLDS A LARGE MODEL. Training is a
   distributed systems problem.                            */

/* --- THE PARALLELISM AXES --- */
    DATA PARALLEL       every device holds a FULL COPY of
                        the model, processes a different
                        batch slice, and gradients are
                        ALL-REDUCED. Simple; limited by
                        model memory.

    ZeRO / FSDP         SHARD the optimiser state, then the
                        gradients, then the parameters
                        across devices, gathering each layer
                        only when needed. Removes the
                        Adam-state memory wall (chapter 11)
                        at the cost of extra communication.

    TENSOR PARALLEL     SPLIT INDIVIDUAL MATRICES across
                        devices - each holds part of a
                        weight matrix. Needs very fast
                        interconnect (NVLink), so it stays
                        WITHIN a node.

    PIPELINE PARALLEL   different LAYERS on different
                        devices. Creates a "bubble" of idle
                        time, mitigated by splitting the
                        batch into micro-batches.

    EXPERT PARALLEL     MoE experts on different devices
                        (chapter 22).

    IN PRACTICE, ALL AT ONCE: tensor parallel inside a node,
    pipeline parallel across nodes, data parallel across
    replicas. "3-D parallelism".

/* --- MIXED PRECISION --- */
    bf16 for the forward and backward passes; fp32 for the
    master weights and the optimiser state.

    BF16, NOT FP16. Same exponent range as fp32, fewer
    mantissa bits. It cannot overflow where fp16 does, so
    NO LOSS SCALING IS NEEDED. This alone removed a whole
    class of training instability, and it is why every
    modern accelerator supports it.

/* --- GRADIENT CHECKPOINTING (chapter 07) --- */
    Recompute activations in the backward pass instead of
    storing them. ~30% more compute, a large memory saving.
    Standard.

/* --- WHAT THE OPERATIONS ACTUALLY LOOK LIKE --- */
    · CHECKPOINT FREQUENTLY. Hardware fails; at 16,000 GPUs
      something fails every few hours. Meta reported a
      failure roughly every 3 hours during Llama 3 training.
    · MONITOR THE LOSS CURVE CONTINUOUSLY. LOSS SPIKES ARE
      NORMAL. The standard response is to roll back to a
      checkpoint and skip the offending data batch
      (chapter 11).
    · MFU (model FLOPs utilisation) is the efficiency
      metric: achieved FLOPs / peak FLOPs. 40-55% is good.
      Below 30% means something is wrong - usually
      communication or data loading.
    · DATA LOADING MUST KEEP UP. Feeding thousands of
      accelerators is itself a serious I/O problem.

/* --- THE SEQUENCE OF A REAL RUN --- */
    1. tokenise and shard the corpus into fixed blocks
       (chapter 16) - no padding, documents concatenated
       with separators
    2. warmup over ~2000 steps (chapter 11)
    3. cosine decay to ~10% of peak
    4. AdamW, beta2 = 0.95, weight decay 0.1, grad clip 1.0
    5. batch size ramped up over training - small batches
       early are more sample-efficient
    6. evaluate on held-out perplexity and a benchmark suite
       every few thousand steps
    7. a final "cooldown" or annealing phase on
       high-quality data, which reliably improves the final
       model                                              */
Emergence, and the argument about it

Some capabilities appear abruptly with scale — multi-digit arithmetic, chain-of-thought benefit, in-context learning — showing near-zero performance below a threshold and useful performance above it. But a well-supported critique argues much of this is an artefact of discontinuous metrics: exact-match accuracy on a 5-digit sum is all-or-nothing, so a smooth improvement in per-token probability looks like a sudden jump. Measured with a continuous metric, many "emergent" curves become smooth. Some genuine phase changes remain — induction-head formation (chapter 19) is a real, abrupt structural transition. Treat "emergence" claims as needing a look at the metric first.

What it costs, and what it produces

Concrete figures are worth stating because they explain the shape of the industry — who can pretrain, who fine-tunes, and why the base-model layer consolidated.

/* --- ROUGH TRAINING COSTS --- */
    GPT-2 (1.5B, 2019)         ~$50k
    GPT-3 (175B, 2020)         ~$5M
    Llama 3 70B (2024)         ~$30-60M
    Frontier models (2025-26)  hundreds of millions

    NOT INCLUDING: failed runs, data pipeline work,
    salaries, or the evaluation infrastructure - which
    collectively often exceed the compute bill.

/* --- WHAT YOU GET: A BASE MODEL --- */
    A base model COMPLETES TEXT. It does not follow
    instructions, answer questions, or refuse anything.

        prompt: "What is the capital of France?"
        base model: "What is the capital of Germany?
                     What is the capital of Italy?
                     ..."

    It continued the pattern - a list of questions - which
    is exactly what the objective asked for. IT IS NOT
    BROKEN; it is doing precisely what it was trained to do.

    TURNING IT INTO AN ASSISTANT IS A SEPARATE STAGE
    (chapter 24), and that stage costs a tiny fraction of
    pretraining while changing the user-visible behaviour
    almost entirely.

/* --- AND WHAT THIS MEANS IF YOU ARE NOT OPENAI --- */

    YOU WILL NOT PRETRAIN A FRONTIER MODEL. What you will
    do:

      · FINE-TUNE an open base model (chapter 24) -
        hundreds to thousands of dollars
      · PRETRAIN A SMALL DOMAIN MODEL - a 10-100M parameter
        model on your own corpus (sensor logs, protocol
        traces, domain text) is genuinely feasible on one
        GPU in days, and for a narrow embedded task it may
        beat anything general-purpose
      · DISTIL a large model into a small one (chapter 26)
      · or simply USE an existing model well

    THE SCALING LAWS ARE STILL USEFUL AT YOUR SCALE: they
    tell you how to split a fixed budget between model size
    and data, and 20 tokens per parameter remains a sound
    starting point for a from-scratch run. Chapter 28's
    project is exactly this, at the smallest possible size.
                                                          */

How it shows up

SymptomCause
Base model does not answer questionsWorking as designed — it completes text; it needs post-training
Model memorises passages verbatimDuplicated data — deduplicate
Benchmark scores far above real qualityTest-set contamination — decontaminate
Large model underperforms a smaller oneUndertrained — check tokens per parameter
MFU below 30%Communication overhead or a starved data loader
Loss spikes mid-runNormal at scale — roll back and skip the batch
Training unstable in fp16Use bf16 — no loss scaling needed
Out of memory with a model that should fitOptimiser state — use ZeRO/FSDP and checkpointing
Serving cost dominates the budgetChinchilla-optimal sizing — overtrain a smaller model instead
An "emergent" capability vanishes under a different metricThe metric was discontinuous, not the capability

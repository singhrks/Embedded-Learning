What a design interview actually tests

An embedded design round is not a quiz with answers. It is a deliberately underspecified problem, and what is being measured is how you narrow it down, what you choose to ask, and whether your numbers are real. This chapter is the map of the genre before we start practising it.

The four question genres

GenreLooks likeReally measuring
Design round"Design a battery-powered air quality monitor"Structured thinking under ambiguity; whether you ask before you answer
Rapid-fire fundamentals"What does volatile do? What does it not do?"Whether the basics are genuinely internalised or memorised
Debugging scenario"The board resets every few hours in the field. Go."Method. Do you form hypotheses and order them by cost, or guess?

This track covers all four. The design rounds get the most space because they are the least practised and the most decisive — a strong design round can carry a weak rapid-fire round, rarely the reverse.

What is being scored

   FOUR AXES, and interviewers weight them differently by
   level. Almost nobody tells you this explicitly.

   1. BREADTH
      Do you know that a decision exists? A candidate who
      never mentions brown-out detection has not made a
      wrong choice - they have failed to see a choice.

   2. TRADE-OFF REASONING
      Every real decision costs something. "I'd use DMA"
      is half an answer; "I'd use DMA because per-byte
      interrupts would be 18% CPU at this rate, and I
      accept the buffer RAM and the cache-coherency work"
      is a whole one.

   3. NUMBERS
      The single strongest signal. Candidates who estimate
      - even roughly, even wrongly, out loud - are ranked
      far above candidates who speak only in adjectives.
      "About 11 kB/s, so per-byte interrupts are fine"
      beats "it should be fast enough" every time.

   4. COMMUNICATION
      Can the interviewer follow you? Do you signal when
      you are guessing? Do you park a tangent and come
      back? A brilliant design explained badly reads as a
      lucky guess.

   WHAT IS NOT BEING SCORED, despite what candidates fear:
     - knowing a specific part number
     - recalling a register name
     - producing the same design the interviewer had in mind
     - finishing. Most design rounds are deliberately too
       big to finish.

The senior signal, in one sentence

Junior and mid candidates describe what they would build; senior candidates describe what they would not build, and why. "We could add a second MCU for the safety loop, but the certification cost doubles and I think we can meet the requirement with the watchdog plus a hardware interlock — let me show the timing" is the sentence that moves a candidate a level. It demonstrates breadth, trade-off reasoning, numbers and communication in one breath.

How candidates actually lose

   IN ORDER OF FREQUENCY, from the interviewer's side of
   the table:

   1. SOLVING BEFORE UNDERSTANDING
      Thirty seconds in, already choosing an MCU. You do
      not yet know the power budget, the volume, the
      environment or the deadline. Everything after this
      is unanchored.

   2. NO NUMBERS AT ALL
      "It needs to be low power." How low? For how long?
      From what battery? Without an energy budget you
      cannot choose anything, and the interviewer knows it.

   3. ONE-WAY DOORS TAKEN SILENTLY
      Choosing a part, a bus or an RTOS without noting
      what it forecloses. The choice may be right; making
      it invisibly is the problem.

   4. OVER-ENGINEERING
      A secure element, a hypervisor and OTA on a device
      that ships 200 units and lives in a locked cabinet.
      Cost and schedule are requirements too.

   5. UNDER-SPECIFYING THE FAILURE PATH
      A design that works is table stakes. What happens
      when the sensor NACKs? When the flash write is
      interrupted? When the battery is at 2.9 V?

   6. HAND-WAVING THE HARD PART
      "...and then we sync the clocks" is where the
      difficulty lives, and skipping past it reads as not
      knowing.

   7. ARGUING WITH A HINT
      An interviewer nudging you is giving you marks, not
      taking them. Candidates who defend a wrong turn
      score worse than candidates who say "good point,
      let me reconsider".

How to use this track

  • Read chapter 02 first — the framework is the thing you will actually use under pressure, and everything after it hangs off that structure.
  • Answer before revealing. Every question in this track is collapsed. Say your answer out loud, then open it. Reading model answers passively builds recognition, not recall — and interviews test recall.
  • Do the arithmetic. Where a question involves numbers, work them before opening the answer. This is the habit that separates candidates.
  • Use the scenarios as timed practice. Chapters 18–22 are full design rounds. Give yourself 45 minutes and a whiteboard before reading the walkthrough.
  • Follow the cross-references. Most answers link to the chapter on this site that covers the mechanism properly. If an answer feels like a recital rather than an explanation, go read that.

Questions

What is the first thing you should do when handed an open-ended design question?Junior

Establish the requirements and constraints, out loud, before proposing anything. Concretely: what does the device do, how many are made, what powers it, what environment does it live in, what must happen in real time, what is the unit cost target, and what is the schedule.

Then restate the problem back in your own words with your assumptions attached: "So: a battery-powered device, 10,000 units, must run a year on a coin cell, reports once a minute over BLE, indoor, under $15 BOM. I'll assume no display and no user interface beyond a button." That restatement is worth a lot — it proves you listened and it gives the interviewer a cheap chance to correct you before you spend twenty minutes on the wrong problem.

What the interviewer is listening forWhether you can tolerate ambiguity without either freezing or bulldozing through it. Asking three or four sharp questions is a strong signal; asking twenty is stalling.
Weak answersNaming a microcontroller. Drawing a block diagram immediately. Asking "what language should I use?" — nobody cares yet.
  • Follow-up: "The customer says all of it is required. What do you do?" — find which requirement is driving the cost, quantify it, and present the trade to them.
Why do interviewers care so much about estimation, when your numbers will be wrong anyway?Mid

Because the order of magnitude is what decides the architecture, and being within 3× is almost always enough to make the right decision. Whether a UART link is 11 kB/s or 300 kB/s decides interrupts versus DMA; whether sleep current is 2 µA or 50 µA decides whether a coin cell is viable at all. An engineer who cannot produce the estimate cannot make the choice on any grounds except habit.

The second reason: estimating out loud exposes your model of the system. An interviewer learns more from watching you decompose "how much energy per measurement?" into current × time for each phase than from any answer you could give.

What the interviewer is listening forThat you volunteer arithmetic unprompted, state your assumptions, and sanity-check the result ("that's 40% of the budget on the radio, which sounds right").
Weak answers"It depends." "I'd have to measure." Both are true and neither is an answer — you can always bound it.
You are asked to design something in a domain you have never worked in. How do you handle it?Mid

Say so once, plainly, and then reason from first principles rather than bluffing. "I have not built a medical device, so I do not know the specific standards. I do know the design has to be auditable and that failure analysis drives the architecture, so let me reason about it that way and you can correct my assumptions."

Interviewers are usually testing transferable judgement, not domain trivia. The material in this track — power budgets, real-time decomposition, failure analysis, update strategy — transfers across every embedded domain. What does not transfer is the regulatory detail, and admitting that costs you nothing while pretending costs you the interview.

What the interviewer is listening forCalibrated confidence: knowing what you know, flagging what you do not, and continuing to make progress regardless.
Weak answersInventing domain facts. Going silent. Redirecting to a domain you do know without acknowledging the switch.
The interviewer interrupts and suggests a different approach. What does that mean?Junior

Almost always that they are helping you. Interviewers steer when a candidate is heading somewhere that will waste the remaining time, or when they want to see a specific skill and your path avoids it. The correct response is to take the hint seriously, think about it visibly, and either adopt it or explain concretely why you would not.

Defending a position against a hint is one of the most reliable ways to lose a round — not because disagreement is bad, but because it usually indicates you are not listening. Disagreeing well ("that would work and it's simpler; the reason I went the other way is the 5 ms deadline, but if we can relax that your version is better") scores higher than either blind agreement or resistance.

What the interviewer is listening forCoachability. Can you work with someone on a problem?
What separates a mid-level answer from a senior one on the same question?Senior

Scope and subtraction. A mid-level answer designs the thing that was asked for and designs it correctly. A senior answer also covers what happens around it: how it is tested, how it is manufactured, how it is updated in the field, what it costs, what happens when it fails at 3 a.m. in a customer's building — and, crucially, which parts of the design should be deleted because they are not earning their cost.

The other marker is knowing which single number or constraint dominates. Senior engineers converge on that quickly ("everything here is decided by the energy budget; let me start there") instead of treating all decisions as equally weighted.

What the interviewer is listening forWhether you have owned something through production, or only built it.
  • Follow-up: "What would you cut from your own design if the BOM had to drop by $3?" — a direct test of whether you know your design's cost structure.
Is it acceptable to not finish a design round?Junior

Yes — most rounds are scoped so that finishing is not possible in the time. What matters is that you spend the time on the parts that matter and that you signal your plan: "There are five subsystems here. I think the power path and the radio duty cycle are where the risk is, so I'll go deep on those and stay shallow on the rest unless you want otherwise."

That sentence does two things: it shows you can prioritise, and it invites the interviewer to redirect you toward whatever they wanted to probe. Both are worth marks.

What the interviewer is listening forDeliberate allocation of time rather than drift.
Weak answersRacing through every subsystem at the same shallow depth so that everything is "covered" and nothing is demonstrated.

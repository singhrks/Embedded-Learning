The framework: a repeatable structure

Under time pressure you will not invent a method, so you need one already loaded. This is a six-stage structure that fits any embedded design question, keeps you moving when you are stuck, and makes your reasoning legible to the person marking you.

Six stages

   1. CLARIFY      what is actually being asked
   2. BUDGET       the numbers that decide everything
   3. PARTITION    where each piece of work happens
   4. ARCHITECT    the blocks and the interfaces between them
   5. FAIL         what breaks, what notices, what recovers
   6. PROVE        how you test, bring up and manufacture it

   ROUGH TIME ALLOCATION in a 45-minute round:

   CLARIFY    |=====|                        5 min
   BUDGET     |=========|                    8 min
   PARTITION  |=====|                        5 min
   ARCHITECT  |===============|             13 min
   FAIL       |=========|                    8 min
   PROVE      |=====|                        5 min
                                            -------
                                             44 min

   Nobody follows this exactly, and you should not try.
   Its value is that when you run dry - and you will -
   you always have a next move: "I've covered the
   architecture; let me talk about how it fails."

   SAY THE STRUCTURE OUT LOUD at the start. "I'll spend a
   few minutes on requirements, then work the power budget
   since I suspect that decides the design, then draw the
   architecture, then failure modes." That single sentence
   makes everything after it easier to follow, and buys
   you credit before you have designed anything.

1 · Clarify

THE SEVEN QUESTIONS that shape almost every embedded design.
Ask the three or four that matter most for this problem, not
all seven.

  1. FUNCTION      What does it do, in one sentence? What is
                   explicitly out of scope?

  2. VOLUME        10 units or 10 million? This changes
                   everything: NRE amortisation, whether a
                   custom PCB is worth it, whether you can
                   afford a $12 SoC, whether certification
                   cost matters.

  3. POWER         Mains, battery, or harvested? If battery:
                   what chemistry, what capacity, what
                   replacement interval? This is usually the
                   dominant constraint and often the first
                   number worth computing.

  4. ENVIRONMENT   Indoor bench, factory floor, vehicle,
                   outdoors, human body? Sets temperature
                   range, EMC exposure, ingress rating,
                   vibration, and whether you need automotive
                   or medical qualified parts.

  5. TIMING        What has a deadline, and what happens if
                   it is missed? "The motor commutates within
                   5 us or it stalls" and "the display
                   updates within 100 ms or it feels laggy"
                   are different universes.

  6. CONNECTIVITY  Does it talk to anything? To what, how
                   often, over what, and who initiates?

  7. LIFECYCLE     How long in the field? Can it be updated?
                   Can it be physically reached? Ten-year
                   deployment with no field access is a
                   completely different design from a
                   consumer gadget replaced in two years.

WRITE THE ANSWERS DOWN where the interviewer can see them.
They become the thing you point at when justifying a
decision: "I'm choosing the coin cell here because of
requirement 3."
The one question that most changes an embedded design

"How is it powered, and for how long?" More than processor choice, more than connectivity, the energy budget cascades into everything: which MCU, whether Linux is possible at all, the radio, the duty cycle, the sample rate, whether you can afford to keep RAM alive in sleep. If you only get to ask one question, ask this one. If the answer is "mains powered", a large fraction of the difficulty evaporates and you should say so — noticing that a constraint is absent is itself a signal.

2 · Budget

FOUR BUDGETS. Do the ones that bind; say why the others do
not. All four are arithmetic you can do on a whiteboard.

--- ENERGY ---------------------------------------------
  average current = sum(current_i x time_i) / period
  life = capacity / average_current

  Worked, for a sensor waking every 10 s:
    sleep    9.8 s  x   2 uA  =  19.6 uA-s
    measure  0.15 s x 8 mA    = 1200   uA-s
    radio    0.05 s x 15 mA   =  750   uA-s
                                --------
    per 10 s cycle             1969.6 uA-s
    average = 1969.6 / 10 = 197 uA
    220 mAh coin cell / 0.197 mA = 1117 h = 47 DAYS.

  A year needs ~25 uA, so the design must change: sample
  less often, or batch radio transmissions, or use a bigger
  cell. THAT conclusion - reached in 90 seconds of
  arithmetic - is worth more than any amount of hand-waving
  about "low power design".

--- TIME (CPU and deadlines) ----------------------------
  utilisation = sum(compute_i / period_i)  for tasks
              + sum(isr_duration / isr_period)  for ISRs

  Fixed-priority scheduling is safe to about 70%
  (mcu chapter 20). Above that, deadlines are not
  guaranteed and you must do exact response-time analysis.

--- MEMORY ---------------------------------------------
  RAM  = .data + .bss + stacks(per task) + heap
         + DMA buffers + protocol buffers
  Flash= code + constants + .data initialisers
         + OTA slot (usually DOUBLE the app, chapter 12)

  The OTA slot is the one candidates forget, and it can
  double your flash requirement outright.

--- BANDWIDTH ------------------------------------------
  Per link: bytes/s needed vs bytes/s available, and the
  CPU cost of moving them (mcu chapter 12).
    e.g. 8 kHz x 2 ch x 2 bytes = 32 kB/s audio
         -> trivial for SPI, impossible for per-byte
            interrupts at high rates, fine with DMA.

3 · Partition

   WHERE DOES EACH PIECE OF WORK HAPPEN? Four boundaries,
   and each is a real decision with a cost.

   HARDWARE vs SOFTWARE
     A comparator and a MOSFET can enforce an over-current
     limit in nanoseconds; software cannot. Anything whose
     deadline is shorter than your interrupt latency MUST
     be hardware (mcu chapter 11's dead-time and break
     inputs are the canonical example).

   ONE PROCESSOR vs TWO
     The common product architecture is an application
     processor for interface and connectivity plus a
     microcontroller for the real-time control loop. It
     costs a part, a bus and a protocol; it buys
     determinism that Linux cannot give you (rpi chapter
     01) and often a smaller certification scope.

   ON-DEVICE vs OFF-DEVICE
     Do you filter and decide locally, or ship raw data and
     decide in the cloud? Local costs compute and memory;
     remote costs energy (the radio is usually the biggest
     consumer), bandwidth and availability.

   PERIPHERAL vs CPU
     DMA, hardware CRC, hardware crypto, PWM comparators,
     PIO. Every job you push into a peripheral is CPU you
     get back and jitter you remove.

   SAY THE BOUNDARY OUT LOUD WITH ITS COST:
   "I'll put the commutation loop on a Cortex-M alongside
   the Linux SoC. That's an extra $3 and a SPI link to
   define, and it buys a hard 5 us deadline that Linux
   cannot promise."

4 · Architect

NOW draw the block diagram - not before. It should fall out
of stages 1-3 almost mechanically.

WHAT A GOOD BLOCK DIAGRAM SHOWS:
  - the blocks, obviously
  - the INTERFACE on every line: which bus, what rate,
    who is master, who initiates
  - the power domains and what is switched
  - where data is buffered, and how much

  +----------+  I2C 400k  +---------+
  | MCU      |<---------->| sensor  |  (int line on GPIO17)
  | Cortex-M |  INT       |         |
  |          |<-----------+---------+
  |          |
  |          |  UART 115k +---------+
  |          |<---------->| radio   |  (SW-controlled
  |          |            | module  |   power rail)
  +----------+            +---------+

THEN THE SOFTWARE DECOMPOSITION. For a real-time design,
state the tasks, their periods, their priorities and their
worst-case compute (mcu chapter 20):

  task          period   WCET   prio   why
  ------------- ------- ------ ------ ------------------
  control loop    1 ms   180us   high  rate-monotonic:
  sensor read    10 ms   400us   med   shortest period
  comms         100 ms     2ms   low   gets the highest
  housekeeping  1000 ms    5ms   idle  priority

  utilisation = 0.18 + 0.04 + 0.02 + 0.005 = 0.245
  well under the 0.757 bound for 4 tasks. Schedulable.

AND THE SHARED STATE. For each thing two contexts touch:
who writes it, who reads it, and what protects it
(chapter 11).

5 · Fail

   THE STAGE THAT SEPARATES CANDIDATES. Walk the system and
   ask, for each element: how does this fail, what notices,
   and what happens next?

   ELEMENT        FAILS HOW          DETECTED BY      RESPONSE
   -------------------------------------------------------------
   sensor         NACKs / silent     I2C error /      retry, then
                                     stale timestamp  mark stale,
                                                      report fault
   radio          no ack / no link   timeout          backoff,
                                                      queue locally
   flash          bad block / full   write verify     wear-level,
                                                      drop oldest
   battery        sags under load    ADC + brown-out  shed load,
                                     detector         safe state
   firmware       hangs              watchdog         reset, log
                                     (rpi ch 19)      cause
   power          cut mid-write      journal / atomic power-fail
                                     rename           safe update

   THREE QUESTIONS THAT SCORE WELL HERE:

   "What is the SAFE STATE?" Every device has one - motor
   off, valve closed, heater disabled. Say what it is and
   how you reach it from any failure.

   "How do I know it failed in the FIELD?" A device that
   fails silently and a device that reports are different
   products. Crash logs in non-volatile memory, a fault
   counter, a reset-cause register read at boot.

   "What is the WORST failure this design allows?" Naming
   your own design's worst case, unprompted, is a strong
   senior signal.

6 · Prove

The stage almost every candidate skips, and the one that
most distinguishes people who have shipped hardware.

  BRING-UP     What is the order? Power rails, clocks,
               console, then peripherals one at a time.
               What is the first thing you check with a
               scope? (rpi chapter 05: fit the serial
               console before you need it.)

  TEST POINTS  Have you left yourself a UART, a spare GPIO
               to toggle, a JTAG/SWD header, and a way to
               measure current? These cost pennies at
               design time and are impossible to add later.

  PRODUCTION   How is each unit tested on the line? How
               long does that take, and what does it cost
               per unit? How is calibration data and a
               unique key or serial number written into
               each device? (chapter 12.)

  FIELD        How do you diagnose a unit you cannot
               reach? Logs, counters, a fault code the
               customer can read out.

  UPDATE       Can firmware be replaced? What if the
               update is interrupted? (chapter 12's A/B.)

MENTIONING DESIGN FOR TEST UNPROMPTED is a reliable way to
signal production experience, and it takes thirty seconds:
"I'd bring out SWD and a UART on a tag-connect footprint,
and add a 0.1 ohm sense resistor in the battery path so the
line can measure sleep current."

Questions

You have 45 minutes and a question with six subsystems. How do you allocate the time?Mid

Spend the first five minutes clarifying, then explicitly identify which one or two subsystems carry the risk and say you are going deep there. Announce the plan: "The radio duty cycle and the power path are what make or break this; I'll go deep on those and stay at block-diagram level on the rest — stop me if you'd rather see something else."

That invitation matters. The interviewer usually has a specific thing they want to probe, and this hands them a cheap way to steer you to it without it feeling like a rescue.

What the interviewer is listening forDeliberate prioritisation, and the recognition that depth in the right place beats uniform shallow coverage.
Weak answersWorking through subsystems in the order they occurred to you until time runs out.
Which budget would you compute first, and why?Mid

Whichever one is most likely to be infeasible — because a budget that fails changes the architecture, and you want to discover that in minute five rather than minute forty.

For a battery device that is almost always energy: it decides the processor class, the radio, the duty cycle and whether Linux is possible at all. For a control system it is time (utilisation and deadlines). For a data-heavy device it is bandwidth or memory. Say which you are picking and why: "I'll do energy first because if a coin cell cannot support this, everything downstream changes."

What the interviewer is listening forThat you can identify the binding constraint rather than mechanically working through a checklist.
A device wakes every 10 s: 150 ms at 8 mA measuring, 50 ms at 15 mA transmitting, 2 µA asleep. Will it run a year on a 220 mAh coin cell?Junior

No — about 47 days.

Per 10-second cycle: sleep 9.8 s × 2 µA = 19.6 µA·s; measure 0.15 s × 8000 µA = 1200 µA·s; transmit 0.05 s × 15000 µA = 750 µA·s. Total 1969.6 µA·s per 10 s, so average current = 197 µA. Life = 220 mAh ÷ 0.197 mA ≈ 1117 hours ≈ 47 days.

A year needs roughly 25 µA average, so the design must change by about 8×. The levers, in order of effectiveness: transmit less often (batch readings — the radio is 38% of the budget), measure less often or faster, and only then chase sleep current (which is just 1% here and therefore not where the problem is).

What the interviewer is listening forThe arithmetic, and then the correct conclusion about which term to attack. Candidates who immediately propose optimising sleep current have not read their own numbers.
Weak answers"It depends on the duty cycle" — you were given the duty cycle. Guessing without computing.
  • Follow-up: "Batch ten readings per transmission. Now?" — radio drops to 75 µA·s per cycle, average ≈ 129 µA, ≈ 71 days. Still short: the measurement phase now dominates and must be attacked next.
When must a function be implemented in hardware rather than firmware?Mid

When its deadline is shorter than your worst-case interrupt latency, or when a software failure must not be able to defeat it.

The clearest examples are motor bridge dead-time and break inputs: shoot-through destroys the bridge in microseconds, and a software delay can be preempted, so the dead time is generated by the timer hardware and the fault input forces the outputs to a safe state without the CPU (mcu chapter 11). The same logic covers over-current comparators, hardware interlocks, and analog filters ahead of an ADC.

The safety framing is the stronger one: hardware enforcement survives a hung CPU, a corrupted variable and a bad firmware update. That is why safety cases lean on it.

What the interviewer is listening forThe latency argument and the independence argument — many candidates give only the first.
Why do so many products use both an application processor and a microcontroller?Mid

Because the two jobs have incompatible requirements. The application processor gives you Linux: networking, a display stack, a filesystem, a package ecosystem, and the ability to hire people who can work on it. It cannot give you a bounded response time — a page fault, a thermal event or the scheduler can cost you milliseconds (rpi chapter 01).

The microcontroller gives you determinism measured in cycles and microamp sleep, and cannot give you a TCP/IP stack you would want to maintain. So the control loop, the safety interlocks and the power management go on the MCU; the interface, connectivity and updates go on the AP; they talk over SPI, UART or shared memory.

Secondary benefits worth mentioning: the safety-critical code is now a small, auditable binary on a simple core, which dramatically shrinks a certification scope; and the MCU can keep the system alive and safe while the AP reboots for an update.

What the interviewer is listening forThat you frame it as a partitioning decision with costs (an extra part, a bus, a protocol, two build systems) rather than as an obvious default.
What is a "safe state", and why does every design need one named?Senior

The configuration the device enters when it cannot trust itself: motor de-energised, valve closed, heater off, outputs high-impedance, radio silent. It must be reachable from any fault, including a hung CPU, which usually means hardware enforces it — a break input, a watchdog wired to a reset that de-asserts the drive enable, a relay that is only held closed by an active signal.

It needs naming explicitly because "safe" is domain-specific and sometimes counter-intuitive. For a heater, off is safe. For a fail-open valve in a fire system, open is safe. For an aircraft control surface, neither extreme is safe and you need a defined position. Getting this wrong is how a correct-looking failure response makes the situation worse.

What the interviewer is listening forThat it is reachable without working firmware, and that you recognise it as a domain question rather than a technical default.
  • Follow-up: "What if the safe state itself needs power to hold?" — then you need an energy reserve, or a mechanism that is passively safe (spring-return, normally-closed relay).
You are running out of time and have not covered testing. Is that a problem?Mid

It costs you a signal that is cheap to buy back, so spend thirty seconds on it even if you have to cut something else. A short, concrete statement is enough: "I'd bring SWD and a UART to a test footprint, add a sense resistor in the battery path so the production line can measure sleep current, and put a fault log in a .noinit section so a returned unit can tell us why it reset."

That sentence signals production experience more efficiently than almost anything else you can say, because candidates who have only built prototypes do not think about it.

What the interviewer is listening forAny evidence you have shipped something rather than just made one work on a bench.
The interviewer says "assume infinite budget". What changes, and what does not?Senior

Component selection loosens — you can afford a better MCU, a secure element, more flash, a nicer radio, industrial-rated parts. What does not change is the physics and the architecture: energy per bit transmitted, thermal limits, real-time determinism, the latency of a PCIe round trip, the fact that Linux cannot promise a 5 µs deadline. Nor does complexity cost disappear: every extra part is another thing to qualify, source, test and support for ten years.

The good answer notes that "infinite budget" usually removes the wrong constraint. Most embedded designs are bounded by energy, time and physics, not by unit cost, and saying so shows you understand what is actually binding.

What the interviewer is listening forWhether you know which of your constraints were economic and which were physical.

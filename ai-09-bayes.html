Bayes, likelihood and estimation

How to update a belief when evidence arrives, and how to choose the parameters of a model from data. Maximum likelihood is the principle that justifies every loss function in this track.

Bayes' theorem

Bayes' theorem is two lines of algebra with an unusually large amount of philosophy attached. The algebra is what matters here, and it is worth being able to rederive.

   From the definition of conditional probability:

        P(A|B) P(B) = P(A, B) = P(B|A) P(A)

   Rearranged:

                    P(B|A) P(A)
        P(A|B)  =  -------------
                       P(B)

   THE NAMES, which are worth learning because papers use
   them constantly:

        P(A)     PRIOR       what you believed before
        P(B|A)   LIKELIHOOD  how well A explains the
                             evidence B
        P(A|B)   POSTERIOR   what you believe after
        P(B)     EVIDENCE    a normalising constant

   AND THE FORM THAT MATTERS IN PRACTICE:

        POSTERIOR  is proportional to  LIKELIHOOD x PRIOR

   The denominator only makes it sum to 1, so it is often
   ignored until the end.

   THE CLASSIC WORKED EXAMPLE - a medical test, because the
   result is genuinely surprising:

     disease prevalence          P(D)   = 0.001
     test sensitivity            P(+|D) = 0.99
     false positive rate         P(+|~D)= 0.05

     A patient tests positive. What is P(D|+)?

        P(+) = 0.99 * 0.001  +  0.05 * 0.999
             = 0.00099 + 0.04995 = 0.05094

        P(D|+) = 0.00099 / 0.05094 = 0.0194

     ABOUT 2%. Not 99%.

   THE LESSON - and it is the one people get wrong: A RARE
   CONDITION MEANS MOST POSITIVES ARE FALSE POSITIVES,
   however good the test. The prior dominates.

   THE SAME ARITHMETIC APPLIES TO ANY RARE-EVENT CLASSIFIER
   YOU BUILD: fault detection on a machine that rarely
   fails, intrusion detection, defect screening. A model
   with 99% accuracy on a 0.1% base rate is generating
   fifty false alarms for every true one, and reporting
   "99% accurate" is actively misleading. Report PRECISION
   AND RECALL instead (chapter 12).

Maximum likelihood

Maximum likelihood is the principle behind almost every loss function in this track, including the one used to train every language model. Seeing it stated explicitly makes those losses stop looking arbitrary.

/* THE QUESTION: I have data. What parameters make that data
   most probable?

        theta_MLE = argmax  P(data | theta)

/* IN LOG SPACE, always (chapter 08) - products of many
   small numbers underflow, and sums are easier to
   differentiate:

        theta_MLE = argmax  sum_i log P(x_i | theta)

/* AND SINCE OPTIMISERS MINIMISE:

        theta_MLE = argmin  -sum_i log P(x_i | theta)
                             ^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^
                             THE NEGATIVE LOG LIKELIHOOD

   THIS IS THE LOSS FUNCTION. Not an analogy - it is
   literally what "cross-entropy loss" computes
   (chapter 10).

/* --- WORKED: A COIN --- */
    n flips, k heads, parameter p.

        L(p) = p^k (1-p)^(n-k)
        log L = k log p + (n-k) log(1-p)
        d/dp  = k/p - (n-k)/(1-p) = 0
        =>  p = k/n

    The MLE is the observed frequency. Reassuringly obvious -
    but note it derived rigorously from a principle rather
    than being assumed.

/* --- WORKED: A GAUSSIAN, AND WHY MSE EXISTS --- */
    Assume  y_i = f(x_i) + noise,  noise ~ Normal(0, sigma^2)

        log P(y_i | x_i)
            = -(y_i - f(x_i))^2 / (2 sigma^2)
              - log(sigma sqrt(2 pi))

    Maximising this over the parameters of f means
    MINIMISING sum (y_i - f(x_i))^2.

    SO MEAN SQUARED ERROR IS NOT AN ARBITRARY CHOICE. IT IS
    MAXIMUM LIKELIHOOD UNDER AN ASSUMPTION OF GAUSSIAN
    NOISE.

    And that tells you when NOT to use it: if your errors are
    heavy-tailed, MSE's implicit Gaussian assumption makes it
    over-react to outliers. Huber loss or an L1 loss
    corresponds to a heavier-tailed noise model, which is
    exactly why they are more robust.

/* --- WORKED: CLASSIFICATION, AND WHY CROSS-ENTROPY
       EXISTS --- */
    The model outputs P(class | x) via softmax. The
    likelihood of the correct label is that probability, so

        NLL = -sum_i log P(y_i | x_i)

    which IS cross-entropy loss (chapter 10). One derivation
    produces both of the loss functions you will ever use.

/* --- MAP: MAXIMUM A POSTERIORI --- */
    Include a prior:

        theta_MAP = argmax  P(data|theta) P(theta)
                  = argmin  -log P(data|theta)
                            - log P(theta)
                                    ^^^^^^^^^^^^^
                                    A REGULARISATION TERM

    AND THE TWO STANDARD REGULARISERS FALL OUT DIRECTLY:

      a GAUSSIAN prior on the weights
          -> -log P(theta) proportional to ||theta||^2
          -> L2 REGULARISATION / WEIGHT DECAY

      a LAPLACE prior
          -> proportional to ||theta||_1
          -> L1 REGULARISATION, which drives weights to
             exactly zero (sparsity)

    So weight decay is not a hack. IT IS A STATEMENT THAT
    YOU BELIEVE SMALL WEIGHTS ARE MORE LIKELY A PRIORI
    (chapter 15).                                          */
The single most useful idea in this chapter

Every loss function is a negative log likelihood under some assumed noise model, and every regulariser is a negative log prior. If you know what distribution you are assuming, you know which loss to use — and if a loss is behaving strangely, ask what distributional assumption it encodes and whether your data actually satisfies it.

A worked classifier

A worked example is the fastest way to make the previous two sections concrete, and this classifier is simple enough to follow end to end while still being genuinely useful.

"""NAIVE BAYES - the simplest useful application of Bayes'
   theorem, and still a strong baseline for text
   classification. Worth building once because it makes the
   abstract machinery concrete."""

import numpy as np
from collections import defaultdict

class NaiveBayes:
    """P(class | words) proportional to
       P(class) * product of P(word | class)

       "Naive" = assuming words are INDEPENDENT given the
       class. That assumption is obviously false, and the
       classifier works well anyway - a recurring lesson."""

    def fit(self, docs, labels):
        self.classes = set(labels)
        self.log_prior = {}
        self.log_lik = {}
        vocab = set(w for d in docs for w in d)
        V = len(vocab)

        for c in self.classes:
            c_docs = [d for d, l in zip(docs, labels)
                      if l == c]
            self.log_prior[c] = np.log(len(c_docs) /
                                       len(docs))

            counts = defaultdict(int)
            total = 0
            for d in c_docs:
                for w in d:
                    counts[w] += 1
                    total += 1

            # LAPLACE SMOOTHING: add 1 to every count.
            # WITHOUT IT, a word unseen in this class gives
            # P = 0, and one zero makes the WHOLE PRODUCT
            # zero - a single unseen word vetoes the class.
            # This is the same "never assign zero probability
            # to something possible" principle that appears
            # again in chapter 10.
            self.log_lik[c] = {
                w: np.log((counts[w] + 1) / (total + V))
                for w in vocab
            }
            self.log_lik[c]['<UNK>'] = np.log(1 /
                                              (total + V))

    def predict(self, doc):
        best, best_score = None, -np.inf
        for c in self.classes:
            # SUM OF LOGS, never a product of probabilities
            score = self.log_prior[c]
            for w in doc:
                score += self.log_lik[c].get(
                             w, self.log_lik[c]['<UNK>'])
            if score > best_score:
                best, best_score = c, score
        return best


docs = [["buy", "now", "cheap"], ["meeting", "at", "three"],
        ["cheap", "offer", "now"], ["project", "meeting"]]
labels = ["spam", "ham", "spam", "ham"]

nb = NaiveBayes()
nb.fit(docs, labels)
nb.predict(["cheap", "meeting"])

/* WHY THIS IS WORTH KNOWING IN 2026:
   · it is a genuine probabilistic model you can read in
     full
   · it trains in one pass and runs in microseconds - which
     makes it a real option on an MCU where a transformer is
     not
   · the smoothing problem it exposes is universal
   · and it is the honest baseline: if a neural network
     cannot beat naive Bayes on your task, the network is
     not the problem, the data is.                       */

Uncertainty, and why models are overconfident

Models report confidence, and that confidence is frequently wrong in a specific and predictable direction. This matters enormously the moment a model's output feeds a decision.

/* TWO KINDS OF UNCERTAINTY, and the distinction matters:

   ALEATORIC   irreducible randomness in the data itself.
               A coin flip. Sensor noise. MORE DATA DOES NOT
               HELP.
   EPISTEMIC   uncertainty about the model or its
               parameters, from limited data. MORE DATA
               REDUCES IT.

   A model far outside its training distribution has huge
   EPISTEMIC uncertainty - and a standard softmax has NO WAY
   TO EXPRESS IT. It will report 99% confidence on an input
   unlike anything it has ever seen. That is not a bug in
   the training; it is a limitation of the output
   parameterisation.

/* CALIBRATION: does "70% confident" mean right 70% of the
   time?

   Modern neural networks are systematically OVERCONFIDENT.
   The standard measurement is a RELIABILITY DIAGRAM - bin
   predictions by confidence, plot accuracy per bin against
   the bin's confidence, and look for the diagonal.

   THE CHEAP FIX - TEMPERATURE SCALING:
     after training, fit ONE scalar T on a validation set to
     minimise NLL:
         p = softmax(logits / T)
     T > 1 softens overconfident predictions. One parameter,
     no retraining, and it works remarkably well.

/* ESTIMATING EPISTEMIC UNCERTAINTY, in increasing cost:
     MC DROPOUT       keep dropout on at inference, run n
                      forward passes, look at the spread.
                      Cheap and approximate.
     DEEP ENSEMBLES   train k models, compare. Expensive,
                      and the most reliable method in
                      practice.
     BAYESIAN NNs     distributions over weights.
                      Principled, rarely practical at scale.

/* AND FOR LANGUAGE MODELS SPECIFICALLY:
   The token-level probabilities are a poor guide to factual
   correctness. A model can be highly confident in each token
   of a fabricated citation, because each token is a
   plausible continuation of the previous ones - the
   confidence is about FLUENCY, not TRUTH. This is worth
   understanding precisely, because it explains why
   "just check the model's confidence" does not solve
   hallucination.

   WHAT DOES HELP: sampling several answers and checking
   agreement (self-consistency), retrieving evidence and
   requiring citation, and constraining the output format so
   invalid answers are unrepresentable. All engineering, not
   probability.                                           */

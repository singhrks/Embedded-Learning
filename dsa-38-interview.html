The interview itself, and a mixed final set

The last chapter. What actually happens in the room, how you are scored, and a final set of problems presented without telling you which pattern they use — which is the only realistic test.

What you are actually scored on

It is worth knowing what is actually being measured, because it is not what most candidates assume — and several of the axes can be scored well even on a question you do not finish.

   MOST CANDIDATES BELIEVE THE SCORE IS "did you solve it?"
   It is not. A typical rubric has four axes:

     PROBLEM SOLVING   did you make progress systematically?
                       did you use hints well?
     CODING            is the code clean, correct, and does
                       it match what you said?
     VERIFICATION      did you test it yourself, or wait to
                       be told?
     COMMUNICATION     could the interviewer follow your
                       reasoning throughout?

   A CANDIDATE WHO REASONS CLEARLY TO AN O(n log n) SOLUTION
   USUALLY OUTSCORES ONE WHO SILENTLY TYPES A MEMORISED
   O(n) ANSWER. Two of the four axes are not about the
   algorithm at all.

   ---------------------------------------------------------
   THE TIMELINE OF A 45-MINUTE INTERVIEW

     0-5    introductions, the problem is stated
     5-10   YOU CLARIFY. Restate it. Ask about constraints,
            edge cases, input size, whether you may modify
            the input.
     10-15  YOU DISCUSS APPROACHES. Brute force first, with
            its complexity. Then improve it. State the plan
            and the target complexity BEFORE coding.
     15-32  YOU CODE, narrating.
     32-40  YOU TEST. Trace the example by hand. Then the
            edge cases.
     40-45  follow-ups, and your questions for them.

   THE MOST COMMON TIMING FAILURE is starting to code at
   minute 6. The five minutes of clarification are the
   highest-value part of the whole interview, and skipping
   them is how people solve the wrong problem.

   ---------------------------------------------------------
   WHAT TO SAY WHEN YOU ARE STUCK

   Silence is the only genuinely bad option. Say one of:

     "Let me work through a small example by hand."
     "The brute force is O(n^2) because ... — let me look
      for what work is being repeated."
     "n is up to 10^5, so I need about O(n log n). That
      rules out checking every pair."
     "This feels like it might be a sliding window because
      the subarray is contiguous — let me test that on the
      example."
     "I'm stuck between two approaches. Can I talk through
      both?"

   ASKING FOR A HINT IS NOT A FAILURE. Using one well —
   taking it and running with it — scores better than
   grinding silently for ten minutes.

   ---------------------------------------------------------
   THE THINGS THAT LOSE POINTS UNNECESSARILY

     · coding before agreeing the approach
     · never stating a complexity
     · claiming it works without tracing anything
     · ignoring the empty / single-element / all-equal cases
     · going silent for more than about twenty seconds
     · arguing when told there is a better solution
     · a variable named `x` doing three different jobs

   AND THE ONE THAT SURPRISES PEOPLE: FINDING YOUR OWN BUG
   SCORES POSITIVELY. Tracing your code and saying "this is
   wrong when the array is empty — let me fix it" is a
   strong signal, not a weak one.

   ---------------------------------------------------------
   THE EDGE CASES TO CHECK, EVERY TIME

     empty input                 []  ""  null
     one element
     two elements
     all identical
     already sorted / reverse sorted
     negatives and zero
     the target absent
     duplicates
     maximum size (does it overflow? is it fast enough?)

   Say them aloud as a list. It takes fifteen seconds and it
   is the cheapest scoring opportunity in the interview.
The one-minute opening, verbatim 
"So, to make sure I understand: I'm given [restate],
 and I need to return [restate]. A few questions —
 how large can n be? Can the values be negative? Is
 the input sorted? Am I allowed to modify it? And
 what should I return if [edge case]?"

 ... then ...

"The brute force is [X], which is O(n²). I think I
 can do better by [insight] — that would be
 O(n log n). Shall I code that?"

That is ninety seconds and it hits three of the four rubric axes before you have written a line.

The mixed set

Four problems, no pattern label. Work out which technique applies before you start — that identification step is the one you have been practising, and it is the one the interview actually tests. Give each a full twenty minutes before opening the hints.

After the interview

The half-hour after an interview is the most valuable practice time you will get, and almost nobody uses it. This is what to do with it.

/* --- WRITE IT DOWN, THE SAME DAY --- */
    the problem, your approach, where you got stuck,
    what the interviewer nudged you toward, and what you
    would do differently.

    After five interviews this log tells you your actual
    weakness, which is almost never what you assumed. Most
    people discover it is communication or edge cases, not
    algorithms.

/* --- ASK THEM QUESTIONS --- */
    You are also evaluating them. Reasonable questions:
      what does the team own end to end?
      what does the on-call rotation look like?
      how do changes get from a laptop to production?
      what is the most frustrating part of the codebase?

    The last one gets unusually honest answers.

/* --- IF YOU ARE REJECTED --- */
    Ask for feedback; sometimes you get it. Most companies
    allow a reapplication after 6-12 months, and people do
    get in on the second attempt regularly. A rejection is
    a sample of one interviewer on one day, not a
    measurement of your ability.

/* --- AND THE LAST PIECE OF ADVICE --- */

    THE STRONGEST PREDICTOR OF PASSING IS HAVING PRACTISED
    OUT LOUD. Not problem count. Not language. Talking
    through your reasoning while typing is a separate skill
    from solving, it feels unnatural at first, and it is
    almost entirely learnable in a couple of weeks.

    Explain three problems out loud to an empty room before
    your next interview. It is the highest-value hour left
    available to you.                                      */
